The words for pride in Scripture are words of height. A heart is "lifted up", a spirit is "haughty", eyes are "raised too high", a soul is "puffed up". The same Hebrew words that name human pride also name God's majesty. "In the greatness of your majesty you overthrow your adversaries" (Exodus 15:7) uses the word that elsewhere is "the pride of Moab" (Jeremiah 48:29). The English chooses "majesty" for God and "pride" for people, but the Hebrew does not change. So I cannot read pride in Scripture without asking whose height it is, and where it comes from.

Pride also speaks. It boasts, and it mocks. This section holds all three, because the verses keep them together: "'Scoffer' is the name of the arrogant, haughty man who acts with arrogant pride" (Proverbs 21:24).

The words

  • Height. The heart, the eyes, the spirit and the horn are "lifted up" or "high". The same verbs are used for God being high (Psalm 138:6; Isaiah 57:15) and for the proud heart (2 Chronicles 26:16; Daniel 5:20).
  • Pride and majesty. One family of Hebrew words is "pride", "arrogance" and "pomp" in people, and "majesty" and "triumphed gloriously" in God (Exodus 15:1, 7; Psalm 93:1).
  • Presumption. Another family names acting beyond what one was given: speaking where one was not sent, going up when told not to.
  • Swelling. The Greek pictures pride as being inflated: "puffed up" (1 Corinthians 4:6), "swollen with conceit" (2 Timothy 3:4).
  • The boast. Pride said aloud. The same Greek word is rendered "boast" and "rejoice" (Romans 5:2–3).
  • The mock. Pride turned on another in laughter and taunts. These words are also used of God's laughter at those who rise against him (Psalm 2:4).

Given, then held as one's own

The clearest thing the verses say about pride is where it comes from. In account after account a person is given something, and the heart rises on it.

  • Strength. Uzziah "was marvelously helped, till he was strong. But when he was strong, he grew proud, to his destruction" (2 Chronicles 26:15–16).
  • Healing. Hezekiah was healed and given a sign, and gifts were brought "so that he was exalted in the sight of all nations". Then: "But Hezekiah did not make return according to the benefit done to him, for his heart was proud" (2 Chronicles 32:23–25).
  • Majesty. "The Most High God gave Nebuchadnezzar your father kingship and greatness and glory and majesty … But when his heart was lifted up and his spirit was hardened so that he dealt proudly, he was brought down from his kingly throne" (Daniel 5:18–20).
  • Wealth. "By your great wisdom in your trade you have increased your wealth, and your heart has become proud in your wealth" (Ezekiel 28:5).
  • Beauty and height. Of the great tree God says, "I made it beautiful", and then, "its heart was proud of its height" (Ezekiel 31:9–10). Chapter 12 follows the king of Tyre's beauty the same way.

The law saw it coming: "then your heart be lifted up, and you forget the Lord your God … Beware lest you say in your heart, 'My power and the might of my hand have gotten me this wealth.' You shall remember the Lord your God, for it is he who gives you power to get wealth" (Deuteronomy 8:14, 17–18). Paul says it as a question: "What do you have that you did not receive? If then you received it, why do you boast as if you did not receive it?" (1 Corinthians 4:7).

So the standing in these verses is real. It was given. Pride holds it as if it had not been given. I read that this is the line the verses draw. It does not run between having height and having none. It runs between height that is one's own by nature, height that is given, and height that is taken as one's own.

It credits its own hand. The king of Assyria says, "By the strength of my hand I have done it, and by my wisdom, for I have understanding" (Isaiah 10:13). In the same passage he is the rod in the Lord's hand: "Shall the axe boast over him who hews with it?" (10:5, 15). Nebuchadnezzar: "Is not this great Babylon, which I have built by my mighty power … for the glory of my majesty?" (Daniel 4:30). I read pride here as crediting to itself what was done through it.

It gives itself the glory. The great city "glorified herself and lived in luxury" (Revelation 18:7). The Greek verb is the one used where people give glory to God: "so that they may see your good works and give glory to your Father who is in heaven" (Matthew 5:16). Here she is the one who gives the glory and the one who receives it. What it brings her is in Where it ends, below.

The same word can name an honour God gives. "He chose our heritage for us, the pride of Jacob whom he loves" (Psalm 47:4). To a city "forsaken and hated": "I will make you majestic forever, a joy from age to age" (Isaiah 60:15). "The Lord is restoring the majesty of Jacob" (Nahum 2:2). Israel's triumph is the Lord himself: "the sword of your triumph" (Deuteronomy 33:29). Where God chooses, gives or restores it, the word is honour. And what was given can be turned. God's own sanctuary became "the pride of your power, the delight of your eyes, and the yearning of your soul" (Ezekiel 24:21). His beautiful ornament "they used for pride, and they made their abominable images … of it" (Ezekiel 7:20).

The heart that says what God says

Pride lives in the heart, and the verses let it speak.

God says of himself: "I am the Lord, and there is no other, besides me there is no God" (Isaiah 45:5). "I am God, and there is no other; I am God, and there is none like me" (Isaiah 46:9). Two chapters later Babylon is addressed as the one "who say in your heart, 'I am, and there is no one besides me'" (Isaiah 47:8), and again, "you said in your heart, 'I am, and there is no one besides me'" (47:10). Nineveh "said in her heart, 'I am, and there is no one else'" (Zephaniah 2:15). The words are nearly the same. I read that pride at its full height says of itself what is true only of God.

The same claim comes in other words.

  • "You said in your heart, 'I will ascend to heaven … I will make myself like the Most High'" (Isaiah 14:13–14).
  • The prince of Tyre: "you have said, 'I am a god' … yet you are but a man, and no god, though you make your heart like the heart of a god" (Ezekiel 28:2). The verse names the gap itself: a heart made like a god's, in a man.
  • At the end, one "who opposes and exalts himself against every so-called god or object of worship, so that he takes his seat in the temple of God, proclaiming himself to be God" (2 Thessalonians 2:4).

It deceives the one who holds it. "The pride of your heart has deceived you, you who live in the clefts of the rock, in your lofty dwelling, who say in your heart, 'Who will bring me down to the ground?'" (Obadiah 3). Edom had a second deceiver: "The horror you inspire has deceived you, and the pride of your heart" (Jeremiah 49:16). The fear that others felt fed what Edom told itself.

It tells itself that loss will not come. Babylon in Isaiah says more than "I am". She says, "I shall not sit as a widow or know the loss of children" (Isaiah 47:8). Before it: "You said, 'I shall be mistress forever,' so that you did not lay these things to heart or remember their end" (47:7). The answer: "These two things shall come to you in a moment, in one day" (47:9). Revelation gives the great city the same voice: "in her heart she says, 'I sit as a queen, I am no widow, and mourning I shall never see'" (Revelation 18:7). And the same answer: "For this reason her plagues will come in a single day, death and mourning and famine" (18:8). I read Revelation as taking up Isaiah's words. Pride's heart says that it will not be moved from where it sits, and that nothing will be taken from it. The verse in Isaiah names what goes with it: the end is not laid to heart.

It shuts God out of the thoughts. "In the pride of his face the wicked does not seek him; all his thoughts are, 'There is no God'" (Psalm 10:4). "Behold, his soul is puffed up; it is not upright within him, but the righteous shall live by his faith" (Habakkuk 2:4). The puffed-up soul is set against faith. The next verse gives it no rest: "an arrogant man who is never at rest. His greed is as wide as Sheol" (2:5).

It shows. In the eyes: "Haughty eyes and a proud heart, the lamp of the wicked, are sin" (Proverbs 21:4). In the walk: the daughters of Zion "walk with outstretched necks, glancing wantonly with their eyes" (Isaiah 3:16). Worn: "Therefore pride is their necklace" (Psalm 73:6), and the "therefore" points back to ease: "they have no pangs until death" (73:4). It is placed in the heart and in the spirit (Chapter 12), and Jesus names it among what comes "from within, out of the heart of man" (Mark 7:21–22).

Pride and hearing

Across all its words, pride is set against hearing.

  • "Hear and give ear; be not proud, for the Lord has spoken" (Jeremiah 13:15). And then: "if you will not listen, my soul will weep in secret for your pride" (13:17).
  • "By insolence comes nothing but strife, but with those who take advice is wisdom" (Proverbs 13:10).
  • The scoffer is known by what he will not receive. He "does not listen to rebuke" (Proverbs 13:1); he "does not like to be reproved; he will not go to the wise" (15:12). "Do not reprove a scoffer, or he will hate you; reprove a wise man, and he will love you" (9:8). The same rebuke meets two hearts. "A scoffer seeks wisdom in vain" (14:6). When he is struck, others learn: "Strike a scoffer, and the simple will learn prudence" (19:25).
  • "Now therefore do not scoff, lest your bonds be made strong … Give ear, and hear my voice" (Isaiah 28:22–23).

Presumption is pride acting on its own word.

  • The prophet "who presumes to speak a word in my name that I have not commanded him to speak" (Deuteronomy 18:20). Of such a word: "You need not be afraid of him" (18:22).
  • Israel was told, "Do not go up or fight, for I am not in your midst". "So I spoke to you, and you would not listen; but you rebelled against the command of the Lord and presumptuously went up into the hill country" (Deuteronomy 1:42–43). It came just after "We have sinned against the Lord. We ourselves will go up" (1:41). I read a presumption that wore the words of repentance and went up when the time to go up had passed.
  • "They and our fathers acted presumptuously and stiffened their neck and did not obey your commandments … and were not mindful of the wonders that you performed among them" (Nehemiah 9:16–17). God's answer is in the same breath: "But you are a God ready to forgive" (9:17).
  • The psalmist asks to be kept from it: "Keep back your servant also from presumptuous sins; let them not have dominion over me!" (Psalm 19:13).

God works to keep pride from a person. "He opens the ears of men and terrifies them with warnings, that he may turn man aside from his deed and conceal pride from a man" (Job 33:16–17). Paul, given "the surpassing greatness of the revelations", was given a second thing "to keep me from becoming conceited": "a thorn … in the flesh" (2 Corinthians 12:7). The answer to his prayer was "My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in weakness" (12:9).

What the verses set against pride is hearing, and fear. "So do not become proud, but fear" (Romans 11:20). "The fear of the Lord is hatred of evil. Pride and arrogance and the way of evil and perverted speech I hate" (Proverbs 8:13). Section 10.2 takes this up.

Pride over another

Pride is seldom alone in these verses. It stands over someone.

  • The poor. "In arrogance the wicked hotly pursue the poor" (Psalm 10:2). "Whoever mocks the poor insults his Maker" (Proverbs 17:5). The mocking reaches past the poor man to the one who made him.
  • The widow. "The Lord tears down the house of the proud but maintains the widow's boundaries" (Proverbs 15:25).
  • The needy left unaided. "Behold, this was the guilt of your sister Sodom: she and her daughters had pride, excess of food, and prosperous ease, but did not aid the poor and needy" (Ezekiel 16:49). Pride is listed with plenty and ease, and with what was not done.
  • The righteous. Lips "which speak insolently against the righteous in pride and contempt" (Psalm 31:18). "The insolent utterly deride me … The insolent smear me with lies … The insolent have dug pitfalls for me" (Psalm 119:51, 69, 85). Against each, the one who bears it holds to the law: "but I do not turn away from your law" (119:51).
  • With the heart closed. "They close their hearts to pity; with their mouths they speak arrogantly" (Psalm 17:10).
  • Against love. "'Knowledge' puffs up, but love builds up" (1 Corinthians 8:1). "Love … does not envy or boast; it is not arrogant" (13:4). Conceit goes with provoking and envying: "Let us not become conceited, provoking one another, envying one another" (Galatians 5:26).
  • Where mourning belongs. "And you are arrogant! Ought you not rather to mourn?" (1 Corinthians 5:2).

It is also felt from below. "Our soul has had more than enough of the scorn of those who are at ease, of the contempt of the proud" (Psalm 123:4). The psalm's answer is to look elsewhere: "our eyes look to the Lord our God, till he has mercy upon us" (123:2). Section 10.10 follows pride between people.

The boast

A boast is what a person stands on, said aloud. The verses do not condemn boasting as such. They judge it by what it rests on.

In oneself. "Let not the wise man boast in his wisdom, let not the mighty man boast in his might, let not the rich man boast in his riches" (Jeremiah 9:23). "Those who trust in their wealth and boast of the abundance of their riches" are told, "Truly no man can ransom another" (Psalm 49:6–7). "Do not boast about tomorrow, for you do not know what a day may bring" (Proverbs 27:1); "Let not him who straps on his armor boast himself as he who takes it off" (1 Kings 20:11). "Like clouds and wind without rain is a man who boasts of a gift he does not give" (Proverbs 25:14). "If you have bitter jealousy and selfish ambition in your hearts, do not boast and be false to the truth" (James 3:14).

Excluded. "For by grace you have been saved through faith. And this is not your own doing; it is the gift of God, not a result of works, so that no one may boast" (Ephesians 2:8–9). "Then what becomes of our boasting? It is excluded" (Romans 3:27). God chose what is low "so that no human being might boast in the presence of God" (1 Corinthians 1:29). A boast is shut out because what is held was given.

In shame. Paul names many who "walk as enemies of the cross of Christ. Their end is destruction, their god is their belly, and they glory in their shame, with minds set on earthly things" (Philippians 3:18–19). The word is "glory" (doxa), not the boast word. I read it as doing the boast's work, saying what a person stands on, and as turned upside down: it rests on what should shame. Paul tells it "even with tears" (3:18; 10.10). Against it he sets, "But our citizenship is in heaven", and a Savior who "will transform our lowly body to be like his glorious body" (3:20–21). "Glorious" is the same word. Their glory is in their shame, and the glory awaited is his.

Moved. The boast is not taken away. It is turned. "But let him who boasts boast in this, that he understands and knows me, that I am the Lord who practices steadfast love, justice, and righteousness in the earth. For in these things I delight, declares the Lord" (Jeremiah 9:24). "Let the one who boasts, boast in the Lord" (1 Corinthians 1:31). "My soul makes its boast in the Lord; let the humble hear and be glad" (Psalm 34:2). The same Greek word becomes joy: "we rejoice in hope of the glory of God. Not only that, but we rejoice in our sufferings" (Romans 5:2–3).

In weakness. Forced to boast, Paul turns it round: "If I must boast, I will boast of the things that show my weakness" (2 Corinthians 11:30). He holds back even a true boast, "so that no one may think more of me than he sees in me or hears from me" (12:6). "I will boast all the more gladly of my weaknesses, so that the power of Christ may rest upon me" (12:9). I read this as the opposite of pride's standing: no more is claimed than is so.

Within a measure. "We will not boast beyond limits, but will boast only with regard to the area of influence God assigned to us" (2 Corinthians 10:13). It matches the measure for thinking of oneself: "not to think of himself more highly than he ought to think, but to think with sober judgment, each according to the measure of faith that God has assigned" (Romans 12:3). And its opposite: "For if anyone thinks he is something, when he is nothing, he deceives himself" (Galatians 6:3).

Of others. Paul's most frequent boast is in other people. "I have great pride in you; I am filled with comfort" (2 Corinthians 7:4). "What is our hope or joy or crown of boasting before our Lord Jesus at his coming? Is it not you?" (1 Thessalonians 2:19).

The mock

Mocking is pride turned outward, on another.

  • It takes the sufferer's trust as its target. "All who see me mock me; they make mouths at me; they wag their heads; 'He trusts in the Lord; let him deliver him'" (Psalm 22:7–8). The sufferer answers God, not them: "Yet you are he who took me from the womb" (22:9).
  • It belittles. Sanballat "was angry and greatly enraged, and he jeered at the Jews. And he said … 'What are these feeble Jews doing?'" (Nehemiah 4:1–2).
  • It is aimed at the one who carries God's word. "I have become a laughingstock all the day; everyone mocks me … the word of the Lord has become for me a reproach and derision all day long" (Jeremiah 20:7–8). And still: "there is in my heart as it were a burning fire shut up in my bones, and I am weary with holding it in, and I cannot" (20:9).
  • It mocks the word until there is no way back. God "sent persistently to them by his messengers, because he had compassion on his people … But they kept mocking the messengers of God, despising his words and scoffing at his prophets, until the wrath of the Lord rose against his people, until there was no remedy" (2 Chronicles 36:15–16). When Hezekiah's couriers called the people to return, "they laughed them to scorn and mocked them. However, some men … humbled themselves and came" (2 Chronicles 30:10–11). The same call was met by mocking and by humbling.
  • "Do not be deceived: God is not mocked, for whatever one sows, that will he also reap" (Galatians 6:7).

Jesus was mocked as king, as prophet and as saviour, and he did not answer in kind. That is told in 13.1.

Where it ends

Its own fall. In the proverbs no other hand is named: "Pride goes before destruction, and a haughty spirit before a fall" (Proverbs 16:18). "One's pride will bring him low" (Proverbs 29:23). "When pride comes, then comes disgrace" (Proverbs 11:2). The rod blossoms and pride buds: "the rod has blossomed; pride has budded" (Ezekiel 7:10).

God brings it down. "God opposes the proud but gives grace to the humble" (James 4:6). "He has scattered the proud in the thoughts of their hearts" (Luke 1:51). "The haughty looks of man shall be brought low, and the lofty pride of men shall be humbled, and the Lord alone will be exalted in that day" (Isaiah 2:11). "Not from the east or from the west and not from the wilderness comes lifting up, but it is God who executes judgment, putting down one and lifting up another" (Psalm 75:6–7). Death brings it down too: "Your pomp is brought down to Sheol" (Isaiah 14:11).

A like measure. "As she glorified herself and lived in luxury, so give her a like measure of torment and mourning" (Revelation 18:7). The measure of what comes to the city is the measure of the glory she gave herself and the luxury she lived in. The mourning she said she would never see is what she is given. The verse before says the same of her deeds: "Pay her back as she herself has paid back others … mix a double portion for her in the cup she mixed" (18:6). Those who shared her luxury "will stand far off, in fear of her torment" (18:9–10). And before it all comes a call: "Come out of her, my people, lest you take part in her sins, lest you share in her plagues" (18:4).

God's height does not do what pride does. "For though the Lord is high, he regards the lowly, but the haughty he knows from afar" (Psalm 138:6). I read the contrast the verses give: the one who is high by nature comes near the low, and those who make themselves high are known from afar.

Pride can be named wrongly. Eliab, angry, says to his young brother, "I know your presumption and the evil of your heart" (1 Samuel 17:28), and the chapter has shown that David came because his father sent him (17:17–18). Eliphaz explains a man brought low: "For when they are humbled you say, 'It is because of pride'" (Job 22:29). I read that the charge of pride can come from another's anger, or from a ready explanation, and the verses do not make it true.

The counterpart, humbling oneself, is in 10.14.

In Daniel's vision the horn "became great, even as great as the Prince of the host" (Daniel 8:11), as the ram before it "did as he pleased and became great" (8:4; 10.7).

The other face

The same words are not pride when the height is given, or when it faces God.

  • A heart lifted up in God's ways. Jehoshaphat "sought the God of his father and walked in his commandments … His heart was courageous in the ways of the Lord" (2 Chronicles 17:4, 6). The word for "courageous" is the word for Uzziah's heart that "grew proud" (26:16). I read that the lifting is the same and the direction differs.
  • Strength of soul given. "On the day I called, you answered me; my strength of soul you increased" (Psalm 138:3). The word is the one rendered "insolent" in Isaiah 3:5.
  • A heart not lifted up. "O Lord, my heart is not lifted up; my eyes are not raised too high … But I have calmed and quieted my soul, like a weaned child with its mother" (Psalm 131:1–2). The arrogant man is "never at rest" (Habakkuk 2:5). The heart that is not lifted up is at rest.
  • The honour given, and the boast in the Lord (above).

Chapter 11 (§4) sets this among the words that change character.
