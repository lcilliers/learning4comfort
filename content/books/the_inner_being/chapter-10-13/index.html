Scripture never defines anger. It describes what anger does, and it uses the same words for God and for people.

  • It is kindled, burns and is poured. "So the anger of the Lord was kindled against Israel" (Judges 2:20). "At this the king became enraged, and his anger burned within him" (Esther 1:12). "My anger and my wrath will be poured out on this place" (Jeremiah 7:20).
  • It is set off by something heard or seen, on both sides. "When the Lord heard it, his anger was kindled" (Numbers 11:1). "As soon as his master heard the words that his wife spoke to him … his anger was kindled" (Genesis 39:19). "When Haman saw Mordecai in the king's gate … he was filled with wrath" (Esther 5:9).
  • It moves, and it has a span. "Wrath has gone out against you from the Lord" (2 Chronicles 19:2). "For his anger is but for a moment, and his favor is for a lifetime" (Psalm 30:5). Edom "kept his wrath forever" (Amos 1:11).
  • It is felt in the body. "So Cain was very angry, and his face fell" (Genesis 4:5). "There is no soundness in my flesh because of your indignation" (Psalm 38:3).
  • It is drunk. "You who have drunk from the hand of the Lord the cup of his wrath" (Isaiah 51:17).

The same pictures are used for God and for people. Where the verses mark a difference, they mark it in restraint: "I will not execute my burning anger … for I am God and not a man" (Hosea 11:9).

The verses do say what human anger does not do: "the anger of man does not produce the righteousness of God" (James 1:20). And they name anger by its absence: "lifting holy hands without anger or quarreling" (1 Timothy 2:8). Love "is not irritable or resentful" (1 Corinthians 13:5).

The words

The English words anger, wrath, fury and indignation stand for many Hebrew and Greek words. Some are used of God and of people alike, and some are kept almost entirely for God.

  • ʾAph, "nostril, anger". It is the commonest word. Its first sense is the nose, and the verses keep the picture: "pressing the nose produces blood, and pressing anger produces strife" (Proverbs 30:33). It is used of God's anger and of people's. It is also the word of slow to anger, which is literally long of nostril.
  • Ḥēmāh, "heat, wrath". It is poured and kindled: "Therefore my wrath and my anger were poured out and kindled in the cities of Judah" (Jeremiah 44:6). The same word is the venom of the serpent: "They have venom like the venom of a serpent" (Psalm 58:4). It is also the heat of wine: "the princes became sick with the heat of wine" (Hosea 7:5).
  • Ḥārāh, "to burn", and ḥārôn, "burning". The verb is used of people and of God. The noun, fierce anger, is God's every time it is anger. Many of its verses speak of it turning: "Turn from your burning anger and relent from this disaster against your people" (Exodus 32:12).
  • Kāʿas, "to provoke, vex". As a verb it is mostly people provoking God (Deuteronomy 4:25). As a noun it is mostly what a person carries: Hannah's "great anxiety and vexation" (1 Samuel 1:16), "Sorrow is better than laughter" (Ecclesiastes 7:3). One word covers provoking God and Hannah's grief.
  • Qeṣep̄ and zaʿam, "wrath" and "indignation". These are almost always God's. Zaʿam names a period with an end: "the latter end of the indignation, for it refers to the appointed time of the end" (Daniel 8:19).
  • ʿEbrāh, "overflow, wrath". It is God's wrath, and it is also pride that overflows: "'Scoffer' is the name of the arrogant, haughty man who acts with arrogant pride" (Proverbs 21:24).
  • Zāʿap̄, "rage". It is Uzziah's anger (2 Chronicles 26:19), and also a troubled face: "Why are your faces downcast today?" (Genesis 40:7).
  • The Greek words. Orgē, "wrath", is God's in most of its uses. The same noun is the anger people are told to put away (Colossians 3:6, 8). Orgizō, "to be angry", is never God's own anger. Thymos, "passion, wrath", is God's, people's and the devil's. Aganakteō, "to be indignant", is used of Jesus, the disciples and the religious leaders. Paroxynō, "to provoke", is both a spirit "provoked within him" (Acts 17:16) and "stir up one another to love" (Hebrews 10:24).

So the verses do not give God one vocabulary and people another. They share most of the words, and they keep a few for God.

A person's anger

What it rises at

Human anger in the verses is often set off by something perceived. Sometimes the verse names hearing or seeing; sometimes the setting gives the cause.

  • A report: Potiphar's anger rose on his wife's words (Genesis 39:19). It was not tested, and it was false.
  • A sight: "when Haman saw Mordecai in the king's gate, that he neither rose nor trembled before him, he was filled with wrath" (Esther 5:9).
  • A demand, an expectation or a slight. Rachel demanded children, and "Jacob's anger was kindled against Rachel, and he said, 'Am I in the place of God?'" (Genesis 30:2). Naaman: "Behold, I thought that he would surely come out to me" (2 Kings 5:11). Queen Vashti refused to come, and the king's "anger burned within him" (Esther 1:12).
  • A rival's honour: the women sang of David's ten thousands, and "Saul was very angry" (1 Samuel 18:8). The elder brother heard the music for his returned brother, "was angry and refused to go in" (Luke 15:28).
  • A wrong done to another. Jacob's sons were indignant "because he had done an outrageous thing in Israel" (Genesis 34:7). David burned at the rich man in Nathan's parable (2 Samuel 12:5). Nehemiah: "I was very angry when I heard their outcry" (Nehemiah 5:6). Paul's "spirit was provoked within him as he saw that the city was full of idols" (Acts 17:16).
  • Good done, where it breaks a rule: the ruler of the synagogue was "indignant because Jesus had healed on the Sabbath" (Luke 13:14).
  • A word that exposes. At Nazareth, "When they heard these things, all in the synagogue were filled with wrath" (Luke 4:28). At Stephen's speech, "they cried out with a loud voice and stopped their ears and rushed together at him" (Acts 7:57).
  • Something within. "What causes quarrels and what causes fights among you? Is it not this, that your passions are at war within you?" (James 4:1).

Whether what sets it off is true is part of the account. Potiphar acted on a lie. The officials raged at Jeremiah over a charge he denied: "It is a lie; I am not deserting" (Jeremiah 37:14). The disciples were indignant at the waste of ointment, and Jesus answered, "she has done a beautiful thing to me" (Matthew 26:10).

Where it sits

The verses place anger within the person. "His anger burned within him" (Esther 1:12). Ezekiel went "in bitterness in the heat of my spirit" (Ezekiel 3:14). "Be not quick in your spirit to become angry, for anger lodges in the heart of fools" (Ecclesiastes 7:9). Where it is aimed at God, "his heart rages against the Lord" (Proverbs 19:3).

It is also borne in the body over time. Hannah, provoked year after year, "wept and would not eat" (1 Samuel 1:7). Of vexation: "My eye is wasted from grief; my soul and my body also" (Psalm 31:9). Ahab, refused Naboth's vineyard, "lay down on his bed and turned away his face and would eat no food" (1 Kings 21:4).

What it does next

What follows the anger is where the cases differ. The verses follow it into what the person does.

  • The hand acts at once. Moses "threw the tablets out of his hands and broke them" (Exodus 32:19). Balaam "struck the donkey with his staff" (Numbers 22:27).
  • Where there is power, a command. Pharaoh "put them in custody" (Genesis 40:3). Nebuchadnezzar "commanded that all the wise men of Babylon be destroyed" (Daniel 2:12). Herod "sent and killed all the male children in Bethlehem" (Matthew 2:16). "A king's wrath is like the growling of a lion" (Proverbs 19:12).
  • Killing. "For in their anger they killed men" (Genesis 49:6). God asked Cain, "Why are you angry, and why has your face fallen?" (Genesis 4:6), and Cain "rose up against his brother Abel and killed him" (4:8).
  • A watching eye, and a plot. "And Saul eyed David from that day on" (1 Samuel 18:9). Sanballat and Tobiah "all plotted together" (Nehemiah 4:8). "The wicked plots against the righteous and gnashes his teeth at him" (Psalm 37:12).
  • Rage that wants to kill at once. The council heard the apostles and "were enraged and wanted to kill them" (Acts 5:33). This is not a plan laid in advance: the wish follows the rage straight away. Gamaliel stopped it by having the men put outside for a little while: "take care what you are about to do with these men" (5:34–35). Plotting is in 10.10.
  • The face falls, and the body withdraws. Cain's face fell (Genesis 4:5). Jonathan "ate no food the second day of the month" (1 Samuel 20:34).
  • Counsel, and then a charge. Nehemiah: "I took counsel with myself, and I brought charges against the nobles" (Nehemiah 5:7).
  • Silence, and waiting. Absalom "spoke to Amnon neither good nor bad" (2 Samuel 13:22). Two years later he had him killed.
  • Prayer. "And Samuel was angry, and he cried to the Lord all night" (1 Samuel 15:11). The verses give what was behind it: Saul and the people "spared Agag and the best … and would not utterly destroy them" (15:9), and God said, "he has turned back from following me" (15:11).
  • Fear. After Uzzah was struck, David was angry, and "David was afraid of the Lord that day" (2 Samuel 6:9).
  • Speech that must come out. Elihu, angry and long silent: "I must speak, that I may find relief" (Job 32:20). Moses at Meribah: "they made his spirit bitter, and he spoke rashly with his lips" (Psalm 106:33).
  • Mercy. Saul, after the Spirit came on him in anger and the victory was won, said, "Not a man shall be put to death this day" (1 Samuel 11:13).
  • Staying outside. The elder brother "refused to go in" (Luke 15:28).
  • Strife. "A hot-tempered man stirs up strife" (Proverbs 15:18). "One given to anger causes much transgression" (Proverbs 29:22).
  • Separation. Paul and Barnabas: "there arose a sharp disagreement, so that they separated from each other" (Acts 15:39).

Some of these end in harm, and some in counsel, prayer or mercy. The verses do not say anger always leads to one place. They follow each person to where it led.

What the verses say about it

Proverbs and the letters speak about human anger directly.

  • Its course in the fool: "The vexation of a fool is known at once, but the prudent ignores an insult" (Proverbs 12:16). "Surely vexation kills the fool" (Job 5:2).
  • Its company: "Make no friendship with a man given to anger … lest you learn his ways and entangle yourself in a snare" (Proverbs 22:24–25).
  • Its fierceness, cursed. Jacob over Simeon and Levi: "Cursed be their anger, for it is fierce, and their wrath, for it is cruel!" (Genesis 49:7).
  • What can stand against it: "Wrath is cruel, anger is overwhelming, but who can stand before jealousy?" (Proverbs 27:4).
  • Fretting: "Fret not yourself; it tends only to evil" (Psalm 37:8).
  • Its judgment: "everyone who is angry with his brother will be liable to judgment" (Matthew 5:22).

Governed, and put away

The verses also speak of anger held in check, and they do it in the same words they use for God.

  • Slow to anger: "Whoever is slow to anger is better than the mighty, and he who rules his spirit than he who takes a city" (Proverbs 16:32). "Whoever is slow to anger has great understanding" (Proverbs 14:29).
  • Turned by an answer: "A soft answer turns away wrath, but a harsh word stirs up anger" (Proverbs 15:1). "With patience a ruler may be persuaded" (Proverbs 25:15).
  • Limited in time: "Be angry and do not sin; do not let the sun go down on your anger, and give no opportunity to the devil" (Ephesians 4:26–27).
  • Put away: "Let all bitterness and wrath and anger and clamor and slander be put away from you, along with all malice" (Ephesians 4:31).

The first and last of these stand five verses apart in one chapter, in different Greek words. One allows anger and limits it, and the other tells the reader to put it away. I set them side by side and do not reconcile them.

In the letters, anger is named in lists, and its neighbours are envy, jealousy and words of the mouth: "enmity, strife, jealousy, fits of anger, rivalries, dissensions, divisions, envy" (Galatians 5:20–21). Each list faces an opposite. "But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, patience" (Galatians 5:22). "Be kind to one another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another" (Ephesians 4:32). The anger list in Ephesians comes straight after "do not grieve the Holy Spirit of God" (Ephesians 4:30).

How it is answered

In several passages the answer to a person's anger is a question or a reframing, not a rebuke.

  • God asks Cain: "Why are you angry, and why has your face fallen?" (Genesis 4:6). He asks Jonah: "Do you do well to be angry?" (Jonah 4:4).
  • Joseph to his brothers: "do not be distressed or angry with yourselves because you sold me here, for God sent me before you to preserve life" (Genesis 45:5).
  • Naaman's servants: "My father, it is a great word the prophet has spoken to you; will you not do it?" (2 Kings 5:13).
  • Aaron answered Moses' anger with his reason, "And when Moses heard that, he approved" (Leviticus 10:20).
  • The father "came out and entreated him" (Luke 15:28), and said, "this your brother was dead, and is alive" (15:32).

In two of these, Jonah and the elder brother, the account ends before the angry one answers. I leave them open, as Scripture does.

Angry with oneself, and at God

Anger can turn inward. Joseph tells his brothers not to be "angry with yourselves" (Genesis 45:5).

It can be aimed at God. Moses was displeased when the people wept for meat (Numbers 11:10). David "was angry because the Lord had broken out against Uzzah" (2 Samuel 6:8). Jonah was angry at God's mercy to Nineveh, and quoted God's own words back: "I knew that you are a gracious God and merciful, slow to anger" (Jonah 4:2). In hunger, people "will speak contemptuously against their king and their God" (Isaiah 8:21). "When a man's folly brings his way to ruin, his heart rages against the Lord" (Proverbs 19:3).

Jesus' anger

In the Gospels Jesus' anger is described in human words. Twice the verses name it.

  • At hardness of heart, with grief. Asked whether it was lawful on the Sabbath "to save life or to kill", they were silent. "And he looked around at them with anger, grieved at their hardness of heart, and said to the man, 'Stretch out your hand'" (Mark 3:4–5). The anger and the grief have the same object, and the act that follows is a healing. The next verse is the plot: "The Pharisees went out and immediately held counsel with the Herodians against him, how to destroy him" (3:6).
  • For the children. "When Jesus saw it, he was indignant and said to them, 'Let the children come to me; do not hinder them'" (Mark 10:14).

The word used for Jesus' indignation is used for the disciples at the woman's ointment (Matthew 26:8) and for the ruler of the synagogue at a healing (Luke 13:14). One word is aimed three ways.

God's anger

Scripture speaks of God's anger more often than of human anger. The verses state what kindles it, at whom it is aimed, how it is spoken of, why it is held back, and how it ends.

What kindles it

The verses name a cause each time.

  • A covenant broken: "So the anger of the Lord was kindled against Israel, and he said, 'Because this people have transgressed my covenant'" (Judges 2:20).
  • Complaint heard: "When the Lord heard it, his anger was kindled" (Numbers 11:1).
  • Unwilling to listen: "But they rebelled against me and were not willing to listen to me … Then I said I would pour out my wrath upon them" (Ezekiel 20:8). The next verse holds it back: "But I acted for the sake of my name" (20:9).
  • A hardened hearing: "They made their hearts diamond-hard lest they should hear the law … Therefore great anger came from the Lord of hosts" (Zechariah 7:12).
  • Pride: "his heart was proud. Therefore wrath came upon him and Judah and Jerusalem" (2 Chronicles 32:25).
  • Messengers mocked: "they kept mocking the messengers of God … until the wrath of the Lord rose against his people, until there was no remedy" (2 Chronicles 36:16).
  • What is done to the weak: "If you do mistreat them, and they cry out to me, I will surely hear their cry, and my wrath will burn" (Exodus 22:23–24).
  • The truth held down: "For the wrath of God is revealed from heaven against all ungodliness and unrighteousness of men, who by their unrighteousness suppress the truth" (Romans 1:18).

Provoking him is spoken of as something between persons: "a people who provoke me to my face continually" (Isaiah 65:3). "Why have they provoked me to anger with their carved images?" is asked inside a lament (Jeremiah 8:19). The acts that provoke are named each time (How it is provoked, below).

It is joined with jealousy, in the setting of other gods and of a broken bond. "For the Lord your God in your midst is a jealous God—lest the anger of the Lord your God be kindled against you" (Deuteronomy 6:15). And for a people: "I am jealous for Zion with great jealousy, and I am jealous for her with great wrath" (Zechariah 8:2).

At whom

The verses name who it is aimed at.

  • His own people: "he gave them over to plunderers" (Judges 2:14).
  • One person's act, and a whole people: "the people of Israel broke faith in regard to the devoted things, for Achan … took some of the devoted things. And the anger of the Lord burned against the people of Israel" (Joshua 7:1). Moses asked the question it raises: "shall one man sin, and will you be angry with all the congregation?" (Numbers 16:22).
  • A named person: "the anger of the Lord was kindled against Moses" (Exodus 4:14); against Uzzah (2 Samuel 6:7); against Solomon, "because his heart had turned away from the Lord" (1 Kings 11:9).
  • Those who spoke wrongly of him: "My anger burns against you and against your two friends, for you have not spoken of me what is right, as my servant Job has" (Job 42:7).
  • The nations and the oppressor: "Then he will speak to them in his wrath, and terrify them in his fury" (Psalm 2:5).

The same verse that warns the kings opens a way out: "Kiss the Son, lest he be angry, and you perish in the way, for his wrath is quickly kindled. Blessed are all who take refuge in him" (Psalm 2:12).

How it is spoken of

The verses describe God's anger in pictures, and they keep the pictures.

  • Fire, smoke and breath: "By the breath of God they perish, and by the blast of his anger they are consumed" (Job 4:9). "Why does your anger smoke against the sheep of your pasture?" (Psalm 74:1).
  • A poured liquid: "he has poured out his fury like fire" (Lamentations 2:4).
  • A cup. "Take from my hand this cup of the wine of wrath, and make all the nations to whom I send you drink it" (Jeremiah 25:15). It is drunk to the dregs and then taken away: "Behold, I have taken from your hand the cup of staggering … and I will put it into the hand of your tormentors" (Isaiah 51:22–23). A man's wrath is also poured as a drink: "Woe to him who makes his neighbors drink—you pour out your wrath and make them drunk" (Habakkuk 2:15), and "The cup in the Lord's right hand will come around to you" (2:16).
  • A storm: "Behold, the storm of the Lord! Wrath has gone forth, a whirling tempest; it will burst upon the head of the wicked" (Jeremiah 23:19).
  • Weight: "Your wrath lies heavy upon me" (Psalm 88:7).
  • In the New Testament, it is revealed (Romans 1:18), stored up (Romans 2:5), it remains (John 3:36), and it is finished (Revelation 15:1).

God's heart named with his anger

Three times the verses name God's heart in the same breath as his anger.

  • "My heart recoils within me; my compassion grows warm and tender. I will not execute my burning anger" (Hosea 11:8–9).
  • "The anger of the Lord will not turn back until he has executed and accomplished the intents of his heart. In the latter days you will understand it clearly" (Jeremiah 23:20).
  • "For the day of vengeance was in my heart, and my year of redemption had come" (Isaiah 63:4). The next verse: "I looked, but there was no one to help; I was appalled, but there was no one to uphold; so my own arm brought me salvation, and my wrath upheld me" (63:5).

No verse says that God's own heart is hardened. The one place where his heart turns away from a people is this: "Though Moses and Samuel stood before me, yet my heart would not turn toward this people" (Jeremiah 15:1).

Slow to anger

God proclaims his own name with this phrase: "The Lord, the Lord, a God merciful and gracious, slow to anger, and abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness" (Exodus 34:6). The same words are then used by many speakers for different ends.

  • Moses pleads with them: "'The Lord is slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love …' Please pardon the iniquity of this people" (Numbers 14:18–19).
  • Joel calls people back with them: "Return to the Lord your God, for he is gracious and merciful, slow to anger" (Joel 2:13).
  • Nahum warns with them: "The Lord is slow to anger and great in power, and the Lord will by no means clear the guilty" (Nahum 1:3).
  • Jonah complains with them (Jonah 4:2).
  • The wise man's virtue is the same phrase: "Whoever is slow to anger has great understanding" (Proverbs 14:29).

Why it is held back

God gives his own reasons for restraining his anger.

  • Compassion, and what we are: "Yet he, being compassionate, atoned for their iniquity and did not destroy them; he restrained his anger often and did not stir up all his wrath. He remembered that they were but flesh" (Psalm 78:38–39).
  • The spirit that would faint: "For I will not contend forever, nor will I always be angry; for the spirit would grow faint before me" (Isaiah 57:16).
  • His name: "For my name's sake I defer my anger" (Isaiah 48:9). "I acted for the sake of my name, that it should not be profaned in the sight of the nations" (Ezekiel 20:9).
  • What the enemy would say: "had I not feared provocation by the enemy" (Deuteronomy 32:27).
  • Mercy: "I will not look on you in anger, for I am merciful, declares the Lord" (Jeremiah 3:12).
  • Their humbling: "They have humbled themselves. I will not destroy them" (2 Chronicles 12:7).
  • What he keeps: "I have no wrath" (Isaiah 27:4), of the vineyard he guards.

Paul gives the purpose of the delay: "God's kindness is meant to lead you to repentance" (Romans 2:4).

Turned, and not turned

Many verses speak of the burning anger as turning. Moses asked it: "Turn from your burning anger and relent" (Exodus 32:12). Hezekiah acted for it: "Now it is in my heart to make a covenant with the Lord … in order that his fierce anger may turn away from us" (2 Chronicles 29:10). The king of Nineveh hoped for it with no promise: "Who knows? God may turn and relent and turn from his fierce anger, so that we may not perish" (Jonah 3:9).

It is also said not to turn. "For all this his anger has not turned away, and his hand is stretched out still" (Isaiah 5:25). After Josiah, "who turned to the Lord with all his heart", it is written: "Still the Lord did not turn from the burning of his great wrath … because of all the provocations with which Manasseh had provoked him" (2 Kings 23:25–26). The two verses stand together. The text gives the reason it did not turn, and it does not take back what it said of Josiah.

How it ends

The verses speak of God's anger as having an end, and they name what follows it.

  • Spent: "Thus shall my anger spend itself, and I will vent my fury upon them and satisfy myself. And they shall know that I am the Lord" (Ezekiel 5:13).
  • Calm: "I will be calm and will no more be angry" (Ezekiel 16:42).
  • An appointed end: "in a very little while my fury will come to an end" (Isaiah 10:25).
  • A moment, measured by what follows: "In overflowing anger for a moment I hid my face from you, but with everlasting love I will have compassion on you" (Isaiah 54:8).
  • Comfort: "though you were angry with me, your anger turned away, that you might comfort me" (Isaiah 12:1).
  • Healing: "I will heal their apostasy; I will love them freely, for my anger has turned from them" (Hosea 14:4).
  • Finished: "seven angels with seven plagues, which are the last, for with them the wrath of God is finished" (Revelation 15:1).

And on the other side: "whoever does not obey the Son shall not see life, but the wrath of God remains on him" (John 3:36).

Living under God's anger

I had expected the verses of God's anger to be about God, and in one sense they are. But when I read them asking what the human being is doing, I found a whole inner life lived in relation to that anger. People provoke it, fear it, plead against it, read it in their ruins, bear it with and without understanding, hide from it, and turn back under it. Some come through it singing. This part of the account is about them.

I hold one verse beside everything that follows, because Scripture itself puts it in the middle of Lamentations: "For the Lord will not cast off forever, for, though he cause grief, he will have compassion according to the abundance of his steadfast love; for he does not afflict from his heart or grieve the children of men" (Lamentations 3:31–33).

How it is provoked

The texts are generous about the roots of provocation. They name what was going on inside before the turn to other gods.

  • Forgetting: "You were unmindful of the Rock that bore you, and you forgot the God who gave you birth" (Deuteronomy 32:18).
  • Fullness: "Jeshurun grew fat, and kicked … then he forsook God who made him" (Deuteronomy 32:15).
  • A heart that turns, or is turned: "Take care lest your heart be deceived, and you turn aside" (Deuteronomy 11:16). Solomon's "heart had turned away from the Lord" (1 Kings 11:9).
  • False security: a man "blesses himself in his heart, saying, 'I shall be safe, though I walk in the stubbornness of my heart'" (Deuteronomy 29:19).
  • Distress that drives further away: "In the time of his distress he became yet more faithless to the Lord — this same King Ahaz" (2 Chronicles 28:22).
  • Not remembering: "Because you have not remembered the days of your youth, but have enraged me with all these things" (Ezekiel 16:43).
  • Never being satisfied: "you played the whore also with the Assyrians, because you were not satisfied" (Ezekiel 16:28).

Whole households join in. "The children gather wood, the fathers kindle fire, and the women knead dough … to provoke me to anger" (Jeremiah 7:18). And the provocation turns on those who provoke: "Is it I whom they provoke? declares the Lord. Is it not themselves, to their own shame?" (7:19). Those who followed idols "became false" (2 Kings 17:15).

It is handed on. A king's sins are the sins "which he sinned and made Israel to sin" (1 Kings 14:16). "Like mother, like daughter" (Ezekiel 16:44).

And God's anger is drawn by what is done to the weak, as surely as by idols. "If you do mistreat them, and they cry out to me, I will surely hear their cry, and my wrath will burn" (Exodus 22:23–24). "He has told you, O man, what is good; and what does the Lord require of you but to do justice, and to love kindness, and to walk humbly with your God?" (Micah 6:8).

God swears by his own life in judgment as well as in mercy. "As I live, declares the Lord God, I will not be inquired of by you" (Ezekiel 20:3). To Edom: "therefore, as I live, declares the Lord God, I will deal with you according to the anger and envy that you showed because of your hatred against them" (Ezekiel 35:11). His anger can reach life itself: "He made a path for his anger; he did not spare them from death, but gave their lives over to the plague" (Psalm 78:50). Of Korah's company: "and they go down alive into Sheol, then you shall know that these men have despised the Lord" (Numbers 16:30). Chapter 2 (Living before God) gives the oath.

Pleading, and standing in the breach

People plead with God in the face of his anger, and they do it boldly.

Abraham knows the distance, "I who am but dust and ashes" (Genesis 18:27), and asks again anyway: "Oh let not the Lord be angry, and I will speak again but this once" (18:32). The anger he feared never came.

Moses stands between the anger and the people. God himself leaves the door open: "Now therefore let me alone, that my wrath may burn hot against them" (Exodus 32:10). Moses does not let him alone. He argues from God's ownership of the people, from his reputation, and from his promises (32:11–13), "and the Lord relented" (32:14). He lay on his face forty days without food. What drove him was fear: "For I was afraid of the anger and hot displeasure that the Lord bore against you" (Deuteronomy 9:19). The psalm remembers it as standing "in the breach before him, to turn away his wrath" (Psalm 106:23).

Moses also used God's own words. God had proclaimed himself "slow to anger" (Exodus 34:6). Moses quoted it back: "'The Lord is slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love …' Please pardon the iniquity of this people" (Numbers 14:18–19). God answered, "I have pardoned, according to your word" (14:20).

But the breach is not always filled.

  • "I sought for a man among them who should build up the wall and stand in the breach before me for the land, that I should not destroy it, but I found none" (Ezekiel 22:30).
  • Even the best could only save themselves: "even if Noah, Daniel, and Job were in it … they would deliver but their own lives by their righteousness" (Ezekiel 14:20).
  • Jeremiah, who had stood before God "to speak good for them, to turn away your wrath from them", found that "they have dug a pit for my life". He then prayed for the anger to fall on them (Jeremiah 18:20–23).

And the most surprising intercessor is the sufferer. Job's friends explained his pain by God's anger. In the end God says to them, "My anger burns against you", and "my servant Job shall pray for you, for I will accept his prayer" (Job 42:7–8). The man they explained by God's anger stood between them and it.

The verses also set the leaders apart in how they bear it.

  • Moses bears it on the people's account: "But the Lord was angry with me because of you" (Deuteronomy 3:26). He turns his own exclusion into a warning: "Take care, lest you forget the covenant of the Lord your God" (4:23).
  • Aaron "stood between the dead and the living, and the plague was stopped" (Numbers 16:48).
  • David asks to bear it alone: "let your hand be against me and against my father's house" (2 Samuel 24:17).

Reading the ruin

When ruin comes, people ask why. The question is expected in advance: "What caused the heat of this great anger?" (Deuteronomy 29:24). And it is answered: "It is because they abandoned the covenant of the Lord" (29:25). The passage ends with a limit on reading: "The secret things belong to the Lord our God, but the things that are revealed belong to us and to our children forever, that we may do all the words of this law" (29:29).

The ruin can be read rightly or wrongly.

  • Rightly, it is the first step back. Hezekiah tells his people, "the wrath of the Lord came on Judah and Jerusalem … as you see with your own eyes" (2 Chronicles 29:8). Then he says, "Now it is in my heart to make a covenant with the Lord" (29:10).
  • As God's absence: "Have not these evils come upon us because our God is not among us?" (Deuteronomy 31:17).
  • Seen and not taken in. The survivors in Egypt saw the ruin (Jeremiah 44:2) and did what caused it.

And some of it is understood only later. "The anger of the Lord will not turn back until he has executed and accomplished the intents of his heart. In the latter days you will understand it clearly" (Jeremiah 23:20). In Ezekiel, again and again, the anger is said to end in knowledge: "And they shall know that I am the Lord" (Ezekiel 5:13).

The breath that gives life is also named in his anger. "By the breath of God they perish, and by the blast of his anger they are consumed" (Job 4:9). "I will gather you and blow on you with the fire of my wrath" (Ezekiel 22:21). Chapter 2 follows the breath that gives life.

Some deaths are recorded with their inner cause named:

  • "But Er, Judah's firstborn, was wicked in the sight of the Lord, and the Lord put him to death" (Genesis 38:7)
  • "the anger of the Lord was kindled against Uzzah, and God struck him down there because of his error" (2 Samuel 6:7)
  • "So Saul died for his breach of faith. He broke faith with the Lord" (1 Chronicles 10:13)
  • the men who brought back a bad report of the land "died by plague before the Lord" (Numbers 14:37)

A psalm says it of a whole generation: "He made a path for his anger; he did not spare them from death" (Psalm 78:50). Sin forgiven can still be followed by a death (2 Samuel 12:13–14; Chapter 2).

Dread and trembling

Dread comes with God's anger. Isaiah describes it as a chain with no way out: "Terror and the pit and the snare are upon you, O inhabitant of the earth! He who flees at the sound of the terror shall fall into the pit, and he who climbs out of the pit shall be caught in the snare" (Isaiah 24:17–18). Jeremiah says the same words of Moab, and adds whose act it is: "For I will bring these things upon Moab, the year of their punishment, declares the Lord" (Jeremiah 48:43–44).

In the curse the dread is given into the heart, and it changes even the hours of the day: "In the morning you shall say, 'If only it were evening!'" (Deuteronomy 28:65–67; 10.1). Understanding itself can be dread: "it will be sheer terror to understand the message" (Isaiah 28:19). To grasp what is coming is the terror.

Ezekiel was made to show it in his own body before it came: "eat your bread with quaking, and drink water with trembling and with anxiety", because the people "shall eat their bread with anxiety, and drink water in dismay" (Ezekiel 12:18–19). The prophet trembles first, as a sign of what the people will feel.

Those who watch another fall are shaken for themselves. "They shall tremble every moment, every one for his own life, on the day of your downfall" (Ezekiel 32:10). Another's ruin becomes fear for one's own life.

Wisdom uses the same storm picture for those who refused her: "when terror strikes you like a storm and your calamity comes like a whirlwind" (Proverbs 1:27). There it is Wisdom who speaks, not God's anger, but the terror is the same.

The earth and the heavens tremble too. "The foundations also of the mountains trembled and quaked, because he was angry" (Psalm 18:7). "I will make the heavens tremble, and the earth will be shaken out of its place, at the wrath of the Lord of hosts" (Isaiah 13:13). The verses use for the mountains the words they use for people. Chapter 15 follows this.

Under the curse, life itself hangs in dread: "Your life shall hang in doubt before you. Night and day you shall be in dread and have no assurance of your life" (Deuteronomy 28:66; Chapter 2, Life weighed).

Bearing it

Scripture gives several ways of bearing God's anger, and it does not flatten them into one.

Without knowing why. Job feels it inside: "the arrows of the Almighty are in me; my spirit drinks their poison" (Job 6:4). He feels himself counted as an enemy: "He has kindled his wrath against me and counts me as his adversary" (19:11). He is given no cause. Psalm 88 goes further and has no turn at all: "Your wrath has swept over me" (Psalm 88:16), and its last word is darkness.

Asking for the measure. "Correct me, O Lord, but in justice; not in your anger, lest you bring me to nothing" (Jeremiah 10:24). "O Lord, rebuke me not in your anger, nor discipline me in your wrath" (Psalm 6:1). The correction is accepted. Only the anger is asked to be taken out of it.

Willingly, because of sin. "I will bear the indignation of the Lord because I have sinned against him, until he pleads my cause and executes judgment for me" (Micah 7:9). The one who bears the anger expects the same God to take up his cause.

In lament, as a people. "O God, why do you cast us off forever? Why does your anger smoke against the sheep of your pasture?" (Psalm 74:1). "How long, O Lord? Will you be angry forever?" (79:5). The anger is felt as reaching even prayer: "how long will you be angry with your people's prayers?" (80:4). And, worse, as taking away the will to pray: "There is no one who calls upon your name, who rouses himself to take hold of you" (Isaiah 64:7). Yet the lament still holds on: "But now, O Lord, you are our Father; we are the clay, and you are our potter" (64:8).

From inside the ruin. Lamentations speaks with the voice of the city and of one survivor. "Is it nothing to you, all you who pass by?" (Lamentations 1:12). "I am the man who has seen affliction under the rod of his wrath" (3:1). The body is in it: "He has made my flesh and my skin waste away; he has broken my bones" (3:4). So is the heart: "Pour out your heart like water before the presence of the Lord!" (2:19). In the middle comes hope (3:21–22), and then the confession that God "does not afflict from his heart" (3:33). The book ends without resolving: "unless you have utterly rejected us, and you remain exceedingly angry with us" (5:22).

As the condition of a whole life. "For we are brought to an end by your anger; by your wrath we are dismayed" (Psalm 90:7). The answer the psalm asks for is wisdom: "So teach us to number our days that we may get a heart of wisdom" (90:12).

With grief held back, or poured out. When Aaron's sons died near the holy, he was told not to mourn, "lest you die, and wrath come upon all the congregation", "and Aaron held his peace" (Leviticus 10:6, 3). Later, when the wrath had come, mourning was commanded: "For this put on sackcloth, lament and wail, for the fierce anger of the Lord has not turned back from us" (Jeremiah 4:8).

Watching another live with it. After Uzzah died, David was angry, then afraid, and left the ark with Obed-edom. "And it was told King David, 'The Lord has blessed the household of Obed-edom …' So David went and brought up the ark … with rejoicing" (2 Samuel 6:12). He did not reason his way past his fear. He watched another household live near the holy and be blessed.

Shown its end ahead of time. Daniel was shown the indignation's end ahead of time, and it overwhelmed him: "I fell into a deep sleep with my face to the ground. But he touched me and made me stand up" (Daniel 8:18). He was told that within that time "some of the wise shall stumble, so that they may be refined, purified, and made white" (Daniel 11:35).

The heart that turns, and the heart that does not

What happens next depends on the heart.

Josiah heard the book read and tore his clothes. The answer named his heart: "because your heart was penitent, and you humbled yourself before the Lord … I also have heard you" (2 Kings 22:19). Hezekiah's "heart was proud", and wrath came. "But Hezekiah humbled himself for the pride of his heart … so that the wrath of the Lord did not come upon them in the days of Hezekiah" (2 Chronicles 32:25–26). The same heart provoked the anger and turned it.

Turning can be partial. Rehoboam humbled himself and was spared, "and he did evil, for he did not set his heart to seek the Lord" (2 Chronicles 12:14).

Distress turns people both ways. Under the judges, "the Lord was moved to pity by their groaning" (Judges 2:18). Manasseh, "when he was in distress, he entreated the favor of the Lord his God and humbled himself greatly" (2 Chronicles 33:12). Ahaz, in the same distress, became "yet more faithless" (28:22). The texts do not say why one turns and the other does not. They set the cases side by side.

Some hearts do not take the blow in. "It set him on fire all around, but he did not understand; it burned him up, but he did not take it to heart" (Isaiah 42:25). Others make themselves unable to hear: "They made their hearts diamond-hard lest they should hear the law … Therefore great anger came from the Lord of hosts" (Zechariah 7:12).

And some cover the danger over with false peace. "You say, 'I am innocent; surely his anger has turned from me'" (Jeremiah 2:35). Prophets "say continually to those who despise the word of the Lord, 'It shall be well with you'" (Jeremiah 23:17). John the Baptist meets the same thing: "do not presume to say to yourselves, 'We have Abraham as our father'" (Matthew 3:9).

Chapter 4 follows the heart in anger more fully.

Returning

God calls people back, and the call is to the heart. "Yet even now," declares the Lord, "return to me with all your heart, with fasting, with weeping, and with mourning; and rend your hearts and not your garments" (Joel 2:12–13). The outward signs are not refused, but the heart is what must be torn.

Returning comes with no guarantee. Joel says, "Who knows whether he will not turn and relent" (2:14). The king of Nineveh, with no promise at all, says the same: "Who knows? God may turn and relent and turn from his fierce anger, so that we may not perish" (Jonah 3:9). "When God saw what they did, how they turned from their evil way, God relented" (3:10).

God even gives the words to return with. "Take with you words and return to the Lord; say to him, 'Take away all iniquity; accept what is good …'" (Hosea 14:2). The answer is "I will heal their apostasy; I will love them freely, for my anger has turned from them" (14:4).

Shelter, and who can stand

Some of the time, the call is to shelter while the anger passes. "Come, my people, enter your chambers, and shut your doors behind you; hide yourselves for a little while until the fury has passed by" (Isaiah 26:20). That is exactly what Job had asked for: "Oh that you would hide me in Sheol, that you would conceal me until your wrath be past" (Job 14:13). God offers his people what Job asked.

At the end, the reverse: people call "to the mountains and rocks, 'Fall on us and hide us from the face of him who is seated on the throne, and from the wrath of the Lamb'" (Revelation 6:16). One shelter is in God; the other is from him. Isaiah: "Enter into the rock and hide in the dust from before the terror of the Lord, and from the splendor of his majesty" (Isaiah 2:10), "when he rises to terrify the earth" (2:19).

"Who can stand before his indignation? Who can endure the heat of his anger?" (Nahum 1:6). The next verse answers by pointing to the same God: "The Lord is good, a stronghold in the day of trouble; he knows those who take refuge in him" (1:7).

The psalmist asks a question that joins God's anger and the fear of him: "Who considers the power of your anger, and your wrath according to the fear of you?" (Psalm 90:11). God's deeds are called awesome, and his enemies cringe before them. "How awesome are your deeds! So great is your power that your enemies come cringing to you" (Psalm 66:3). "The Lord will be awesome against them; for he will famish all the gods of the earth" (Zephaniah 2:11).

The hand that is felt

People under God's anger often speak of his hand as heavy. "Day and night your hand was heavy upon me" (Psalm 32:4). "He has made my chains heavy" (Lamentations 3:7). I have looked at these closely, and I notice two things.

First, the texts often correct the feeling from inside. The psalmist's heavy hand came while he kept silent about his sin, and it lifted when he confessed: "I acknowledged my sin to you … and you forgave" (Psalm 32:5). The poet who felt the heavy chains is the one who says God "does not afflict from his heart" (Lamentations 3:33). The servant who said "I knew you to be a hard man" also says "so I was afraid" (Matthew 25:24–25), and the master does not accept it.

Second, the same words for the hand are used in both directions. The "mighty hand" brought Israel out of Egypt (Deuteronomy 6:21). Later God says to Israel, "I myself will fight against you with outstretched hand and strong arm, in anger and in fury" (Jeremiah 21:5).

I have found no text in which God's own heart is hardened. The one place where his inner self turns away from a people is this: "Though Moses and Samuel stood before me, yet my heart would not turn toward this people" (Jeremiah 15:1).

Delivered from the wrath to come

The New Testament speaks of wrath still to come, and of people who know they are delivered from it. "We all once lived in the passions of our flesh, carrying out the desires of the flesh and the mind, and were by nature children of wrath, like the rest of mankind. But God, being rich in mercy …" (Ephesians 2:3–4). "Since, therefore, we have now been justified by his blood, much more shall we be saved by him from the wrath of God" (Romans 5:9). "God has not destined us for wrath, but to obtain salvation" (1 Thessalonians 5:9).

What they do with this knowledge is not fear. They wait "for his Son from heaven … Jesus who delivers us from the wrath to come" (1 Thessalonians 1:10). They "encourage one another and build one another up" (5:11).

Jesus speaks of a rising that ends in judgment: "those who have done evil to the resurrection of judgment" (John 5:29). Daniel was told that some "shall awake … to shame and everlasting contempt" (Daniel 12:2). Chapter 2 (The body raised) sets these beside the resurrection of life. Revelation names "the second death", and its list opens with "the cowardly, the faithless" (Revelation 21:8).

It changes how they treat their enemies. "Beloved, never avenge yourselves, but leave it to the wrath of God … To the contrary, 'if your enemy is hungry, feed him'" (Romans 12:19–20). The psalmists had asked God to pour out his anger on their enemies. Here the same confidence that wrath belongs to God leads to feeding the enemy.

And it changes how they treat their own anger. "On account of these the wrath of God is coming … But now you must put them all away: anger, wrath, malice, slander, and obscene talk from your mouth" (Colossians 3:6, 8).

At the very end, those who have come through sing. They "sing the song of Moses … and the song of the Lamb, saying, 'Great and amazing are your deeds, O Lord God the Almighty! Just and true are your ways'" (Revelation 15:3). Others, struck by the same plagues, "cursed the name of God … They did not repent and give him glory" (16:9). The same wrath meets two kinds of heart.

The responses, gathered

Gathered together, the verses show a person responding to God's anger in these ways. Each is stated by its own verses.

  • Pleading: "Oh let not the Lord be angry, and I will speak" (Genesis 18:30).
  • Humbling oneself: "when he humbled himself the wrath of the Lord turned from him" (2 Chronicles 12:12).
  • Acting to remove the cause: Hezekiah's covenant (2 Chronicles 29:10).
  • Reading the ruin: "Why has the Lord done thus to this land? What caused the heat of this great anger?" (Deuteronomy 29:24).
  • Crying out from distress: "when the people of Israel cried out to the Lord, the Lord raised up a deliverer" (Judges 3:9).
  • Refusing the warning, "until there was no remedy" (2 Chronicles 36:16).
  • Asking for correction without the anger: "Correct me, O Lord, but in justice; not in your anger" (Jeremiah 10:24).
  • Fearing being cast off: "Turn not your servant away in anger" (Psalm 27:9).
  • Asking how long: "How long, O Lord? Will you be angry forever?" (Psalm 79:5).
  • Standing in awe: "Who can stand before you when once your anger is roused?" (Psalm 76:7).
  • Taking it into the whole of life: "For all our days pass away under your wrath" (Psalm 90:9), and the prayer, "So teach us to number our days that we may get a heart of wisdom" (90:12).
  • Sheltering while it passes: "hide yourselves for a little while until the fury has passed by" (Isaiah 26:20).
  • Holding a false peace: "surely his anger has turned from me" (Jeremiah 2:35).
  • Mourning: "For this put on sackcloth, lament and wail" (Jeremiah 4:8).
  • Not taking it in: "it burned him up, but he did not take it to heart" (Isaiah 42:25).
  • Coming to know: "And they shall know that I am the Lord" (Ezekiel 5:13).
  • Returning without a guarantee: "Who knows?" (Jonah 3:9).
  • Bearing it because of one's sin: "I will bear the indignation of the Lord because I have sinned against him, until he pleads my cause" (Micah 7:9).
  • Leaving vengeance to God: "never avenge yourselves, but leave it to the wrath of God" (Romans 12:19), and then, "if your enemy is hungry, feed him" (12:20).
  • Putting off what the wrath comes on: "But now you must put them all away: anger, wrath, malice, slander, and obscene talk from your mouth" (Colossians 3:8), two verses after "On account of these the wrath of God is coming" (3:6).
  • Singing that it is just: "Just and true are your ways" (Revelation 15:3).
  • Speaking of it as a wrath one is delivered from: "Jesus who delivers us from the wrath to come" (1 Thessalonians 1:10).

Distress under the anger goes two ways, and the verses do not say why. Ahaz "in the time of his distress … became yet more faithless to the Lord" (2 Chronicles 28:22). Manasseh, "when he was in distress, he entreated the favor of the Lord his God and humbled himself greatly" (2 Chronicles 33:12).

Prayer and the anger meet in several ways.

  • The anger reaches the prayer: "how long will you be angry with your people's prayers?" (Psalm 80:4).
  • It blocks it: "you have wrapped yourself with a cloud so that no prayer can pass through" (Lamentations 3:44).
  • Intercession is forbidden: "As for you, do not pray for this people" (Jeremiah 7:16).
  • No one prays: "There is no one who calls upon your name" (Isaiah 64:7).
  • Prayer is answered in kind: "As I called, and they would not hear, so they called, and I would not hear" (Zechariah 7:13).
  • Prayer rests on mercy alone: "we do not present our pleas before you because of our righteousness, but because of your great mercy" (Daniel 9:18).

I had thought of God's anger as something that happens to people. Reading the verses for the human side, I find that it is also something people live in relation to: provoking, pleading, reading, bearing, turning, hiding, waiting and, at the last, singing.

Two books end on an unanswered question. Lamentations ends with "unless you have utterly rejected us" (Lamentations 5:22). Jonah ends with God asking, "should not I pity Nineveh?" (Jonah 4:11). I leave them open, as Scripture does.

Those who carry it out

Some people carry out God's anger, and the verses judge how they do it.

  • Assyria is the rod, and has its own heart: "Woe to Assyria, the rod of my anger … But he does not so intend, and his heart does not so think; but it is in his heart to destroy" (Isaiah 10:5, 7).
  • Babylon goes too far: "I gave them into your hand; you showed them no mercy" (Isaiah 47:6). "While I was angry but a little, they furthered the disaster" (Zechariah 1:15).
  • Israel's army goes past the commission: "you have killed them in a rage that has reached up to heaven" (2 Chronicles 28:9).
  • Saul is judged for not carrying it out: "Because you did not obey the voice of the Lord and did not carry out his fierce wrath against Amalek, therefore the Lord has done this thing to you this day" (1 Samuel 28:18).
  • The governing authority: "he is the servant of God, an avenger who carries out God's wrath on the wrongdoer. Therefore one must be in subjection, not only to avoid God's wrath but also for the sake of conscience" (Romans 13:4–5).

Other parties

Each party that comes into anger's verses does so in its own way.

  • The Spirit of God comes with anger. "And the Spirit of God rushed upon Saul when he heard these words, and his anger was greatly kindled" (1 Samuel 11:6). The Spirit rushed upon Samson, and he went down to Ashkelon and struck down thirty men; after it, "In hot anger he went back to his father's house" (Judges 14:19). Ezekiel, lifted by the Spirit, went "in the heat of my spirit, the hand of the Lord being strong upon me" (Ezekiel 3:14). The anger list in Ephesians is set beside the Spirit grieved (Ephesians 4:30–31).
  • The prophet is filled with God's wrath: "Therefore I am full of the wrath of the Lord; I am weary of holding it in" (Jeremiah 6:11). "I sat alone, because your hand was upon me, for you had filled me with indignation" (Jeremiah 15:17).
  • Angels. The angel of the Lord stood in Balaam's way, seen by the donkey and not by the man: "God's anger was kindled because he went, and the angel of the Lord took his stand in the way as his adversary" (Numbers 22:22). Balaam's own anger fell on the donkey (22:27). God "let loose on them his burning anger … a company of destroying angels" (Psalm 78:49). An angel asked how long: "O Lord of hosts, how long will you have no mercy on Jerusalem … against which you have been angry these seventy years?", and "the Lord answered gracious and comforting words" (Zechariah 1:12–13).
  • The devil: "Woe to you, O earth and sea, for the devil has come down to you in great wrath, because he knows that his time is short!" (Revelation 12:12).
  • The master in Jesus' parables, a figure standing for God. "In anger his master delivered him to the jailers" (Matthew 18:34), after "should not you have had mercy on your fellow servant, as I had mercy on you?" (18:33). The applied lesson places the requirement in the hearer's heart: "So also my heavenly Father will do to every one of you, if you do not forgive your brother from your heart" (18:35). In another parable the anger turns into a wider invitation: "the master of the house became angry and said to his servant, 'Go out quickly … and bring in the poor and crippled and blind and lame'" (Luke 14:21).
  • Onlookers: "Do not rejoice when your enemy falls … lest the Lord see it and be displeased, and turn away his anger from him" (Proverbs 24:17–18).
  • The physical world bears it. "My anger and my wrath will be poured out on this place, upon man and beast, upon the trees of the field and the fruit of the ground" (Jeremiah 7:20). "The foundations also of the mountains trembled and quaked, because he was angry" (Psalm 18:7).
  • Enemies' anger, as the one attacked bears it: "then they would have swallowed us up alive, when their anger was kindled against us" (Psalm 124:3). Answered by God's hand: "you stretch out your hand against the wrath of my enemies, and your right hand delivers me" (Psalm 138:7). And by "do not fear": "Be careful, be quiet, do not fear, and do not let your heart be faint because of these two smoldering stumps of firebrands, at the fierce anger of Rezin" (Isaiah 7:4). "By faith he left Egypt, not being afraid of the anger of the king" (Hebrews 11:27).
  • A ruler's anger, as those beneath it bear it: "A king's wrath is a messenger of death, and a wise man will appease it" (Proverbs 16:14).

What changes it

The verses show anger changing character with its circumstances.

  • Who is angry. The same words are used for God and for people, and "for I am God and not a man" (Hosea 11:9) is said in restraint.
  • Whether what sets it off is true. Potiphar acted on a lie (Genesis 39:19).
  • For whom it burns. For the widow and fatherless (Exodus 22:24); for the children (Mark 10:14); for one wronged (2 Samuel 12:5).
  • What it is joined with in the verse. Jealousy (Deuteronomy 6:15); grief, "with anger, grieved at their hardness of heart" (Mark 3:5); the Spirit (1 Samuel 11:6); hatred, "the anger and envy that you showed because of your hatred" (Ezekiel 35:11); fear, "he shall be afraid and withdraw, and shall turn back and be enraged" (Daniel 11:30); compassion set against it (Hosea 11:8–9).
  • Time. God's "for a moment" (Psalm 30:5) and "finished" (Revelation 15:1); a man's wrath kept "forever" (Amos 1:11); "do not let the sun go down on your anger" (Ephesians 4:26).
  • Power. The ruler's anger becomes a command (Daniel 2:12).
  • The one who carries it out, and their own heart (Isaiah 10:7).
  • One and many (Numbers 16:22; Joshua 7:1).
  • What meets it. A soft answer, a servants' question, a father who comes out, a counsellor (Proverbs 15:1; 2 Kings 5:13; Luke 15:28; Acts 5:34–35). Or no one: "I looked, but there was no one to help" (Isaiah 63:5).
  • Correction with or without the anger: "Correct me, O Lord, but in justice; not in your anger" (Jeremiah 10:24).
  • The measure: "storing up wrath for yourself on the day of wrath" (Romans 2:5).
  • The same root, rendered as another movement. ʿEbrāh is wrath and pride (Proverbs 21:24). Kāʿas is provoking God and Hannah's grief (1 Samuel 1:16). Ḥēmāh is wrath and venom (Psalm 140:3). Paroxysmos is a sharp disagreement and stirring up to love (Acts 15:39; Hebrews 10:24). Thymos is God's wrath and Babylon's passion (Revelation 14:8, 10).

Chapter 11 (§4) gathers what changes the character of a word across all the activities. These are anger's part in it.

Connections

Anger touches many of the activities in this Part. The verses join it to the others in their own wording: by "so", "therefore" and "lest", by "but", by naming them together, and by sharing a root.

  • Fear (10.12): Moses afraid of the anger, and heard (Deuteronomy 9:19); afraid, then enraged (Daniel 11:30); angry, then afraid (2 Samuel 6:8–9); not afraid of the king's anger (Hebrews 11:27). The two share a root in rāgaz, "to tremble", rendered "Be angry" (Psalm 4:4). "Who considers the power of your anger, and your wrath according to the fear of you?" (Psalm 90:11).
  • Feeling (10.1): anger beside grief. Jonathan "was grieved for David" (1 Samuel 20:34). Jesus was angry and grieved at once (Mark 3:5). "Godly grief produces a repentance", and with it indignation (2 Corinthians 7:10–11).
  • Knowing and hearing (10.2): anger is kindled on hearing, and God's anger is said to end in knowing (Ezekiel 5:13) and to be understood later (Jeremiah 23:20).
  • Remembering (10.3): "Remember and do not forget how you provoked the Lord your God to wrath" (Deuteronomy 9:7). When the king's anger abated, "he remembered Vashti" (Esther 2:1).
  • Speaking (10.5): "A soft answer turns away wrath" (Proverbs 15:1); rash words from an embittered spirit (Psalm 106:33); "slow to speak, slow to anger" (James 1:19).
  • Wanting (10.6): jealousy with anger (Proverbs 6:34); "You covet and cannot obtain, so you fight and quarrel" (James 4:2).
  • Relating to God (10.9): God's anger, provoked, pleaded against, borne and turned.
  • Relating to others (10.10): the ruler's anger; strife between people; turning another's anger away.
  • Going wrong (Chapter 12): the lists of what is put away; "one given to anger causes much transgression" (Proverbs 29:22).
  • Made new (Chapter 14): putting off anger with the old self (Colossians 3:8–10); delivered from the wrath to come.
  • Life and death (Chapter 2): "the wrath of God remains on him" (John 3:36); "A king's wrath is a messenger of death" (Proverbs 16:14).
  • The heart and the spirit (Chapters 4 and 6): anger burns within, lodges in the heart of fools, and comes in the heat of the spirit.
