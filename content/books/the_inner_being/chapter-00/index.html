The Inner Being

Introduction

  2. What the study is
  3. How it is put together

This is a study of the part of a person no one can see. Scripture calls it "the hidden person of the heart" (1 Peter 3:4). I call it the inner being: how we think, feel, choose and relate to others, and how we turn ourselves toward meaning and toward God.

I want to say briefly what the study is, where it came from, and how it is put together, so that you know what you are reading.

Where it began

I began in January 2026 with a wish to understand spirit, soul and body better than the accounts I knew allowed. That first work produced two long studies: one on the Holy Spirit, and one on the make-up of a human being.

When I read further into the second, I saw that I had only touched the surface. A first count of what the soul does came to twenty-eight things. The verses plainly held far more. So I turned to a fuller exploration of the soul, within the wider frame of the whole inner being. That way soul, spirit and body could be looked at together, as they are actually related, rather than one at a time.

What the study is

The study rests on a list of words. I gathered the English words that name something in the inner life, about 180 of them, and traced each to the Hebrew and Greek words the Bible uses for it. To decide which words belong, I used one working description:

the non-physical, internal states, capacities and expressions that make up a person's invisible life: how a person thinks, feels, chooses, relates, and orients themselves toward meaning, others and God.

This description is a filter, not a doctrine of the soul. Where a word sat near its edge, I kept it in. A word wrongly left out is a loss no one sees. A word wrongly kept in can be noticed and set aside later.

From the words come the verses. A verse belongs to the study because one of these Hebrew or Greek words stands in it, not because it suits a point I want to make. That gives several thousand verses, and the aim is to read every one of them in its own setting.

I started with a question about parts. Am I two parts or three? Chapter 1 tells how that question gave way to another one: how does the inner being work, as Scripture itself describes it? This book is my answer so far.

How it is put together

The inner being does not divide neatly into compartments. Fear reaches into choosing, anger into speaking, desire into how we treat each other. So I do not work through the words one by one in a fixed order. I take one theme at a time, such as fear, anger, choosing, wanting, humility or pride, and follow it wherever its verses lead. Each theme opens onto others. Over time the threads come together into one picture.

For each theme I gather every verse that carries its words. I read each one in its passage, asking what it shows about the inner life: what moves in it, what moves it, and what follows. A word often plays a small part in a verse whose main action lies elsewhere, so I read for the main action first. The same word can also change character with its setting. Anger, fear and jealousy each have more than one face, and I try to keep each face, with the circumstances in which it appears and the reason the verses give.

What I find is then written into the chapters, not added on at the end. A new theme often changes what an earlier chapter said, and when it does, the earlier chapter changes. The new reading is not bent to fit the old account. That is why the book is never finished: each theme read adds to it, and some chapters have been rewritten many times.

Three things keep the account honest:

  • Every statement rests on verses. Wherever I say more than a verse says, the statement is kept in a separate record with the verses it rests on. If I cannot point to the verses, the statement does not stay. Where I give my own reading, I say so.
  • Verses are not dropped. A verse that does not sit easily with what I have written is a reason to rewrite, not a reason to leave the verse out.
  • Open questions stay open. Questions I cannot yet answer are noted and held, and taken up again when the verses that bear on them are read.

The book's shape follows the order of what the verses show. It opens with life, breath and death. It moves through the words for the inner person (heart, soul, spirit, flesh, conscience), then to the inner being at work in each of its activities, then to what goes wrong, to other beings, God above all, and to how a person is made new. God's own acts are not the subject of this study; they appear wherever they shape or reach the human inner life. The Holy Spirit has a study of its own.

All quotations are from the English Standard Version, and each one is checked against the text. I read with the Hebrew and Greek alongside, but you do not need either to follow what I write. I have had help with the reading and with keeping the records in order, but the reading itself, and what is written here, is mine to answer for.

What comes later

The Bible is the main way into this subject, but not the only one. Medicine, psychology and the study of the brain look at the same ground from outside. I intend to set what they observe beside what Scripture says, as a second view and not an equal one. Where the two agree I will say so; where they differ I will let the difference stand. That work comes later, after the biblical reading.

"I praise you, for I am fearfully and wonderfully made" (Psalm 139:14). This study is an attempt to look carefully at how.
