The Inner Being

13.1 Divine

In this chapter

  1. Choosing
  2. Purposing
  3. Willing

This section gathers what the verses say of God in his own right, as each part of the book is taken up again. So far it holds what the verses on choosing, on being chosen, and on the others around a choice say of God, what the verses on planning and purposing say of his plans, and what the verses on willing say of his will. What his part means for the one who chooses is in 10.7.

Choosing

The verses on a person's choosing keep speaking of God. They speak of him in his own right, not only as the one a person chooses.

He chooses. The verb used of a person's choosing is also used of his:

  • a place: "the place that the Lord your God will choose out of all your tribes to put his name and make his habitation there" (Deuteronomy 12:5)
  • people, for a purpose: "God made a choice among you, that by my mouth the Gentiles should hear the word of the gospel and believe" (Acts 15:7)
  • an answer to people's choosing: "I also will choose harsh treatment for them and bring their fears upon them, because when I called, no one answered, when I spoke, they did not listen" (Isaiah 66:4). The verse gives the reason for his choice.

He chooses before anything is in the one chosen. "he chose us in him before the foundation of the world" (Ephesians 1:4). He chooses those whom others pass over: "has not God chosen those who are poor in the world to be rich in faith" (James 2:5). He chooses witnesses who are "blind, yet have eyes" (Isaiah 43:8), "that you may know and believe me and understand that I am he" (43:10). His choosing is spoken of with his own love and pleasure:

  • "In love he predestined us for adoption to himself as sons through Jesus Christ, according to the purpose of his will" (Ephesians 1:4–5)
  • "among my father's sons he took pleasure in me to make me king over all Israel" (1 Chronicles 28:4)
  • "who chose me above your father and above all his house" (2 Samuel 6:21)

He has eyes and delight, and they are the measure of a choice. The same verse goes on: "but they did what was evil in my eyes and chose that in which I did not delight" (Isaiah 66:4). Its opposite is those "who choose the things that please me" (56:4).

He acts first. At Shechem he speaks of what he has done before anyone chooses: "I took your father Abraham from beyond the River" (Joshua 24:3); "I gave you a land on which you had not labored" (24:13). In Isaiah he offers himself to people who did not look for him:

  • "I was ready to be sought by those who did not ask for me" (Isaiah 65:1)
  • "I said, 'Here I am, here I am,' to a nation that was not called by my name" (65:1)
  • "I spread out my hands all the day to a rebellious people" (65:2)

He is good, upright and holy. His instructing comes from his goodness: "Good and upright is the Lord; therefore he instructs sinners in the way" (Psalm 25:8). "The friendship of the Lord is for those who fear him" (25:14). Joshua tells the people who have just chosen him what he is: "he is a holy God. He is a jealous God" (Joshua 24:19).

He knows the heart, and gives.

  • "God, who knows the heart, bore witness to them, by giving them the Holy Spirit just as he did to us" (Acts 15:8).
  • "thanks be to God, who put into the heart of Titus the same earnest care I have for you" (2 Corinthians 8:16).
  • The church's decision is spoken as one with the Spirit's: "it has seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us" (Acts 15:28).

He searches hearts, and acts on them. "the Lord searches all hearts and understands every plan and thought" (1 Chronicles 28:9). He acts on the hearts of people who are not his chosen, and the verses give his purpose:

  • of those who "refused to love the truth": "Therefore God sends them a strong delusion, so that they may believe what is false" (2 Thessalonians 2:10–11)
  • of the kings who give their power to the beast: "for God has put it into their hearts to carry out his purpose by being of one mind" (Revelation 17:17)
  • of the rest of Israel, who sought and did not obtain: "God gave them a spirit of stupor, eyes that would not see and ears that would not hear" (Romans 11:8). Paul at once refuses to make it the end: "did they stumble in order that they might fall? By no means!" (11:11). The remnant is "chosen by grace" (11:5).
  • of those who do not believe: "They stumble because they disobey the word, as they were destined to do" (1 Peter 2:8). The verse gives the disobedience as the cause, and does not explain the rest.

Solomon prays to him as the one who alone knows hearts: "for you, you only, know the hearts of all the children of mankind" (1 Kings 8:39). He also asks him to do the turning: "that he may incline our hearts to him" (8:58).

He answers those who set themselves against his choosing. The verses show others resenting the one he chose, and they show him answering.

  • He counts it as against himself, and makes his choice plain. "What is Aaron that you grumble against him?" (Numbers 16:11). "And the staff of the man whom I choose shall sprout. Thus I will make to cease from me the grumblings of the people of Israel, which they grumble against you" (17:5). The staff is kept "that you may make an end of their grumblings against me, lest they die" (17:10).
  • He turns contempt round by his faithfulness. To the one "deeply despised, abhorred by the nation": "Kings shall see and arise; princes, and they shall prostrate themselves; because of the Lord, who is faithful, the Holy One of Israel, who has chosen you" (Isaiah 49:7).
  • He sets his choosing beside the fixed order of creation. When people say "The Lord has rejected the two clans that he chose" (Jeremiah 33:24), he answers: "If I have not established my covenant with day and night and the fixed order of heaven and earth, then I will reject the offspring of Jacob and David my servant … For I will restore their fortunes and will have mercy on them" (33:25–26).
  • He is hated first. "If the world hates you, know that it has hated me before it hated you" (John 15:18). "I chose you out of the world, therefore the world hates you" (15:19).

He calls those who do not know him, for the sake of his chosen. "For the sake of my servant Jacob, and Israel my chosen, I call you by your name, I name you, though you do not know me. … I equip you, though you do not know me, that people may know, from the rising of the sun and from the west, that there is none besides me" (Isaiah 45:4–6).

He blesses at the place he chose, so that his people rejoice. "because the Lord your God will bless you in all your produce and in all the work of your hands, so that you will be altogether joyful" (Deuteronomy 16:15). Those who return to him he gathers there: "I will gather them and bring them to the place that I have chosen, to make my name dwell there" (Nehemiah 1:9).

What he chooses is turned toward others. "Is not this the fast that I choose: to loose the bonds of wickedness, to undo the straps of the yoke, to let the oppressed go free, and to break every yoke?" (Isaiah 58:6). The self-humbling offered beside oppression is not what he chooses (58:3–5).

His wrath is turned away when his chosen one stands before him. "Therefore he said he would destroy them—had not Moses, his chosen one, stood in the breach before him, to turn away his wrath from destroying them" (Psalm 106:23). His anger is taken up in 10.13.

He remembers, and he does not remember. "He remembers his covenant forever, the word that he commanded, for a thousand generations" (Psalm 105:8). "For he remembered his holy promise, and Abraham, his servant" (105:42). When his chosen people had forgotten him, "For their sake he remembered his covenant, and relented according to the abundance of his steadfast love" (106:45). Of their sins he says the opposite: "I, I am he who blots out your transgressions for my own sake, and I will not remember your sins" (Isaiah 43:25). To the one he has chosen: "you will not be forgotten by me" (44:21).

He reasons, answers and judges.

  • He invites a people to reason with him: "Come now, let us reason together, says the Lord" (Isaiah 1:18).
  • He answers a person's verdict on himself. The eunuch said "Behold, I am a dry tree" (56:3), and the Lord says "I will give them an everlasting name that shall not be cut off" (56:5).
  • He gives the last word on those who weighed Job's choices: "you have not spoken of me what is right, as my servant Job has" (Job 42:7).

Purposing

The words used for a person who plans, devises or purposes are also used of God. The verses do not give his plans as a hidden decree working out of sight. They show him forming a plan and saying it aloud, setting it against what people devise, holding it open to their turning, carrying it out, and keeping it fixed where he has sworn.

He forms a plan and makes it known. "Therefore hear the plan that the Lord has made against Edom and the purposes that he has formed against the inhabitants of Teman" (Jeremiah 49:20). The same words are spoken against Babylon (50:45). In both places the plan follows his question, "For who is like me? Who will summon me?" (49:19; 50:44). Of Babylon it is said that "the Lord has both planned and done what he spoke" (51:12). The verse before shows how it went forward, through other people's spirits: "The Lord has stirred up the spirit of the kings of the Medes, because his purpose concerning Babylon is to destroy it" (51:11). His counsel is something to be told in full. Paul counts his own innocence in not holding it back: "I did not shrink from declaring to you the whole counsel of God" (Acts 20:27).

His devising answers what people devise. Micah speaks first of those "who devise wickedness and work evil on their beds" (Micah 2:1), who "covet fields and seize them" (2:2). Then: "Therefore thus says the Lord: behold, against this family I am devising disaster" (2:3). The verb is the same for both, and the "therefore" ties his devising to theirs. In Jeremiah his plan and theirs stand in one exchange:

  • He says: "Behold, I am shaping disaster against you and devising a plan against you. Return, every one from his evil way" (Jeremiah 18:11).
  • They answer: "That is in vain! We will follow our own plans, and will every one act according to the stubbornness of his evil heart" (18:12).

He says what he intends so that people may turn, and he relents. At the potter's house he says, "like the clay in the potter's hand, so are you in my hand" (Jeremiah 18:6). He sets out both directions:

  • "if that nation, concerning which I have spoken, turns from its evil, I will relent of the disaster that I intended to do to it" (18:8)
  • "if it does evil in my sight, not listening to my voice, then I will relent of the good that I had intended to do to it" (18:10)

His intention is spoken so that it can be heard. "It may be they will listen, and every one turn from his evil way, that I may relent of the disaster that I intend to do to them" (26:3). It is written in a scroll for the same reason: "that every one may turn from his evil way, and that I may forgive their iniquity and their sin" (36:3). I read these verses as saying that the hearing and turning of the people belong to what happens to the plan. The verses themselves say "it may be", and leave the outcome open.

He also purposes and does not turn back. "I have spoken; I have purposed; I have not relented, nor will I turn back" (Jeremiah 4:28). The verse before it sets a limit inside the judgment: "yet I will not make a full end" (4:27). Zechariah sets a fixed purpose for disaster beside a fixed purpose for good: "As I purposed to bring disaster to you when your fathers provoked me to wrath, and I did not relent … so again have I purposed in these days to bring good to Jerusalem and to the house of Judah; fear not" (Zechariah 8:14–15). When he wished to show "the unchangeable character of his purpose, he guaranteed it with an oath" (Hebrews 6:17). The verses say both that he relents and that he does not relent or turn back. None of them explains how the two stand together, and I leave them as they are written.

What he purposed is carried out, and people see afterward that it was his. "The Lord has done what he purposed; he has carried out his word, which he commanded long ago" (Lamentations 2:17). "The Lord determined to lay in ruins the wall of the daughter of Zion; he stretched out the measuring line" (2:8). In Zechariah the fathers looked back and recognised it: "As the Lord of hosts purposed to deal with us for our ways and deeds, so has he dealt with us" (Zechariah 1:6).

He has plans for welfare, and takes thought for the one in need.

  • "For I know the plans I have for you, declares the Lord, plans for welfare and not for evil, to give you a future and a hope" (Jeremiah 29:11). This is said to exiles, of what will come "When seventy years are completed" (29:10).
  • "As for me, I am poor and needy, but the Lord takes thought for me" (Psalm 40:17).
  • Against the fact that "We must all die", the wise woman of Tekoa says, "God will not take away life, and he devises means so that the banished one will not remain an outcast" (2 Samuel 14:14).

His plan takes in what people do against him, and does not excuse them. "this Jesus, delivered up according to the definite plan and foreknowledge of God, you crucified and killed by the hands of lawless men" (Acts 2:23). The plan and the guilt are in one sentence. The church, praying Psalm 2, says that those who gathered against Jesus did "whatever your hand and your plan had predestined to take place" (4:28). They then ask, "grant to your servants to continue to speak your word with all boldness" (4:29). Joseph says to his brothers, "you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good" (Genesis 50:20); the same verb is used for both.

His purpose stands before the ages and does not rest on works.

  • "because of his own purpose and grace, which he gave us in Christ Jesus before the ages began" (2 Timothy 1:9)
  • "though they were not yet born and had done nothing either good or bad—in order that God's purpose of election might continue, not because of works but because of him who calls" (Romans 9:11)
  • "according to the purpose of him who works all things according to the counsel of his will" (Ephesians 1:11)

It is made known in Christ: "the mystery of his will, according to his purpose, which he set forth in Christ as a plan for the fullness of time, to unite all things in Christ" (Ephesians 1:9–10). It is "the eternal purpose that he has realized in Christ Jesus our Lord" (3:11). Christ is the one "whom God put forward as a propitiation by his blood … to show God's righteousness" (Romans 3:25).

The Spirit intercedes according to the will of God. Where people do not know how to pray, the Spirit prays, and his praying is matched to God's will: "Likewise the Spirit helps us in our weakness. For we do not know what to pray for as we ought, but the Spirit himself intercedes for us with groanings too deep for words. And he who searches hearts knows what is the mind of the Spirit, because the Spirit intercedes for the saints according to the will of God" (Romans 8:26–27). The one who searches hearts knows the Spirit's mind. Paul does not say here what "mind" is, and I leave the word as he gives it.

It is turned toward those who love him. The next verse goes straight on: "And we know that for those who love God all things work together for good, for those who are called according to his purpose" (Romans 8:28).

A person can refuse it, or serve it. "the Pharisees and the lawyers rejected the purpose of God for themselves" (Luke 7:30), while the people and the tax collectors "declared God just" (7:29). "David, after he had served the purpose of God in his own generation, fell asleep" (Acts 13:36).

What these verses mean for a person is not taken up here. Each touches the inner life in its own way:

  • the plan spoken so that a person may hear and turn
  • the purpose sworn as "a sure and steadfast anchor of the soul" (Hebrews 6:19)
  • "when you seek me with all your heart" (Jeremiah 29:13)
  • "we do not know what to pray for as we ought" (Romans 8:26), the weakness the Spirit helps
  • the heart poured out "like water before the presence of the Lord" after his purpose was carried out (Lamentations 2:19)
  • "do not devise evil in your hearts against one another" (Zechariah 8:17), said straight after his own purposing for good

These belong with a person's own planning, in 10.7 (Planning).

Willing

The words used for a person's willing are used of God too, and of the Son and the Spirit. Where God is "not willing", the verses almost always give the reason. Where his will is spoken of positively, it is free and it stands.

He is not willing to destroy, and the verses say why.

  • After intercession, Moses says: "I myself stayed on the mountain … and the Lord listened to me that time also. The Lord was unwilling to destroy you" (Deuteronomy 10:10).
  • For a promise: "Yet the Lord was not willing to destroy Judah, for the sake of David his servant, since he promised to give a lamp to him and to his sons forever" (2 Kings 8:19). Chronicles says "because of the covenant that he had made with David" (2 Chronicles 21:7). Both verses follow a king who "did what was evil in the sight of the Lord".
  • In grace: "the Lord was gracious to them and had compassion on them, and he turned toward them, because of his covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, and would not destroy them" (2 Kings 13:23).
  • Out of love, against a curse that was paid for: "the Lord your God would not listen to Balaam; instead the Lord your God turned the curse into a blessing for you, because the Lord your God loved you" (Deuteronomy 23:5; Joshua 24:10).

He is not willing to forgive, and the verses say why.

  • One man "blesses himself in his heart, saying, 'I shall be safe, though I walk in the stubbornness of my heart'" (Deuteronomy 29:19). Of him: "The Lord will not be willing to forgive him" (29:20). The verse names what the man said to himself.
  • Manasseh "filled Jerusalem with innocent blood, and the Lord would not pardon" (2 Kings 24:4).

His "not willing" runs both ways. In each case the verses name what it rests on: intercession, promise, covenant, grace, love, a heart that blesses itself, or innocent blood.

He does as he wills. Nebuchadnezzar learned it by being humbled: "till you know that the Most High rules the kingdom of men and gives it to whom he will" (Daniel 4:25; 4:17, 32; 5:21). When his reason returned, he said, "he does according to his will among the host of heaven and among the inhabitants of the earth; and none can stay his hand or say to him, 'What have you done?'" (4:35). He set it beside "those who walk in pride he is able to humble" (4:37).

Paul writes: "So then he has mercy on whomever he wills, and he hardens whomever he wills" (Romans 9:18). He gives the objection in full, "Why does he still find fault? For who can resist his will?" (9:19), and answers it with the potter: "Will what is molded say to its molder, 'Why have you made me like this?'" (9:20). Jeremiah also uses the potter, for plans that God may relent of (Purposing). The two passages use the same picture for different points. I do not join them.

His will brings to life, and waits.

  • "Of his own will he brought us forth by the word of truth, that we should be a kind of firstfruits of his creatures" (James 1:18). The verses before set it against human desire, which "when it has conceived gives birth to sin" (1:15), and they name the Father "with whom there is no variation or shadow due to change" (1:17).
  • "The Lord is not slow to fulfill his promise as some count slowness, but is patient toward you, not wishing that any should perish, but that all should reach repentance" (2 Peter 3:9).
  • His will and his purpose stand together: "when God desired to show more convincingly to the heirs of the promise the unchangeable character of his purpose, he guaranteed it with an oath" (Hebrews 6:17).

The Father's will, the Son's choosing, the Spirit's willing.

  • "yes, Father, for such was your gracious will. All things have been handed over to me by my Father … and anyone to whom the Son chooses to reveal him" (Matthew 11:26–27). Luke adds that Jesus said it as "he rejoiced in the Holy Spirit" (Luke 10:21–22). The word for the Son's choosing is the will word.
  • The Spirit gives gifts "to each one individually as he wills" (1 Corinthians 12:11).

At Gethsemane the Son's will is given up to the Father's. "Father, if you are willing, remove this cup from me. Nevertheless, not my will, but yours, be done." And there appeared to him an angel from heaven, strengthening him. And being in agony he prayed more earnestly (Luke 22:42–44). It is the one place in these verses where two wills are named side by side and one is given up to the other. What it means for a person's own will is in 10.7 (Willing).

Nature. "Is the wild ox willing to serve you? Will he spend the night at your manger?" (Job 39:9). It is one of the creatures God names as beyond a man's control. 13.4 takes up nature.
