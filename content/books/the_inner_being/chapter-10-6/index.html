The Inner Being

10.6 Wanting

The soul is the wanting self (Chapter 5). In Hebrew, your soul desires is simply how you say you want. The heart has desires too: "Delight yourself in the Lord, and he will give you the desires of your heart" (Psalm 37:4). And in the New Testament the flesh becomes the great subject of wrong desire (Chapter 7).

Desire can be aimed at nothing. The Preacher tried everything his heart wanted, and it was "vanity and a striving after wind" (Ecclesiastes 2:11). "Better is the sight of the eyes than the wandering of the appetite [soul]: this also is vanity and a striving after wind" (Ecclesiastes 6:9).

Wanting speaks in the heart, and it drives thinking. "Let them not say in their hearts, 'Aha, our heart's desire!'" (Psalm 35:25). The rich man in Jesus' parable shows the whole chain. Wanting leads to thinking: "he thought to himself, 'What shall I do, for I have nowhere to store my crops?'" (Luke 12:17). Thinking leads to a plan: "I will tear down my barns and build larger ones" (12:18). The plan ends in a word spoken to his own soul: "Soul, you have ample goods laid up for many years; relax, eat, drink, be merry" (12:19).

Wanting stands beside choosing, and the words can cross. When the king says to Barzillai "all that you desire of me I will do for you" (2 Samuel 19:38), "desire" is the Hebrew verb for choosing. Elsewhere the verses set what a person wants next to what they choose:

  • Desire: "For they shall be ashamed of the oaks that you desired; and you shall blush for the gardens that you have chosen" (Isaiah 1:29).
  • The soul's delight: "These have chosen their own ways, and their soul delights in their abominations" (Isaiah 66:3).
  • Envy, named before the choice it would lead to: "Do not envy a man of violence and do not choose any of his ways" (Proverbs 3:31).
  • Wanting weighed against something better: of wisdom, "nothing you desire can compare with her" (Proverbs 3:15).

A desire still has to be carried through. Paul writes "so that your readiness in desiring it may be matched by your completing it" (2 Corinthians 8:11). Section 10.7 gives the account of choosing.

A want can be met and still be refused. "David said longingly, 'Oh, that someone would give me water to drink from the well of Bethlehem'" (2 Samuel 23:15). Three men broke through the enemy camp to bring it, "But he would not drink of it. He poured it out to the Lord and said, 'Far be it from me, O Lord, that I should do this. Shall I drink the blood of the men who went at the risk of their lives?'" (23:16–17). His longing was real, and he set it aside for what the water had cost others. 10.7 (Willing) follows the will.

The Greek word for willing is also used for wanting turned toward harm. "Those who desire to be rich fall into temptation, into a snare, into many senseless and harmful desires that plunge people into ruin and destruction" (1 Timothy 6:9). Against it Paul sets "godliness with contentment is great gain" (6:6). "Whoever wishes to be a friend of the world makes himself an enemy of God" (James 4:4).

Unmet wanting turns into fighting. "What causes quarrels and what causes fights among you? Is it not this, that your passions are at war within you? You desire and do not have, so you murder. You covet and cannot obtain, so you fight and quarrel. You do not have, because you do not ask" (James 4:1–2). The outer quarrel is the inner war carried outward. And what is wanted against God can be given in anger. Israel craved meat, and "while the food was still in their mouths, the anger of God rose against them" (Psalm 78:30–31). They asked for a king: "I gave you a king in my anger" (Hosea 13:11).

Wanting can end in death. James gives the order: "Then desire when it has conceived gives birth to sin, and sin when it is fully grown brings forth death" (James 1:15). "The desire of the sluggard kills him, for his hands refuse to labor" (Proverbs 21:25). Wanting is also measured against death. Of the arrogant man, "His greed is as wide as Sheol; like death he has never enough" (Habakkuk 2:5). And "love is strong as death, jealousy is fierce as the grave" (Song of Songs 8:6).

A want can be spoken in terms of death:

  • Rachel, envying her sister: "Give me children, or I shall die!" (Genesis 30:1)
  • Esau: "I am about to die; of what use is a birthright to me?" (Genesis 25:32)

Paul names what is to be put to death: "Put to death therefore what is earthly in you: sexual immorality, impurity, passion, evil desire, and covetousness, which is idolatry" (Colossians 3:5). Chapter 2 ("Death and sin") gives the account.

Wanting can reach past this life. "For in this tent we groan, longing to put on our heavenly dwelling … not that we would be unclothed, but that we would be further clothed, so that what is mortal may be swallowed up by life" (2 Corinthians 5:2, 4). "Our citizenship is in heaven, and from it we await a Savior" (Philippians 3:20). Chapter 2 ("The body raised") gives the account.

Wanting can be aimed at the living God. "My soul thirsts for God, for the living God. When shall I come and appear before God?" (Psalm 42:2). The thirst is answered with water: "let the one who is thirsty come; let the one who desires take the water of life without price" (Revelation 22:17). A desire met reaches the whole person: "a desire fulfilled is a tree of life" (Proverbs 13:12). And God himself satisfies it: "You open your hand; you satisfy the desire of every living thing" (Psalm 145:16).

Wanting can also be aimed at possessions, and Jesus says plainly that they are not life: "for one's life does not consist in the abundance of his possessions" (Luke 12:15). Gehazi swore by the Lord's life on his way to get what his master had refused: "As the Lord lives, I will run after him and get something from him" (2 Kings 5:20). Chapter 2 ("Life weighed", "Living before God") gives the account.

Connections: with feeling (10.1), since unmet wanting becomes anger; into thinking (10.4) and choosing (10.7), where desire, delight and envy stand beside the choice; with the soul (Chapter 5) and the flesh (Chapter 7); with life and death (Chapter 2), since desire conceived brings forth death.
