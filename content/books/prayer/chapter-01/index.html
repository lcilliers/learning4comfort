The Practice of Prayer

1. What prayer is

Every reference work gathered for this study begins by saying what prayer is, and each puts it a little differently. Taken together, their definitions show the size of the subject.

Talking with God

At its simplest, prayer is "the addressing and petitioning of God", as the Baker Encyclopedia puts it. It is speaking to God and asking him for things. The encyclopedia gives a classic Christian definition from the Westminster Shorter Catechism: prayer is "an offering up of our desires unto God, for things agreeable to his will, in the name of Christ, with confession of our sins, and thankful acknowledgement of his mercies."

The other works widen the picture. Charles Hodge calls prayer "the converse of the soul with God". In it, he says, we express to God "our reverence, and love for his divine perfection, our gratitude for all his mercies, our penitence for our sins, our hope in his forgiving love, our submission to his authority, our confidence in his care, our desires for his favour". The Lexham Survey of Theology describes prayer as "direct conversation and communion with God, whether vocal or silent, planned or spontaneous, individual or corporate". Like any conversation, it may take many forms: desire or appeal, sorrow or apology, love or admiration, gratitude, frustration, hopes and joys, fears and doubts, questions. The Holman dictionary defines prayer in one word: dialogue, "between God and people, especially His covenant partners".

That last word matters. Prayer is not a speech made into silence. God speaks as well as listens. He called Abraham, and Abraham answered; the people cried out in Egypt, and God heard (Exodus 2:23–24). In the end, the Holman article says, "Dialogue is what is essential to prayer."

What prayer is not

The Lexham Survey is just as clear about what prayer is not.

  • It is not magic. Magic tries to manipulate or appease spiritual forces to get a result. Prayer asks a person, who is free to answer as he wills.
  • It is not only thinking. Quiet meditation on Scripture can be prayer, but only when it turns into conversation with God. Prayer is addressed to someone.
  • It is not made to anyone but God. In Scripture prayer is never addressed to angels or to people, and never made to God through an angel or a human go-between. Paul states the reason: "there is one God, and there is one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus" (1 Timothy 2:5).

The prophets mocked the alternative. The idol-maker cuts down a tree, burns half of it to cook his meal, and from the rest makes a god, and "he prays to it and says, 'Deliver me, for you are my god!'" (Isaiah 44:17). Those who carry their idols about "keep on praying to a god that cannot save" (Isaiah 45:20). Hodge draws the same line: prayer ascribes to the one addressed the power to hear and to answer, so it belongs to God alone.

What prayer takes for granted

Hodge points out that "prayer takes a great deal for granted". To pray at all is to believe certain things about God.

  • That God is a person. "Only a person can say I, or be addressed as Thou." If God were only a name for a force, or for the moral order of the universe, prayer would make no sense. The God of Scripture hears and sees: "He who planted the ear, does he not hear? He who formed the eye, does he not see?" (Psalm 94:9).
  • That God is near. A person might still be far away and take no interest in us. Prayer assumes that God is near, willing to hear, and knows our thoughts, even the ones we do not put into words. "Even before a word is on my tongue, behold, O Lord, you know it altogether" (Psalm 139:4). Paul told the Athenians that God "is actually not far from each one of us, for 'In him we live and move and have our being'" (Acts 17:27–28).
  • That God rules all things. Prayer assumes that God governs nature and history, and the hearts of people too. When rain falls, it is because he sends it: "he prepares rain for the earth; he makes grass grow on the hills" (Psalm 147:8). "The king's heart is a stream of water in the hand of the Lord; he turns it wherever he will" (Proverbs 21:1). If God had no control over the world, there would be nothing for prayer to accomplish.
  • That God loves. The God who hears prayer "is not mere intelligence and power", Hodge writes. "He is love." "As a father shows compassion to his children, so the Lord shows compassion to those who fear him" (Psalm 103:13). Hodge meets the objection that God is too great to care about small things by saying that it forgets his greatness. A God who is truly infinite is not distracted by many cares, and for him the difference between small and great disappears. "Not one of them will fall to the ground apart from your Father ... you are of more value than many sparrows" (Matthew 10:29, 31).

God's invitation, not our search

The Baker Encyclopedia adds something more. The ground on which anyone comes to God in prayer "is never simply 'man's search for God' but God's gracious initiative". God made a covenant with his people and promised them help and deliverance, and that covenant "gives the warrant for prayer". People pray because God has first spoken, invited and promised.

That is why prayer in Scripture is both bold and humble. Abraham argues with God over Sodom, and in the same breath says, "I have undertaken to speak to the Lord, I who am but dust and ashes" (Genesis 18:27). The encyclopedia notes the same blend in the Lord's Prayer: directness in "Our Father", and distance in "in heaven" and "hallowed be your name" (Matthew 6:9). Boldness in prayer is not to be confused with familiarity. Biblical prayer is shot through with a sense of the distance between Creator and creature, of human sin, and of God's grace.

To whom prayer is made

In the Old Testament, prayers are addressed to God as the one God. Jesus taught his disciples to pray to the Father (Matthew 6:9) and to ask in his own name: "whatever you ask of the Father in my name, he will give it to you" (John 16:23).

The Lexham Survey notes that the New Testament goes further. Some prayers are addressed to the Father and the Son together: "Now may our God and Father himself, and our Lord Jesus, direct our way to you" (1 Thessalonians 3:11). Some are addressed to the Son directly. Hodge gives examples. The apostles asked him, "Increase our faith!" (Luke 17:5). The dying thief prayed, "Jesus, remember me when you come into your kingdom" (Luke 23:42). Stephen's last words were, "Lord Jesus, receive my spirit" (Acts 7:59). Paul pleaded with the Lord three times about his thorn (2 Corinthians 12:8), and gave thanks to "Christ Jesus our Lord" (1 Timothy 1:12). The Bible ends with prayer to him: "Come, Lord Jesus!" (Revelation 22:20). The New Testament has neither a command nor an example of prayer to the Holy Spirit, the Lexham Survey adds, though because he is fully God, many hymns down the centuries have been prayers to him.

Why pray?

The plainest reason to pray is that God commands it. The Baker Encyclopedia goes on: the deeper motives are the glory of God and the good of those who pray and of those they pray for. These do not compete, because God is glorified in blessing his people: they live "to the praise of his glorious grace" (Ephesians 1:6).

Prayer also keeps the truth of our dependence in view. Every good thing comes from God, and prayer says so. One reason prayer is so prominent in Scripture, the encyclopedia suggests, is that it "brings the recognition of this dependence into prominent focus".

Hodge adds that prayer is a means of grace, a way God has appointed to give life and holiness. It brings people near to God, "the source of all good". "When the soul thus draws near to God, God draws near to it." James says the same: "Draw near to God, and he will draw near to you" (James 4:8). Hodge does not soften the other side: "A prayerless man is of necessity, and thoroughly irreligious." A soul that never goes out toward God, and lives as if there were no God, is spiritually dead.

So prayer is more than one religious activity among others. It is the breath of a life lived with God. The psalmist gives the simplest reason of all for going on: "I love the Lord, because he has heard my voice and my pleas for mercy. Because he inclined his ear to me, therefore I will call on him as long as I live" (Psalm 116:1–2).
