7. Reminding God of his promises

One of the most striking ways God's people pray in Scripture is by reminding God of what he has said. They quote his promises back to him, recall what he has done before, and appeal to his name. This chapter looks at that way of praying, and at the question it raises: if God has already promised, why ask, and can prayer change what he does?

Not because he forgets

The Logos research assistant's answer, which began this part of the study, put it simply: believers remind God of his word "not because he might forget, but because his promises provide the foundation for confident intercession". God does not need reminding. But the people praying need a ground to stand on, and his promise is that ground.

The commentaries gathered for this study agree. One calls it "an excellent model of prayer", in which believers "prayed God's promises back to him". Another notes that "Bible writers frequently recounted God's historic blessings in prayer" and "repeated his promises back to him". God even invites it. Through Isaiah he says, "Put me in remembrance; let us argue together; set forth your case" (Isaiah 43:26). He set watchmen on Jerusalem's walls with this task: "You who put the Lord in remembrance, take no rest, and give him no rest until he establishes Jerusalem and makes it a praise in the earth" (Isaiah 62:6–7).

Moses pleads the name

The Baker Encyclopedia says that Moses repeatedly "pleads the name of the Lord" in the face of the uncertainties of the wilderness and his people's unbelief. This, it says, "is not to be thought of as an incantation, but as a reminder to God of who he has revealed himself to be". The key is God's revelation of himself at the burning bush. There God made promises to his people, "and in prayer Moses holds God to these promises".

His prayer after the golden calf shows how (Exodus 32:11–13). He gives God three reasons to spare the people, and none of them is that the people deserve it.

  • They are God's own people, and his own work. "O Lord, why does your wrath burn hot against your people, whom you have brought out of the land of Egypt with great power and with a mighty hand?" (Exodus 32:11).
  • God's name is at stake among the nations. "Why should the Egyptians say, 'With evil intent did he bring them out, to kill them in the mountains'?" (Exodus 32:12).
  • God has sworn an oath. "Remember Abraham, Isaac, and Israel, your servants, to whom you swore by your own self, and said to them, 'I will multiply your offspring as the stars of heaven, and all this land that I have promised I will give to your offspring'" (Exodus 32:13).

"And the Lord relented from the disaster that he had spoken of bringing on his people" (Exodus 32:14). Moses prayed the same way in Deuteronomy's account: "Remember your servants, Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. Do not regard the stubbornness of this people" (Deuteronomy 9:27).

When the people refused to enter the land, Moses went further. He quoted back to God the words God had used to describe himself on the mountain (Exodus 34:6–7): "please let the power of the Lord be great as you have promised, saying, 'The Lord is slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love, forgiving iniquity and transgression'" (Numbers 14:17–18). Then he asked, "Please pardon the iniquity of this people, according to the greatness of your steadfast love" (Numbers 14:19). God answered, "I have pardoned, according to your word" (Numbers 14:20).

"But you said"

Jacob, afraid of Esau, prayed the same way. He began, "O Lord who said to me, 'Return to your country and to your kindred, that I may do you good'" (Genesis 32:9). He asked for deliverance, and ended, "But you said, 'I will surely do you good, and make your offspring as the sand of the sea'" (Genesis 32:12). His fear was real, and so was the promise; he held the one up against the other.

David's prayer after the promise of a lasting house is built entirely on God's word. He asks God to "confirm forever the word that you have spoken concerning your servant and concerning his house, and do as you have spoken" (2 Samuel 7:25). He says the promise itself gave him the boldness to pray: "you, O Lord of hosts, the God of Israel, have made this revelation to your servant, saying, 'I will build you a house.' Therefore your servant has found courage to pray this prayer to you" (2 Samuel 7:27). And he rests on God's faithfulness: "you are God, and your words are true, and you have promised this good thing to your servant" (2 Samuel 7:28). Here the promise does not make prayer unnecessary. It is what makes prayer possible.

Solomon at the temple prayed for the same promise: "keep for your servant David my father what you have promised him", and "let your word be confirmed, which you have spoken to your servant David my father" (1 Kings 8:25–26). He could already give thanks that "Not one word has failed of all his good promise, which he spoke by Moses his servant" (1 Kings 8:56).

Remember

The psalms use one word for this way of praying again and again: remember. "Remember your mercy, O Lord, and your steadfast love, for they have been from of old. Remember not the sins of my youth or my transgressions; according to your steadfast love remember me, for the sake of your goodness, O Lord!" (Psalm 25:6–7). The psalmist asks God to remember what is good in God himself, his mercy and steadfast love, and to forget what is bad in the one praying, his sins.

Under the weight of a broken kingdom, another psalmist asks, "Lord, where is your steadfast love of old, which by your faithfulness you swore to David?" (Psalm 89:49). The longest psalm turns the promise into hope: "Remember your word to your servant, in which you have made me hope" (Psalm 119:49), and "I entreat your favor with all my heart; be gracious to me according to your promise" (Psalm 119:58). The psalmist even lies awake over the promises: "My eyes are awake before the watches of the night, that I may meditate on your promise" (Psalm 119:148).

The psalms also show that God does remember. "He remembers his covenant forever, the word that he commanded, for a thousand generations" (Psalm 105:8). In Egypt, "God heard their groaning, and God remembered his covenant with Abraham, with Isaac, and with Jacob" (Exodus 2:24). Zechariah praised God at the birth of John for the same thing, "to remember his holy covenant, the oath that he swore to our father Abraham" (Luke 1:72–73).

Prophets and exiles

Jeremiah, interceding in a time of drought and judgment, appealed to God's name and covenant: "Though our iniquities testify against us, act, O Lord, for your name's sake" (Jeremiah 14:7), and "Do not spurn us, for your name's sake; do not dishonor your glorious throne; remember and do not break your covenant with us" (Jeremiah 14:21).

Nehemiah, hearing of Jerusalem's ruin, reminded God of both the warning and the promise he had given through Moses: "Remember the word that you commanded your servant Moses, saying, 'If you are unfaithful, I will scatter you among the peoples, but if you return to me and keep my commandments and do them, though your outcasts are in the uttermost parts of heaven, from there I will gather them'" (Nehemiah 1:8–9). The scattering had happened. Nehemiah held God to the gathering.

Daniel's great prayer began with reading Scripture. He "perceived in the books the number of years that, according to the word of the Lord to Jeremiah the prophet, must pass before the end of the desolations of Jerusalem, namely, seventy years" (Daniel 9:2). Jeremiah had written: "When seventy years are completed for Babylon, I will visit you, and I will fulfill to you my promise and bring you back to this place" (Jeremiah 29:10). Daniel did not sit back and wait for the promise to come true. He "turned my face to the Lord God, seeking him by prayer and pleas for mercy" (Daniel 9:3). The promise moved him to pray.

God appoints prayer as a means

Daniel's response points to the answer the reference works give to an old objection. If God has already decided what will happen, why pray?

The Baker Encyclopedia answers that God is not one who "has set a mechanical universe in motion", but one who knows the end from the beginning and "works all things according to the counsel of his will" (Ephesians 1:11). To reach his purposes, he uses human actions, including prayer. "He has ordained the ends and the petitionary prayers of believers as one of the means to those ends." It gives Ezekiel as an example. God promised his people a new heart and a restored land (Ezekiel 36:26), and in the same breath said, "This also I will let the house of Israel ask me to do for them" (Ezekiel 36:37). He promised the gift, and wanted to be asked for it. The encyclopedia quotes the hymn writer Joseph Hart:

Prayer was appointed to convey / The blessings God designs to give.

Hodge makes the same point by comparison with eating. If it would be unreasonable to say, "If it be foreordained that I should live, it is not necessary for me to eat," it is no less unreasonable to say, "If it be foreordained that I should receive any good, it is not necessary for me to ask for it." He concludes, "If God has foreordained to bless us, He has foreordained that we should seek his blessing." The encyclopedia adds that the objection, if it worked against prayer, would work against every human action: walking, talking, working. If God's plan made prayer pointless, it would make everything pointless.

Does God change his mind?

But Scripture sometimes says that God "relented" from what he had said he would do: after Moses' prayer (Exodus 32:14), at the threshing floor of Araunah (2 Samuel 24:16), for Hezekiah (Jeremiah 26:19), and for Nineveh (Jonah 3:10). Does prayer change God's will?

The Baker Encyclopedia takes Hezekiah as its test case. Isaiah told him, "Set your house in order, for you shall die; you shall not recover" (2 Kings 20:1). Hezekiah prayed and wept, and God said, "I have heard your prayer; I have seen your tears. Behold, I will heal you" (2 Kings 20:5). Did God intend Hezekiah to recover or not?

The encyclopedia's answer is that in Scripture the warrant for any prayer is God's promise. When God says "Hezekiah will not recover", a condition such as "unless prayer is made for his recovery" is to be understood. What God intended was Hezekiah's recovery upon prayer being made, knowing that it would be made. The warning was meant to call out the very prayer God had ordained as the means of healing him. God himself states the principle through Jeremiah: if a nation he has threatened "turns from its evil, I will relent of the disaster that I intended to do to it" (Jeremiah 18:8). Nineveh's king reasoned the same way: "Who knows? God may turn and relent" (Jonah 3:9).

Why would God go to such lengths? The encyclopedia gives two reasons. Prayer teaches God's children their entire dependence on him for every good thing; and it keeps them, day by day and moment by moment, dependent on him. The alternative, it argues, is worse: a God who has constantly to adjust his will to his creatures' wishes, and who does not know what will happen until they ask.

The Holman dictionary puts the emphasis differently, and the two belong together. Our understanding of prayer, it says, will match our understanding of God. When God is seen as desiring to bless (James 1:5) and as sovereignly free to respond to people (Jonah 3:9), prayer will be seen as real dialogue. "Prayer makes a difference in what happens." James says so bluntly: "You do not have, because you do not ask" (James 4:2).

Prayer and the laws of nature

The Baker Encyclopedia and Hodge both face a second objection: if nature runs by fixed laws, how can God answer a prayer for rain or for healing? Their answers agree. On a biblical view, the laws of nature are descriptions of the regular way God governs his world, not a power that rules him. God ordains both the regularities and, if he chooses, the exceptions. Hodge compares it to the way people produce effects by combining natural forces without breaking any law; God does on the scale of the universe what we do in our small sphere. He concludes that it is not irrational to pray "for rain or fair weather, for prosperous voyages or healthful seasons". The encyclopedia adds a caution that matters more than any theory: "the duty to pray does not depend in any way on being able finally to solve all the difficulties associated with prayer."

Hodge ends his treatment with the power of prayer in history. Moses' prayer saved Israel; at Samuel's prayer the Philistines were scattered; and James writes that "Elijah was a man with a nature like ours, and he prayed fervently that it might not rain, and for three years and six months it did not rain on the earth. Then he prayed again, and heaven gave rain" (James 5:17–18). Mary, Queen of Scots, Hodge recalls, said she feared the prayers of John Knox more than an army.

The promises of the New Testament

The New Testament gives promises for prayer as plain as any in the Old. Smith and Lee, in Handfuls on Purpose, describe them in three words.

  • Sure. "Truly, truly, I say to you, whatever you ask of the Father in my name, he will give it to you" (John 16:23). They say: think of the one who promises, who made heaven and earth, who has all power, "the God who cannot lie". Hebrews says God confirmed his promise with an oath, "so that by two unchangeable things, in which it is impossible for God to lie, we ... might have strong encouragement" (Hebrews 6:18).
  • Simple. "Ask, and it will be given to you; seek, and you will find; knock, and it will be opened to you" (Matthew 7:7). Any child could understand it.
  • Sufficient. "How much more will the heavenly Father give the Holy Spirit to those who ask him!" (Luke 11:13). "Whatever you ask" (John 16:23) is, they say, "a wide door and effectual". And "all the promises of God find their Yes in him" (2 Corinthians 1:20).

The Christian who reminds God of his promises does what Moses, David and Daniel did, but on firmer ground. The promises have their "Yes" in Christ, and "he who promised is faithful" (Hebrews 10:23). The next chapter turns to the hardest part of the subject: the times when what was asked was not given.
