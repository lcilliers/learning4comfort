The Practice of Prayer

The Baker Encyclopedia calls Christian prayer "the end product of a long process of change and development in God's relation to men". Prayer has a history in Scripture. It begins with a family calling on God's name and ends with a church praying in the name of Jesus, by the Spirit, before the throne of grace. This chapter follows that history in outline.

Calling on the name of the Lord

The first man and woman, made for fellowship with God, walked with him. Sin broke that direct relationship. The first mention of prayer after it comes in the days of Seth's son: "At that time people began to call upon the name of the Lord" (Genesis 4:26). The same words are used of Abraham, who at Bethel "built an altar to the Lord and called upon the name of the Lord" (Genesis 12:8), and later of Isaac (Genesis 26:25).

With Abraham, recorded prayers begin. When God made his covenant with him, the relationship was direct. Abraham could speak his disappointment: "O Lord God, what will you give me, for I continue childless?" (Genesis 15:2). He could plead for his son: "Oh that Ishmael might live before you!" (Genesis 17:18). He argued for Sodom (Genesis 18), and he prayed for Abimelech, and "God healed Abimelech" (Genesis 20:17).

Genesis records the prayers of the household too. Abraham's servant, at the well, asked for a sign: "O Lord, God of my master Abraham, please grant me success today" (Genesis 24:12). "Isaac prayed to the Lord for his wife, because she was barren. And the Lord granted his prayer" (Genesis 25:21). Rebekah, troubled in her pregnancy, "went to inquire of the Lord" (Genesis 25:22). Jacob made a vow at Bethel (Genesis 28:20–22), prayed in fear before meeting Esau (Genesis 32:9–12), and wrestled through the night at Peniel, saying, "I will not let you go unless you bless me" (Genesis 32:26).

The Baker Encyclopedia notes that in the time of the patriarchs prayer went together with sacrifice and obedience. Altars and prayers belong together in Genesis.

Moses, the intercessor

At the exodus, Israel became a nation, and the Holman dictionary calls it "a nation born of prayer". The people cried out under their slavery, and "God heard their groaning, and God remembered his covenant" (Exodus 2:24). The Lord told Moses, "I have surely seen the affliction of my people who are in Egypt and have heard their cry" (Exodus 3:7).

Moses spoke with God at length at the burning bush (Exodus 3:1–4:17), raising objection after objection. He became the people's leader, and also their appointed intercessor. Again and again, in the uncertainties of the wilderness and in the face of the people's unbelief, he pleaded with God for them. When they made the golden calf, "Moses implored the Lord his God" (Exodus 32:11), and "the Lord relented from the disaster that he had spoken of bringing on his people" (Exodus 32:14). When they refused to enter the land, he prayed again, "Please pardon the iniquity of this people, according to the greatness of your steadfast love", and the Lord answered, "I have pardoned, according to your word" (Numbers 14:19–20).

Moses also prayed for himself, and honestly. Worn down by the people's complaints, he said, "I am not able to carry all this people alone; the burden is too heavy for me" (Numbers 11:14). Chapter 7 returns to the way he prayed: he reminded God of who he had revealed himself to be and what he had promised.

He prayed even for the enemy. During the plagues Pharaoh asked him again and again, "Plead with the Lord to take away the frogs from me and from my people" (Exodus 8:8), and "Plead for me" (Exodus 8:28). Each time, Moses "went out from Pharaoh and prayed to the Lord", and "the Lord did as Moses asked" (Exodus 8:30–31), though Pharaoh broke his word.

Moses was not the only intercessor. The Baker Encyclopedia names Aaron, Samuel, Solomon and Hezekiah among those who prayed for the people. Samuel told Israel, "far be it from me that I should sin against the Lord by ceasing to pray for you" (1 Samuel 12:23).

Prayer and the place of worship

Once the priesthood and the tabernacle were established, and later the temple, the Baker Encyclopedia observes, worship seems to be marked by distance. The instructions for worship say very little about the people's own prayers. One exception is the words the worshipper was to say when bringing the firstfruits: "Then we cried to the Lord, the God of our fathers, and the Lord heard our voice and saw our affliction" (Deuteronomy 26:7).

The psalms show that sacrifice and prayer went together: "Offer to God a sacrifice of thanksgiving, and perform your vows to the Most High, and call upon me in the day of trouble; I will deliver you, and you shall glorify me" (Psalm 50:14–15). They also show something the instructions for worship do not: people bringing their own perplexities to God. Psalm 73 is an example. The psalmist cannot understand why the wicked prosper, "until I went into the sanctuary of God; then I discerned their end" (Psalm 73:17). His "arguing with God", as the encyclopedia calls it, ends in trust: "Whom have I in heaven but you? And there is nothing on earth that I desire besides you" (Psalm 73:25).

Leaders and kings

The Holman dictionary traces prayer through Israel's leaders. By prayer Joshua discovered the sin that had brought defeat at Ai (Joshua 7:6–9). But he was tricked by the Gibeonites when he did not seek God's mind: "the men took some of their provisions, but did not ask counsel from the Lord" (Joshua 9:14). In the days of the judges, God raised up deliverers when the people called out to him (Judges 3:9).

David's spiritual depth is seen in his prayers of confession: "I have sinned against the Lord" (2 Samuel 12:13), and Psalm 51. Solomon asked for wisdom at the start of his reign (1 Kings 3:5–9) and dedicated the temple in a long prayer (1 Kings 8). God worked wonders through the prayers of Elijah and Elisha: a dead boy raised (1 Kings 17:19–22), and fire from heaven on Carmel (1 Kings 18:36–38). Hezekiah spread the enemy's letter before the Lord (2 Kings 19:14), and prayed on his sickbed (2 Kings 20:2–3). Even Manasseh, the worst of Judah's kings, prayed when he was taken captive to Babylon: "when he was in distress, he entreated the favor of the Lord his God and humbled himself greatly before the God of his fathers. He prayed to him, and God was moved by his entreaty and heard his plea" (2 Chronicles 33:12–13).

The prophets

The prophets were people who prayed, and the Baker Encyclopedia notes that God's word seems to have come to them in prayer. Isaiah's call came with a cry of confession, "Woe is me! For I am lost; for I am a man of unclean lips" (Isaiah 6:5), and ended with a prayer of offering, "Here I am! Send me" (Isaiah 6:8). The Holman article says Isaiah's call shows the intense cleansing and commitment that prayer involves.

The prophets also warned that real prayer must go with right living. God complained through Hosea, "They do not cry to me from the heart, but they wail upon their beds" (Hosea 7:14). Amos mocked worship that went on with injustice: "Come to Bethel, and transgress" (Amos 4:4).

Jeremiah's prayers are the most open of all. Some are settled and trusting (Jeremiah 10:23–25). Others are full of conflict: "O Lord, you have deceived me, and I was deceived; you are stronger than I, and you have prevailed" (Jeremiah 20:7). Even his complaint begins with praise: "Righteous are you, O Lord, when I complain to you; yet I would plead my case before you. Why does the way of the wicked prosper?" (Jeremiah 12:1). The Holman article says Jeremiah teaches honesty in prayer.

Exile and return

When the exile cut the people off from the temple, prayer took on new importance. With the synagogue, praying together became part of Jewish worship, and daily patterned prayer kept faith alive far from Jerusalem. Daniel "got down on his knees three times a day and prayed and gave thanks before his God, as he had done previously" (Daniel 6:10).

After the exile, the Baker Encyclopedia notes, there is an emphasis on spontaneous prayer, and on devotion that is more than routine. Nehemiah, asked a question by the king, "prayed to the God of heaven" before he answered (Nehemiah 2:4). Facing enemies, "we prayed to our God and set a guard as a protection against them day and night" (Nehemiah 4:9). The great prayers of confession from this time, by Daniel, Ezra and the Levites (Daniel 9; Ezra 9; Nehemiah 9), are read in Chapter 6.

Jesus at prayer

The New Testament's teaching on prayer is dominated by Jesus' own example. The Gospels show him praying at the crucial moments of his ministry: at his baptism, "when Jesus also had been baptized and was praying, the heavens were opened" (Luke 3:21); before choosing the twelve, when "all night he continued in prayer to God" (Luke 6:12); and at the transfiguration, "as he was praying, the appearance of his face was altered" (Luke 9:29). He prayed early: "rising very early in the morning, while it was still dark, he departed and went out to a desolate place, and there he prayed" (Mark 1:35). He prayed alone: "he went up on the mountain by himself to pray" (Matthew 14:23). "He would withdraw to desolate places and pray" (Luke 5:16).

He sometimes prayed aloud for the sake of those listening. At Lazarus' tomb he said, "Father, I thank you that you have heard me. I knew that you always hear me, but I said this on account of the people standing around" (John 11:41–42). The night before he died, he prayed for his disciples and for all who would believe through them (John 17). In Gethsemane he prayed three times that the cup might pass (Matthew 26:39–44), and on the cross he prayed for those who crucified him (Luke 23:34) and committed his spirit to his Father (Luke 23:46).

The Baker Encyclopedia points out that Jesus spoke of his relationship with the Father as unique: "I am ascending to my Father and your Father, to my God and your God" (John 20:17). He did not say "our Father" of himself and his disciples together. Yet he brought them into that relationship, and taught them to say "Our Father". It was after watching him pray that a disciple asked, "Lord, teach us to pray" (Luke 11:1).

The early church

Jesus told his disciples that a day was coming when they would pray to the Father in his name (John 16:23–26). After the Spirit came, the church was marked by prayer. Even before Pentecost, "all these with one accord were devoting themselves to prayer" (Acts 1:14). After it, "they devoted themselves to the apostles' teaching and the fellowship, to the breaking of bread and the prayers" (Acts 2:42). The apostles set their priorities: "we will devote ourselves to prayer and to the ministry of the word" (Acts 6:4).

The Holman dictionary lists the occasions. The church prayed when choosing leaders (Acts 1:24; 6:6; 13:3); under persecution, when "they lifted their voices together to God" (Acts 4:24), and when Peter was in prison and "earnest prayer for him was made to God by the church" (Acts 12:5); and before healing (Acts 9:40; 28:8). Calling on the name of the Lord, which began in Genesis, became the first mark of a believer: "everyone who calls upon the name of the Lord shall be saved" (Acts 2:21).

Paul and the Spirit of adoption

The Baker Encyclopedia says that the theology of prayer is most fully developed in Paul's letters. The believer is a son, not only a servant. The Spirit given to the church is the Spirit of adoption, who enables believers to come to God as their Father with all their needs: "you have received the Spirit of adoption as sons, by whom we cry, 'Abba! Father!'" (Romans 8:15; see also Galatians 4:6). The Spirit also helps where prayer is weak: "we do not know what to pray for as we ought, but the Spirit himself intercedes for us with groanings too deep for words" (Romans 8:26).

Paul's own example is as instructive as his teaching. He prayed constantly for the churches, with thanksgiving (Ephesians 1:16), and asked them to pray for him (Ephesians 6:18–19). His prayer for the Ephesians shows what he cared about most: that they would be "strengthened with power through his Spirit in your inner being, so that Christ may dwell in your hearts through faith" and would "know the love of Christ that surpasses knowledge" (Ephesians 3:16–17, 19).

Through the great high priest

The Baker Encyclopedia sums up: the Christian's prayer is rooted objectively in Christ's intercession, and subjectively in the help of the Holy Spirit. The letter to the Hebrews develops the first. The priests of the Old Testament could no more truly intercede for the people than "the blood of bulls and goats" could take away sins (Hebrews 10:4). Christ is the great high priest, who sympathises with human weakness, and who "always lives to make intercession" for those who draw near to God through him (Hebrews 7:25). So the church is told to come boldly: "Let us then with confidence draw near to the throne of grace, that we may receive mercy and find grace to help in time of need" (Hebrews 4:16).

The whole church is now a priesthood, offering "spiritual sacrifices acceptable to God through Jesus Christ" (1 Peter 2:5), and "a sacrifice of praise to God, that is, the fruit of lips that acknowledge his name" (Hebrews 13:15). The long history that began with an altar and a name ends here: every believer may come, at any time, into God's presence, through Christ, by the Spirit.
