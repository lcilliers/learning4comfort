Some prayers in Scripture stand out. They are prayed at turning points, by people under great pressure, and they have been read ever since as patterns. The Lexham Survey lists some of the most important of the Old Testament by period: from the time of Moses, the prayers of Moses and Joshua; from the time of the kings, those of Hannah, Samuel, Jabez, Solomon, Asa, Hezekiah and Habakkuk; from after the exile, those of Daniel, Ezra and Nehemiah. To them the New Testament adds the prayers of Jesus and of the early church. This chapter reads a selection.

Abraham pleads for Sodom

When the Lord told Abraham that he was about to judge Sodom, "Abraham drew near and said, 'Will you indeed sweep away the righteous with the wicked?'" (Genesis 18:23). The Baker Encyclopedia calls this prayer "a striking combination of boldness and persistence, and a recognition of his own smallness and inferiority compared to God".

The boldness is plain. Abraham appeals to God's own justice: "Far be it from you to do such a thing, to put the righteous to death with the wicked ... Shall not the Judge of all the earth do what is just?" (Genesis 18:25). He bargains, from fifty to forty-five, to forty, thirty, twenty and ten. But each step is taken humbly: "Behold, I have undertaken to speak to the Lord, I who am but dust and ashes" (Genesis 18:27), and "Oh let not the Lord be angry, and I will speak again but this once" (Genesis 18:32). And each time God agrees. The prayer shows that God welcomes being pleaded with on the ground of his own character.

Jacob wrestles

Jacob's prayer before meeting Esau is a model of its own (Genesis 32:9–12). He addresses God by his covenant with his fathers, admits, "I am not worthy of the least of all the deeds of steadfast love and all the faithfulness that you have shown to your servant", asks plainly, "Please deliver me from the hand of my brother", and ends by quoting God's promise back to him (Genesis 32:10–12).

That night "a man wrestled with him until the breaking of the day" (Genesis 32:24). Jacob held on: "I will not let you go unless you bless me" (Genesis 32:26). He was given a new name, Israel, "for you have striven with God and with men, and have prevailed" (Genesis 32:28). The Baker Encyclopedia sets Peniel beside Abraham's plea as another example of bold, persistent prayer that still knows its place. Jacob limped away, but blessed.

Moses on the mountain

After the golden calf, Moses' prayer saved the people (Exodus 32:11–14; Chapter 7 looks at how he argued). But the prayer did not stop there. Moses went on to ask for more than pardon. He asked to know God: "please show me now your ways, that I may know you" (Exodus 33:13). He refused to go on without God's presence: "If your presence will not go with me, do not bring us up from here" (Exodus 33:15). God agreed: "This very thing that you have spoken I will do, for you have found favor in my sight, and I know you by name" (Exodus 33:17). Then Moses made the boldest request of all: "Please show me your glory" (Exodus 33:18).

Hannah

Hannah's prayer begins in bitterness. "She was deeply distressed and prayed to the Lord and wept bitterly" (1 Samuel 1:10). She made a vow: if God would give her a son, she would give him back to the Lord (1 Samuel 1:11). She prayed silently, misjudged by the priest, "pouring out my soul before the Lord" (1 Samuel 1:15). When the son came, she said, "For this child I prayed, and the Lord has granted me my petition that I made to him" (1 Samuel 1:27).

Smith and Lee draw from her story that "the Lord is interested in all that concerns His children". What was a reproach to Hannah, her childlessness, the Lord took away. Her second prayer, after she had given Samuel to the Lord, is a song of praise: "There is none holy like the Lord: for there is none besides you; there is no rock like our God" (1 Samuel 2:2).

Solomon dedicates the temple

Solomon's prayer at the dedication of the temple (1 Kings 8:22–53) is one of the longest prayers in Scripture. He stood before the altar with his hands spread out toward heaven, and by the end he was on his knees (1 Kings 8:22, 54).

He begins with praise and with God's promise to David (1 Kings 8:23–26), and then faces the great question: "But will God indeed dwell on the earth? Behold, heaven and the highest heaven cannot contain you; how much less this house that I have built!" (1 Kings 8:27). He does not ask God to live in the temple. He asks God to hear the prayers made toward it: "listen in heaven your dwelling place, and when you hear, forgive" (1 Kings 8:30).

Then he lists the occasions when people will pray: when someone is wronged by a neighbour; when Israel is defeated in battle because of sin; when there is no rain; in famine, plague and siege; when a foreigner comes to pray; when the army goes out to war; and when the people are carried captive to a foreign land (1 Kings 8:31–50). For each he asks the same thing: "then hear in heaven". The prayer covers "whatever prayer, whatever plea is made by any man or by all your people Israel, each knowing the affliction of his own heart and stretching out his hands toward this house" (1 Kings 8:38). It reaches beyond Israel, to the foreigner, "in order that all the peoples of the earth may know your name and fear you" (1 Kings 8:43). And it reaches into the future, to captives far from home who "repent with all their heart and with all their soul in the land of their enemies" (1 Kings 8:48). That is the prayer Daniel later prayed, facing Jerusalem (Daniel 6:10).

God answered: "I have heard your prayer and your plea, which you have made before me" (1 Kings 9:3). The Chronicler records the promise that went with it: "if my people who are called by my name humble themselves, and pray and seek my face and turn from their wicked ways, then I will hear from heaven and will forgive their sin and heal their land" (2 Chronicles 7:14).

Kings in a crisis

Two kings of Judah prayed short prayers when vast armies came against them. Asa prayed, "O Lord, there is none like you to help, between the mighty and the weak. Help us, O Lord our God, for we rely on you" (2 Chronicles 14:11). Jehoshaphat began with God's power, "You rule over all the kingdoms of the nations" (2 Chronicles 20:6), and ended with his own helplessness: "We do not know what to do, but our eyes are on you" (2 Chronicles 20:12).

Hezekiah prayed twice in crisis. When the Assyrian king sent a letter mocking God, Hezekiah "went up to the house of the Lord and spread it before the Lord" (2 Kings 19:14). His prayer moves from who God is, "you are the God, you alone, of all the kingdoms of the earth" (2 Kings 19:15), to the plain facts, and then to God's honour: "save us, please, from his hand, that all the kingdoms of the earth may know that you, O Lord, are God alone" (2 Kings 19:19). Later, told he would die, he turned his face to the wall, prayed, and wept, and God said, "I have heard your prayer; I have seen your tears" (2 Kings 20:5). Chapter 7 returns to this prayer and what it shows about God's purposes.

Prayers of confession after the exile

Three long prayers from after the exile have the same shape. Daniel (Daniel 9:4–19), Ezra (Ezra 9:6–15) and the Levites in Nehemiah's day (Nehemiah 9:5–38) all confess the sins of the nation, including their own, over its whole history, and all justify God in what has happened. "Yet you have been righteous in all that has come upon us, for you have dealt faithfully and we have acted wickedly" (Nehemiah 9:33). "O Lord, the God of Israel, you are just" (Ezra 9:15).

Each rests its plea on God's character and not on the people's merit. Nehemiah 9 recalls that God is "a God ready to forgive, gracious and merciful, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love" (Nehemiah 9:17). Daniel says it most clearly: "we do not present our pleas before you because of our righteousness, but because of your great mercy. O Lord, hear; O Lord, forgive. O Lord, pay attention and act. Delay not, for your own sake, O my God" (Daniel 9:18–19). And Daniel's prayer was answered before he finished. Gabriel came "while I was speaking in prayer", and told him, "At the beginning of your pleas for mercy a word went out" (Daniel 9:21, 23).

From the depths

Some special prayers come from the worst places. Jonah prayed "from the belly of the fish": "When my life was fainting away, I remembered the Lord, and my prayer came to you, into your holy temple" (Jonah 2:1, 7), and ended, "Salvation belongs to the Lord!" (Jonah 2:9). Habakkuk began his book with complaint, and ended it with a prayer set to music that trusts God when everything fails: "Though the fig tree should not blossom ... yet I will rejoice in the Lord; I will take joy in the God of my salvation" (Habakkuk 3:17–18).

The Lord's Prayer

The best-known prayer of all was given in answer to a request: "Lord, teach us to pray" (Luke 11:1). It comes in two forms, in the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 6:9–13) and in Luke (Luke 11:2–4). Matthew's reads:

Our Father in heaven, hallowed be your name. Your kingdom come, your will be done, on earth as it is in heaven. Give us this day our daily bread, and forgive us our debts, as we also have forgiven our debtors. And lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from evil. (Matthew 6:9–13)

The Baker Encyclopedia notes the same blend of nearness and distance found throughout biblical prayer: directness in "Our Father", distance in "in heaven" and "hallowed be your name". It notes the order of the requests: first God, his name, his kingdom and his will; then the disciples' needs, for daily provision, for forgiveness and for deliverance. The Holman dictionary adds that it was taught to disciples who knew that the kingdom had come in Jesus, but not yet in its fullness. So they pray "Your kingdom come" because it is still coming.

Both dictionaries say that Jesus was teaching how to pray, not only what to pray. The prayer is a pattern: put God first, ask for what is needed today, keep short accounts of sin and forgiveness, and depend on God against evil. It was given as a contrast to the prayer of hypocrites who pray to be seen (Matthew 6:5), and of those who "heap up empty phrases" thinking "they will be heard for their many words" (Matthew 6:7).

Jesus prays for his own

John records the prayer Jesus prayed the night before he died (John 17), often called his high-priestly prayer. The Baker Encyclopedia sees it as the height of a life of dependence on the Father. Jesus prays first for himself: "Father, the hour has come; glorify your Son that the Son may glorify you" (John 17:1). Then he prays for his disciples: "I am praying for them ... Holy Father, keep them in your name ... that they may be one, even as we are one" (John 17:9, 11). He does not ask that they be taken out of the world, "but that you keep them from the evil one" (John 17:15), and "Sanctify them in the truth; your word is truth" (John 17:17). Then he prays for the church to come: "I do not ask for these only, but also for those who will believe in me through their word, that they may all be one" (John 17:20–21). He ends with a desire: "Father, I desire that they also, whom you have given me, may be with me where I am, to see my glory" (John 17:24). The Holman dictionary notes that this prayer, like his prayer at Lazarus' tomb, shows his unity with the Father and his desire to give him glory.

Gethsemane and the cross

In Gethsemane Jesus prayed, "My Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass from me; nevertheless, not as I will, but as you will" (Matthew 26:39). He prayed it three times (Matthew 26:44). Mark gives his words in Aramaic: "Abba, Father, all things are possible for you. Remove this cup from me. Yet not what I will, but what you will" (Mark 14:36). Luke adds that "being in agony he prayed more earnestly" (Luke 22:44). The Holman dictionary sees two things here that both matter: the boldness of asking for the Father's will to be changed, and the submission to the hard path of suffering. Chapter 8 returns to this prayer, the greatest of the prayers not granted.

On the cross, he prayed for his enemies, "Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do" (Luke 23:34), and committed himself to his Father: "Father, into your hands I commit my spirit!" (Luke 23:46).

The church under threat

When Peter and John were released and told to stop speaking in Jesus' name, the believers prayed together (Acts 4:24–30). They began with God as creator, "Sovereign Lord, who made the heaven and the earth and the sea and everything in them" (Acts 4:24); read their situation in the light of Psalm 2; and then, instead of asking for safety, asked for courage: "grant to your servants to continue to speak your word with all boldness" (Acts 4:29). "And when they had prayed, the place in which they were gathered together was shaken, and they were all filled with the Holy Spirit and continued to speak the word of God with boldness" (Acts 4:31).

Paul's prayers for the churches

Paul's letters contain prayers that have been prayed ever since. For the Ephesians he asks for understanding, that God would give them "the Spirit of wisdom and of revelation in the knowledge of him, having the eyes of your hearts enlightened" (Ephesians 1:17–18). His second prayer for them, which the Baker Encyclopedia singles out, asks for inner strength, for Christ to dwell in their hearts, and for them "to know the love of Christ that surpasses knowledge, that you may be filled with all the fullness of God" (Ephesians 3:19). It ends in praise "to him who is able to do far more abundantly than all that we ask or think" (Ephesians 3:20). The encyclopedia notes what Paul prays for most: deeper faith in Christ, love for God, and a growing grasp of God's love in return.
