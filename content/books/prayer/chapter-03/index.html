The Practice of Prayer

3. Forms of prayer

In this chapter

  3. Short prayers in the moment

  5. Prayer in the family
  6. Prayer together
  7. Public prayer

Prayer can be sorted in two ways. One is by what is said: praise, thanks, confession, asking. Those are the kinds of prayer, and Chapter 5 looks at them. The other is by the form and setting of the prayer: whether it is a lifelong habit or a set time, a cry in a moment of need or a planned prayer, said alone, in a family or in the gathered church, in free words or in set words. This chapter looks at those forms.

Praying always

Hodge begins with the widest form of prayer. Prayer is converse with God, so it includes every movement of the soul toward him in thought and feeling: reverence, gratitude, sorrow for sin, dependence. In this sense, "the man who lives and walks with God, prays always. He fulfils to the letter the injunction 'Pray without ceasing.'" Paul's words are short: "Rejoice always, pray without ceasing, give thanks in all circumstances" (1 Thessalonians 5:16–18). Hodge gives a picture for it: "The heart should be like the altar of incense, on which the fire never went out."

The psalmist describes the same habit of the heart: "I will bless the Lord at all times; his praise shall continually be in my mouth" (Psalm 34:1). Chapter 10 returns to what it means to pray at all times.

Stated seasons of prayer

Yet Hodge warns that a constant inward communion with God is not enough by itself. "The believer needs, in order to maintain his spiritual health and vigour, regular and stated seasons of prayer, as the body needs its daily meals." It is a law of human nature that thoughts and feelings are clothed in words, and there is power in words actually spoken: a thought or feeling becomes clearer and more vivid, even to the person praying, when it is put into words. So prayer is also speech, set apart in time.

Daniel's three times a day (Daniel 6:10), the psalmist's "evening and morning and at noon" (Psalm 55:17), and Peter and John "going up to the temple at the hour of prayer, the ninth hour" (Acts 3:1) are examples of fixed times. Hodge's counsel is simple: "Let each day, at least, begin and end with God."

Short prayers in the moment

At the other extreme is what older writers called "ejaculatory" prayer. The Cyclopædia of M'Clintock and Strong describes it as short sentences springing spontaneously from the mind and sent up to God in any emergency. The word comes from the Latin for a dart, a prayer thrown upward.

Scripture is full of them. Nehemiah, standing before the king with a question to answer, "prayed to the God of heaven" between the question and his reply (Nehemiah 2:4). Peter, sinking in the sea, "cried out, 'Lord, save me'" (Matthew 14:30). The disciples in the storm woke Jesus: "Save us, Lord; we are perishing" (Matthew 8:25). A blind man by the road "cried out, 'Jesus, Son of David, have mercy on me!'" (Luke 18:38). The tax collector's whole prayer was, "God, be merciful to me, a sinner!" (Luke 18:13). None of these is long, and none was planned. Each is real prayer.

Secret prayer

The Cyclopædia calls the next form secret or "closet" prayer, practised in private as Christ taught: "when you pray, go into your room and shut the door and pray to your Father who is in secret. And your Father who sees in secret will reward you" (Matthew 6:6). The old word "closet" meant a small private room. The Holman dictionary puts it in modern terms: a storage closet or a shed will do.

Jesus is not forbidding prayer with others here. He is warning against prayer meant to impress others, the prayer of those who "love to stand and pray in the synagogues and at the street corners, that they may be seen by others" (Matthew 6:5). The Holman article notes that the warning applies as much to someone praying to impress a congregation as to anyone on a street corner.

Jesus practised what he taught. He went "up on the mountain by himself to pray" (Matthew 14:23), and "would withdraw to desolate places and pray" (Luke 5:16). Hodge draws the lesson: "If the spotless soul of Jesus needed these seasons of converse with God, none of his followers should venture to neglect this important means of grace." The prophets did the same. When Elisha came to the dead child, "he went in and shut the door behind the two of them and prayed to the Lord" (2 Kings 4:33). Hannah's prayer at Shiloh was secret even in a public place: "Hannah was speaking in her heart; only her lips moved, and her voice was not heard" (1 Samuel 1:13).

Prayer in the family

Hodge divides prayer into secret, social and public, and under social prayer he puts the family first. Because people are made for fellowship, he argues, no one can live a religious life for themselves alone, and the family, "the most intimate bond of fellowship among men", should be hallowed by prayer. He urges that the household gather "statedly ... morning and evening, for the worship of God". A man's responsibility to his children, as well as to God, "binds him to make his house a Bethel", a house of God.

Scripture gives glimpses of such prayer. Job rose early to offer sacrifices for each of his children, saying, "It may be that my children have sinned" (Job 1:5). Cornelius was "a devout man who feared God with all his household ... and prayed continually to God" (Acts 10:2). Isaac prayed for his wife (Genesis 25:21), and Manoah asked God to teach him and his wife "what we are to do with the child who will be born" (Judges 13:8). Hodge's view is plain: if religion dies out in the family, it cannot be kept alive anywhere else.

Prayer together

Prayer with others outside the family has a promise of its own: "if two of you agree on earth about anything they ask, it will be done for them by my Father in heaven. For where two or three are gathered in my name, there am I among them" (Matthew 18:19–20). The early church met in homes to pray. When Peter was freed from prison, he went to the house of Mary, the mother of Mark, "where many were gathered together and were praying" (Acts 12:12). When the apostles were threatened, the believers "lifted their voices together to God" (Acts 4:24). In Philippi, Paul looked for "a place of prayer" by the river, where women met (Acts 16:13).

Public prayer

Public prayer belongs to the gathered worship of God's people. Solomon "stood before the altar of the Lord in the presence of all the assembly of Israel and spread out his hands toward heaven" to pray (1 Kings 8:22). God called the temple "a house of prayer for all peoples" (Isaiah 56:7), and Jesus drove out the traders with the same words: "It is written, 'My house shall be called a house of prayer,' but you make it a den of robbers" (Matthew 21:13). Paul told Timothy that "supplications, prayers, intercessions, and thanksgivings be made for all people" (1 Timothy 2:1), and that "in every place the men should pray, lifting holy hands without anger or quarreling" (1 Timothy 2:8). The people's part was to say "Amen". "Let all the people say, 'Amen!'" (Psalm 106:48). Paul insists that public prayer must be understood, or how can anyone "say 'Amen' to your thanksgiving when he does not know what you are saying?" (1 Corinthians 14:16).

The Baker Encyclopedia argues that worship cannot be scriptural without prayer, because the heart of worship is God being addressed as "Thou". More than that, the shape the gospel gives to worship needs prayer at every point. It moves from sin, to grace, to faith: first the recognition of sin and need, in prayers of confession; then the awareness of God's provision of forgiveness and new life in Christ; and then the response of faith, in praise for pardon, thanks for God's gift, and intercession for others. Without prayer, the encyclopedia says, worship is shapeless.

Hodge, writing as a minister, admits that public prayer is often the weak point of a service: "there are more good preachers than good prayers", because ministers prepare their sermons all week and give no thought to their prayers. He lists what public prayer needs:

  • A devout spirit. The feelings and desires the prayer expresses should be alive in the one who leads it.
  • A mind stored with Scripture. No prayers are more fitting than those that use the language of Scripture well, because the Spirit who inspired it is the one who awakens prayer.
  • Good order. The prayer should cover its proper parts in due proportion, so that it does not ramble or repeat itself.
  • Fitness for the occasion. A Lord's day service, a sacrament, a day of thanksgiving and a day of fasting call for different prayers.
  • Simple language. Its words should be "simple, solemn, and correct".
  • Brevity. "The prayers should be short." Length is usually due to repetition. The Preacher said the same long before: "God is in heaven and you are on earth. Therefore let your words be few" (Ecclesiastes 5:2).

Set words or free prayer

Should prayer use fixed words, or be in the words of the moment? The Baker Encyclopedia notes that Christians and churches have divided over this. Some argue that fixed forms make worship dead, inflexible and unable to respond to sudden needs, and "quench the Spirit". Others answer that fixed forms make worship truly shared, and guard against clumsy or odd leadership.

The encyclopedia says the question should not be settled by convenience or custom, but by Scripture. The Old Testament has strict liturgical patterns. The New Testament has none: every recorded prayer is spoken freely, with the possible exception of the Lord's Prayer, and even there Jesus may have been giving a pattern for prayer rather than a set liturgy. The Holman dictionary agrees: "Although it is permissible to repeat this prayer, it may be well to remember Jesus was emphasizing how to pray, not what to pray."

The dispute became bitter when fixed forms were imposed by law. In 1662, the encyclopedia recalls, hundreds of English ministers were ejected from their churches for refusing to accept the legally required Book of Common Prayer. Many of them did not object to set prayers as such, only to being compelled to use them. That event, with the earlier migration of Puritan congregations to America, shaped how prayer is ordered in public worship to this day.

The forms differ, but Scripture uses them all. A cry of three words, a psalm sung by a congregation, a prayer prepared with care, a habit of the heart through the day: each is a way of speaking to the same God.
