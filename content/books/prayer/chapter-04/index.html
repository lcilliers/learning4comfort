The Practice of Prayer

4. How prayer is expressed

In this chapter

  4. Tears
  5. Wailing and lament

The word studies gathered for this study make one point above all: Scripture describes prayer through many different physical and vocal expressions, and each says something about the state of the one who prays. Prayer can be shouted or whispered, wept or sung, spoken standing or lying face down. It can also be wordless. This chapter looks at those expressions, and at the warning that goes with them.

The lifted voice

Hebrew has a phrase for raising the voice: nāśāʾ qôl, "to lift up the voice". Gesenius' lexicon notes that it is used before words for weeping, for crying out and for rejoicing. Esau "lifted up his voice and wept" (Genesis 27:38). Hagar, sitting apart from her dying son, "lifted up her voice and wept", and "God heard the voice of the boy" (Genesis 21:16–17). When the angel of the Lord rebuked Israel at Bochim, "the people lifted up their voices and wept" (Judges 2:4). David and his men at Ziklag "raised their voices and wept until they had no more strength to weep" (1 Samuel 30:4). The same lifting of the voice is used for joy: "They lift up their voices, they sing for joy; over the majesty of the Lord they shout from the west" (Isaiah 24:14).

The phrase is also used of prayer itself. Hezekiah sent word to Isaiah, "therefore lift up your prayer for the remnant that is left" (Isaiah 37:4; 2 Kings 19:4). Prayer, like a song or a lament, is something taken up and lifted with the voice. The lexicon points out the opposite too: of the Lord's servant it is said, "He will not cry aloud or lift up his voice, or make it heard in the street" (Isaiah 42:2), meaning that he will not shout. The voice in prayer runs along a scale, from quiet to emphatic.

The psalms use the whole scale. "I cry aloud to God, aloud to God, and he will hear me" (Psalm 77:1). "With my voice I cry out to the Lord; with my voice I plead for mercy to the Lord" (Psalm 142:1). The early church "lifted their voices together to God" (Acts 4:24). Stephen, dying, "cried out with a loud voice, 'Lord, do not hold this sin against them'" (Acts 7:60). Hebrews says that even Jesus "offered up prayers and supplications, with loud cries and tears" (Hebrews 5:7).

Crying out

Many of the prayers in the verses gathered for this study are simply cries. "Hear my cry, O God, listen to my prayer" (Psalm 61:1). "Out of the depths I cry to you, O Lord!" (Psalm 130:1). "This poor man cried, and the Lord heard him and saved him out of all his troubles" (Psalm 34:6). A cry is prayer at its most basic: a voice raised to God in need, without polish. Again and again, the cry is heard. "Then they cried to the Lord in their trouble, and he delivered them from their distress" (Psalm 107:6). In a battle, the tribes east of the Jordan "cried out to God in the battle, and he granted their urgent plea because they trusted in him" (1 Chronicles 5:20).

Without words

Not every prayer is spoken. Hannah prayed silently: "only her lips moved, and her voice was not heard" (1 Samuel 1:13). Eli thought she was drunk; she was "pouring out my soul before the Lord" (1 Samuel 1:15). The psalmist knows that even what he cannot say is heard: "O Lord, all my longing is before you; my sighing is not hidden from you" (Psalm 38:9). Some prayer is waiting in silence: "For God alone my soul waits in silence; from him comes my salvation" (Psalm 62:1).

Paul says that when believers do not know what to pray, the Spirit himself prays for them "with groanings too deep for words" (Romans 8:26). Hezekiah, describing his illness, puts it in the sounds of birds: "Like a swallow or a crane I chirp; I moan like a dove. My eyes are weary with looking upward" (Isaiah 38:14). Hodge notes that unspoken longings are intelligible to God, who "knows our thoughts afar off".

Tears

The vocabulary of weeping is rich. Vincent's Word Studies sets out three Greek words that come together in one saying of Jesus: "you will weep and lament ... You will be sorrowful" (John 16:20).

  • To be sorrowful (lypeō) is the most general. It covers "every species of pain, of body or of soul", and need not show outwardly at all.
  • To weep (klaiō) is audible weeping, the crying of children. Jesus wept aloud like this over Jerusalem: "when he drew near and saw the city, he wept over it" (Luke 19:41).
  • To lament (thrēneō) is the most formal: to sing a dirge over the dead. The children in Jesus' parable complain, "we sang a dirge, and you did not mourn" (Matthew 11:17), and the women following Jesus to the cross were "mourning and lamenting for him" (Luke 23:27).

There is a fourth word, dakryō, "to shed tears", to weep silently. Vincent notes that it occurs only once in the New Testament, of Jesus at the tomb of Lazarus: "Jesus wept" (John 11:35). Both kinds of weeping, the loud and the silent, are found in Jesus himself.

Tears can be prayer. Albert Barnes, commenting on "the Lord has heard the sound of my weeping" (Psalm 6:8), writes: "Weeping, if unmurmuring, is of the nature of prayer, for God regards the sorrows of the soul as he sees them." God looks with compassion on "the weeping penitent, the weeping sufferer ... even though the sorrows of the soul do not find words to give utterance to them." Barnes links this to Job, whose "eye pours out tears to God" (Job 16:20), and to the Spirit's wordless groaning (Romans 8:26).

The psalms are full of tears offered to God. "I am weary with my moaning; every night I flood my bed with tears" (Psalm 6:6). "Hear my prayer, O Lord, and give ear to my cry; hold not your peace at my tears!" (Psalm 39:12). "You have kept count of my tossings; put my tears in your bottle. Are they not in your book?" (Psalm 56:8). No tear is lost. And tears are answered. To Hezekiah, weeping on his sickbed, God said, "I have heard your prayer; I have seen your tears" (2 Kings 20:5). Hannah "prayed to the Lord and wept bitterly" (1 Samuel 1:10). Nehemiah "sat down and wept and mourned for days, and I continued fasting and praying before the God of heaven" (Nehemiah 1:4).

Wailing and lament

Hebrew has a word for wailing, yĕlālâ, a cry of sorrow "often accompanied by weeping or crying", according to the Lexham Analytical Lexicon. In the prophets it is the wail that goes up when judgment falls: "the cry of the shepherds, and the wail of the lords of the flock" (Jeremiah 25:36). It is the sound of grief with no hope in it.

Lament in Scripture can be addressed to God, and then it becomes prayer. Jerusalem in ruins is told, "Arise, cry out in the night, at the beginning of the night watches! Pour out your heart like water before the presence of the Lord! Lift your hands to him for the lives of your children" (Lamentations 2:19). The priests are told to "weep and say, 'Spare your people, O Lord'" (Joel 2:17). The whole book of Lamentations is grief turned toward God. The difference between the wail of despair and the lament of faith is not the depth of sorrow, but the one to whom it is spoken.

Song

Gesenius' lexicon adds that a song is "lifted up" with the voice just as a prayer is. Song is not mere accompaniment to prayer; it can be prayer. Many psalms were sung prayers, and the second book of Psalms closes, "The prayers of David, the son of Jesse, are ended" (Psalm 72:20). Habakkuk's prayer is headed "A prayer of Habakkuk the prophet, according to Shigionoth" (Habakkuk 3:1), and ends with a direction for the musicians: "To the choirmaster: with stringed instruments" (Habakkuk 3:19). Hannah's song begins, "And Hannah prayed and said, 'My heart exults in the Lord'" (1 Samuel 2:1).

The psalmist sings at night: "at night his song is with me, a prayer to the God of my life" (Psalm 42:8). Paul and Silas in prison "about midnight ... were praying and singing hymns to God" (Acts 16:25). James joins the two: "Is anyone among you suffering? Let him pray. Is anyone cheerful? Let him sing praise" (James 5:13). Paul tells the churches to sing "psalms and hymns and spiritual songs ... making melody to the Lord with your heart" (Ephesians 5:19), and to pray and sing with understanding: "I will pray with my spirit, but I will pray with my mind also; I will sing praise with my spirit, but I will sing with my mind also" (1 Corinthians 14:15). The last psalm calls for every instrument, "trumpet ... lute and harp ... tambourine and dance", and ends, "Let everything that has breath praise the Lord!" (Psalm 150:3–4, 6).

The body in prayer

Scripture also shows prayer expressed through the body. No one posture is commanded, but many are described.

  • Standing. Solomon "stood before the altar of the Lord ... and spread out his hands toward heaven" (1 Kings 8:22). Jesus assumed it: "whenever you stand praying, forgive" (Mark 11:25). In the parable, both the Pharisee and the tax collector stood (Luke 18:11, 13).
  • Kneeling. By the end of his prayer Solomon was on his knees, "where he had knelt with hands outstretched toward heaven" (1 Kings 8:54). Daniel "got down on his knees three times a day" (Daniel 6:10). In Gethsemane, Jesus "knelt down and prayed" (Luke 22:41). Paul "knelt down and prayed with them all" (Acts 20:36), and wrote, "I bow my knees before the Father" (Ephesians 3:14). "Oh come, let us worship and bow down; let us kneel before the Lord, our Maker!" (Psalm 95:6).
  • Face to the ground. Joshua "fell to the earth on his face before the ark of the Lord until the evening" (Joshua 7:6). Moses "lay prostrate before the Lord for these forty days and forty nights" (Deuteronomy 9:25). In Gethsemane, Jesus "fell on his face and prayed" (Matthew 26:39). Elijah on Carmel "bowed himself down on the earth and put his face between his knees" (1 Kings 18:42).
  • Head bowed. The psalmist, praying for his enemies when they were sick, says, "I prayed with head bowed on my chest" (Psalm 35:13). At the reading of the law, the people "bowed their heads and worshiped the Lord with their faces to the ground" (Nehemiah 8:6).
  • Sitting. After the promise of a house, "King David went in and sat before the Lord" (2 Samuel 7:18).
  • Hands lifted or spread out. "Hear the voice of my pleas for mercy ... when I lift up my hands toward your most holy sanctuary" (Psalm 28:2). "Let my prayer be counted as incense before you, and the lifting up of my hands as the evening sacrifice!" (Psalm 141:2). Ezra "fell upon my knees and spread out my hands to the Lord my God" (Ezra 9:5). Paul wanted men everywhere to pray, "lifting holy hands" (1 Timothy 2:8).
  • Eyes lifted, or lowered. Jesus "lifted up his eyes to heaven" to pray (John 17:1). The tax collector "would not even lift up his eyes to heaven, but beat his breast" (Luke 18:13).
  • Turned away from all else. Told that he would die, Hezekiah "turned his face to the wall and prayed to the Lord" (2 Kings 20:2).

Some prayer was expressed by what was done to the body. People tore their clothes, put on sackcloth and fasted. Daniel sought God "by prayer and pleas for mercy with fasting and sackcloth and ashes" (Daniel 9:3). Ezra and the returning exiles "fasted and implored our God for this, and he listened to our entreaty" (Ezra 8:23). And sometimes the body brought the problem itself before God: Hezekiah took the enemy's letter "and spread it before the Lord" (2 Kings 19:14).

Heart and hands together

The word studies end with a description of prayer's range: from inward anguish that may never show, to public outcry. It reflects "prayer's capacity to engage the whole person across varying contexts and intensities".

But the whole person must mean the heart as well as the body. The prophets were blunt about outward signs with nothing behind them. "They do not cry to me from the heart, but they wail upon their beds" (Hosea 7:14). "When you spread out your hands, I will hide my eyes from you; even though you make many prayers, I will not listen; your hands are full of blood" (Isaiah 1:15). Joel calls for weeping and fasting, and then adds, "rend your hearts and not your garments" (Joel 2:13). Lamentations puts the two together in one line: "Let us lift up our hearts and hands to God in heaven" (Lamentations 3:41).

The voice, the tears, the knees and the hands are all ways the heart speaks. God hears all of them, and he sees whether the heart is in them.
