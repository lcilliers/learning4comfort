The Practice of Prayer

5. Kinds of prayer

The Baker Encyclopedia observes that prayer is, typically, an unselfconscious activity: the person praying is taken up with God, not with the type of prayer being offered. Even so, it is possible to tell its elements apart. This chapter sets out the kinds of prayer that Scripture shows.

Two ways of sorting

The most traditional way, given in M'Clintock and Strong's Cyclopædia, sorts prayer by its purpose into five parts: adoration, which expresses God's goodness and greatness; confession, which acknowledges unworthiness; supplication, which asks for pardon or blessing; intercession, which prays for others; and thanksgiving, which expresses gratitude.

The Lexham Survey of Theology groups prayer into three broad kinds of conversation, each with its own parts:

  • Prayers of request: supplication, which comes from a sense of need, whether for oneself (petition) or for others (intercession); confession, which comes from a sense of having offended; and, on rare occasions, imprecation, a request that God deal justly with the wicked.
  • Prayers of worship: adoration, which is praise and admiration; and thanksgiving, which is gratitude.
  • Prayers of communion: fellowship, which is conversing with God on any subject; and grievance, which is telling God one's frustrations, doubts and struggles, called "complaint" in Scripture.

The second scheme adds two kinds the first leaves out: the hard prayers against the wicked, and the honest prayer of complaint. Both are common in Scripture, and both are taken up below.

Adoration and praise

Praise, the Baker Encyclopedia says, recognises who God is and what he does. It is "giving God the glory", not in the sense of adding to his glory, which is impossible, but of willingly, and where fitting publicly, recognising God as God. The encyclopedia points to Psalms 148 and 150 as typical. "Praise the Lord! Praise the Lord from the heavens; praise him in the heights! Praise him, all his angels; praise him, all his hosts!" (Psalm 148:1–2). All creation is called to join: sun, moon and stars, "for he commanded and they were created" (Psalm 148:5).

Praise is often the first thing in a prayer. David blessed God before the assembly: "Yours, O Lord, is the greatness and the power and the glory and the victory and the majesty, for all that is in the heavens and in the earth is yours" (1 Chronicles 29:11). The Levites began their great prayer of confession by praising God: "You are the Lord, you alone. You have made heaven, the heaven of heavens, with all their host" (Nehemiah 9:6). The worship of heaven is adoration: "Worthy are you, our Lord and God, to receive glory and honor and power, for you created all things" (Revelation 4:11).

Thanksgiving

When praise is for what God has done for the one praying, or for others, the encyclopedia calls it thanksgiving. It lists its subjects: life itself, the use and beauty of the physical world, Christ and all his benefits, and specific answers to prayer. Paul's thanks for the greatest gift is one line: "Thanks be to God for his inexpressible gift!" (2 Corinthians 9:15).

Thanks for answered prayer runs through Scripture. When God revealed the king's dream, Daniel prayed, "To you, O God of my fathers, I give thanks and praise, for you have given me wisdom and might, and have now made known to me what we asked of you" (Daniel 2:23). The psalmist says, "I thank you that you have answered me and have become my salvation" (Psalm 118:21). Of ten lepers healed, one "turned back, praising God with a loud voice; and he fell on his face at Jesus' feet, giving him thanks" (Luke 17:15–16).

The New Testament joins thanksgiving to every other kind of prayer. Requests are to be made known "by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving" (Philippians 4:6). Believers are to give "thanks always and for everything to God the Father in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ" (Ephesians 5:20), and to "give thanks in all circumstances" (1 Thessalonians 5:18). Even ordinary food is received with thanks, for "it is made holy by the word of God and prayer" (1 Timothy 4:5).

Confession

Confession of sin, the Baker Encyclopedia says, recognises God's holiness and his supreme moral authority, together with the personal responsibility of the one confessing. It "involves the vindicating or justifying of God": agreeing that God is right. And it is an explicit, unreserved acknowledgement of sin, both in its roots, in sinful motives and dispositions, and in what it does outwardly.

The encyclopedia calls Psalm 51, David's confession after his sin with Bathsheba, "the classic biblical instance of a prayer of confession". It shows all these marks. David appeals to God's mercy: "Have mercy on me, O God, according to your steadfast love" (Psalm 51:1). He owns his sin without excuse: "For I know my transgressions, and my sin is ever before me" (Psalm 51:3). He justifies God: "Against you, you only, have I sinned and done what is evil in your sight, so that you may be justified in your words and blameless in your judgment" (Psalm 51:4). He asks for more than pardon: "Create in me a clean heart, O God, and renew a right spirit within me" (Psalm 51:10). And he knows what God wants: "a broken and contrite heart, O God, you will not despise" (Psalm 51:17).

Holding back from confession is costly, and making it brings relief. "For when I kept silent, my bones wasted away through my groaning all day long ... I said, 'I will confess my transgressions to the Lord,' and you forgave the iniquity of my sin" (Psalm 32:3, 5). The New Testament gives the promise: "If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness" (1 John 1:9).

The leaders of the exile confessed for the whole people, and included themselves. Nehemiah prayed, "confessing the sins of the people of Israel, which we have sinned against you. Even I and my father's house have sinned" (Nehemiah 1:6). Daniel "made confession, saying ... we have sinned and done wrong and acted wickedly and rebelled" (Daniel 9:4–5). Ezra said, "O my God, I am ashamed and blush to lift my face to you" (Ezra 9:6). And no one is beyond confession's reach. Manasseh had filled Jerusalem with idols, but in captivity he "humbled himself greatly before the God of his fathers", and "God was moved by his entreaty and heard his plea" (2 Chronicles 33:12–13).

Petition

Petition is asking for oneself. The Baker Encyclopedia is firm that "Scripture never regards prayer for oneself as sinful or ethically improper", as the Lord's Prayer itself shows: "Give us this day our daily bread" (Matthew 6:11).

The petitions of Scripture cover every need. Jabez prayed for blessing and protection: "Oh that you would bless me and enlarge my border, and that your hand might be with me, and that you would keep me from harm", and "God granted what he asked" (1 Chronicles 4:10). Hannah asked for a son (1 Samuel 1:11). Solomon asked for wisdom (1 Kings 3:9). The psalmist asks for healing, "heal me, O Lord, for my bones are troubled" (Psalm 6:2), and for guidance, "Teach me your way, O Lord, that I may walk in your truth" (Psalm 86:11). Paul's rule is that nothing is too small: "in everything by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known to God" (Philippians 4:6).

Intercession

Petition for others is intercession. "Prayer for others", the encyclopedia says, "is an obvious expression of love for one's neighbor which is fundamental to biblical ethics."

The great intercessors of the Old Testament are Abraham, Moses and Samuel (Chapters 2 and 6). Samuel counted it a sin to stop: "far be it from me that I should sin against the Lord by ceasing to pray for you" (1 Samuel 12:23). Job, after his ordeal, was told to pray for the friends who had wronged him: "my servant Job shall pray for you, for I will accept his prayer", and "the Lord restored the fortunes of Job, when he had prayed for his friends" (Job 42:8, 10).

Jesus interceded for his disciples. He told Peter, "I have prayed for you that your faith may not fail" (Luke 22:32), and prayed for all who would believe through the apostles' word (John 17:20). Paul's letters are full of intercession: "my heart's desire and prayer to God for them is that they may be saved" (Romans 10:1). He names Epaphras, "always struggling on your behalf in his prayers, that you may stand mature and fully assured in all the will of God" (Colossians 4:12). He asked for prayer himself, and believed it made a difference: "You also must help us by prayer, so that many will give thanks on our behalf for the blessing granted us through the prayers of many" (2 Corinthians 1:11).

Intercession reaches beyond friends. Paul urges prayer "for all people, for kings and all who are in high positions" (1 Timothy 2:1–2). Jesus commands, "Love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you" (Matthew 5:44). Moses prayed for Pharaoh (Exodus 8:30). A man of God prayed for the king who had just tried to seize him, and "the king's hand was restored to him" (1 Kings 13:6). The psalmist prayed for those who repaid him evil: "when they were sick — I wore sackcloth; I afflicted myself with fasting; I prayed with head bowed on my chest" (Psalm 35:13).

Prayers against the wicked

The Lexham Survey gives a special note to imprecatory prayer, a common and often troubling kind of prayer in the Psalms (it lists Psalms 12, 35, 52, 58, 59, 69, 70, 83, 109, 137 and 140). These are prayers that call down God's judgment. "O God, break the teeth in their mouths; tear out the fangs of the young lions, O Lord!" (Psalm 58:6). "Pour out your indignation upon them, and let your burning anger overtake them" (Psalm 69:24). The hardest of them ends the lament over Babylon (Psalm 137:8–9).

Many have objected that such prayers contradict the command to love one's enemies. The Lexham Survey points out that this command is in the Old Testament as well as the New: "If you see the donkey of one who hates you lying down under its burden ... you shall rescue it with him" (Exodus 23:5); "If your enemy is hungry, give him bread to eat" (Proverbs 25:21). Job says he has not let his mouth sin "by asking for his life with a curse" (Job 31:30). Some dismiss imprecations as exaggeration; others, such as C. S. Lewis, call them plainly sinful. The Lexham Survey asks that four things be weighed:

  • They are not only in the Psalms. Nehemiah prayed, "Do not cover their guilt" (Nehemiah 4:5), and Jeremiah, "Forgive not their iniquity, nor blot out their sin from your sight" (Jeremiah 18:23).
  • They are not only in the Old Testament. Paul writes, "If anyone has no love for the Lord, let him be accursed" (1 Corinthians 16:22), and says of a gospel-wrecker, "let him be accursed" (Galatians 1:8). Of Alexander he says, "the Lord will repay him according to his deeds" (2 Timothy 4:14). The martyrs in heaven cry, "how long before you will judge and avenge our blood?" (Revelation 6:10). So these prayers are not an Old Testament spirit opposed to a New Testament one.
  • Their language is often picture language. A prayer to "break the teeth" of the wicked may mean taking away their power to harm, "de-fanging" them.
  • They express a particular spirit. They show meekness, leaving judgment to God: this is notable in David, who had the power to punish his enemies himself. They show a godlike, holy hatred of evil; a desire for God's vindication, not personal revenge; and faith that God will surely judge those who persist in wickedness.

The psalmist puts it in one line: "my prayer is continually against their evil deeds" (Psalm 141:5). The prayer is aimed at the evil, and handed over to God.

Fellowship with God

The Lexham Survey's third group, prayers of communion, begins with fellowship: simply conversing with God on any subject. This is prayer as friendship. "The Lord used to speak to Moses face to face, as a man speaks to his friend" (Exodus 33:11). It need not ask for anything. "You have said, 'Seek my face.' My heart says to you, 'Your face, Lord, do I seek'" (Psalm 27:8). The Holman dictionary lists communion among the things the psalms seek (Psalm 63): "O God, you are my God; earnestly I seek you; my soul thirsts for you" (Psalm 63:1). Hodge says that in such fellowship "the soul must be holy and must be blessed".

Complaint

The second prayer of communion is grievance: telling God about frustrations, doubts and struggles. Scripture calls it "complaint", and it is not ashamed of it. "I pour out my complaint before him; I tell my trouble before him" (Psalm 142:2). Psalm 102 is headed, "A Prayer of one afflicted, when he is faint and pours out his complaint before the Lord" (Psalm 102:1). "Evening and morning and at noon I utter my complaint and moan, and he hears my voice" (Psalm 55:17).

The complaint can be sharp. "How long, O Lord? Will you forget me forever? How long will you hide your face from me?" (Psalm 13:1). "O Lord, how long shall I cry for help, and you will not hear?" (Habakkuk 1:2). Job longed to argue his case: "Oh, that I knew where I might find him ... I would lay my case before him and fill my mouth with arguments" (Job 23:3–4). Yet complaint addressed to God is an act of faith. It keeps talking to him. Psalm 13 begins with "How long?" and ends with "I will sing to the Lord, because he has dealt bountifully with me" (Psalm 13:6). The Holman dictionary sums up what the psalms teach: "variety and honesty in prayer are permissible".

All of them together

Hodge calls the Psalms a prayer book, in which every psalm is a prayer "of worship, of thanksgiving, of confession, or of supplication", and in which "all these elements are intermingled". The Holman dictionary picks out Psalm 86 as "an excellent pattern for prayer", and it shows why. In a few verses it asks for help, "Incline your ear, O Lord, and answer me, for I am poor and needy" (Psalm 86:1); grounds the request in who God is, "For you, O Lord, are good and forgiving" (Psalm 86:5); praises, "There is none like you among the gods, O Lord" (Psalm 86:8); asks to be taught, "Teach me your way, O Lord ... unite my heart to fear your name" (Psalm 86:11); and gives thanks, "I give thanks to you, O Lord my God, with my whole heart" (Psalm 86:12). The kinds of prayer can be told apart, but in real prayer they flow into one another.
