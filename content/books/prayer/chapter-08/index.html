The Practice of Prayer

8. When the answer does not come

In this chapter

  6. Praying on

"Not every petition is granted," the Holman dictionary says plainly. Anyone who prays knows it. The Baker Encyclopedia states the question: "Why does God answer some prayers and not others?" This chapter looks first at what Scripture says hinders prayer, and then at the harder cases: faithful people whose prayers were not granted for reasons that had nothing to do with their sin. Those cases were the subject of the last questions put to the research assistant at the start of this study, and they are taken one by one.

Not a machine

The Baker Encyclopedia begins with a warning: "prayer is not to be thought of in mechanical terms." It is part of a personal relationship with God. So the reason God answers one prayer and not another "has nothing to do with volume or length of prayers in themselves". What matters is sincerity, and then the relationship between the one praying and the revealed will of God. "The only warrant for praying at all is that God commands it and desires it, and the only warrant for praying for some particular thing rather than for some other thing is that God wills it, or may will it."

What hinders prayer

Scripture does name things that hinder prayer. The research assistant gathered them; Smith and Lee, in Handfuls on Purpose, list them as hindrances "to the answers", not to praying itself.

  • Sin held on to. "If I had cherished iniquity in my heart, the Lord would not have listened" (Psalm 66:18). "Behold, the Lord's hand is not shortened, that it cannot save, or his ear dull, that it cannot hear; but your iniquities have made a separation between you and your God, and your sins have hidden his face from you so that he does not hear" (Isaiah 59:1–2). Smith and Lee call it wilfulness: being conscious of sin and unwilling to confess it. "First get reconciled to God about this matter, then bring your petition."
  • Turning away from God's word. "If one turns away his ear from hearing the law, even his prayer is an abomination" (Proverbs 28:9). Those who "hated knowledge and did not choose the fear of the Lord" will call, and God "will not answer" (Proverbs 1:28–29).
  • Insincerity. It is possible, Smith and Lee say, "to keep up the form of prayer and to be asking great things from God, while the heart is condemning it all". God rejected prayers lifted by "hands ... full of blood" (Isaiah 1:15).
  • Selfish motives. "You ask and do not receive, because you ask wrongly, to spend it on your passions" (James 4:3). Smith and Lee: "We pray for success that we might be successful."
  • Doubt. "Let him ask in faith, with no doubting, for the one who doubts is like a wave of the sea that is driven and tossed by the wind. For that person must not suppose that he will receive anything from the Lord" (James 1:6–7).
  • Broken relationships. Husbands are to honour their wives, "so that your prayers may not be hindered" (1 Peter 3:7). "Whoever closes his ear to the cry of the poor will himself call out and not be answered" (Proverbs 21:13). Jesus says a worshipper who remembers a quarrel should "first be reconciled to your brother, and then come and offer your gift" (Matthew 5:24).
  • Unforgiveness. "Whenever you stand praying, forgive, if you have anything against anyone, so that your Father also who is in heaven may forgive you your trespasses" (Mark 11:25).
  • Impatience. "I waited patiently for the Lord; he inclined to me and heard my cry" (Psalm 40:1). Smith and Lee's advice is memorable: "Don't be a run-away knocker."

The other side is a promise: "if our heart does not condemn us, we have confidence before God; and whatever we ask we receive from him, because we keep his commandments and do what pleases him" (1 John 3:21–22). "The Lord is far from the wicked, but he hears the prayer of the righteous" (Proverbs 15:29).

When God forbids intercession

There is a stranger case. In Jeremiah's day God told the prophet not to pray for the people at all: "As for you, do not pray for this people, or lift up a cry or prayer for them, and do not intercede with me, for I will not hear you" (Jeremiah 7:16; also 11:14). He went further: "Though Moses and Samuel stood before me, yet my heart would not turn toward this people" (Jeremiah 15:1). Through Ezekiel he said the same: even if Noah, Daniel and Job were in the land, "they would deliver but their own lives by their righteousness" (Ezekiel 14:14).

The research assistant noted that these passages set a threshold, a point of judgment where intercession, however great the one praying, cannot turn God aside. Lamentations speaks from the far side of that line: "though I call and cry for help, he shuts out my prayer" (Lamentations 3:8), and "you have wrapped yourself with a cloud so that no prayer can pass through" (Lamentations 3:44). Yet even there, the same book remembers another day: "I called on your name, O Lord, from the depths of the pit; you heard my plea ... You came near when I called on you; you said, 'Do not fear!'" (Lamentations 3:55–57).

The harder cases

All of that is, as the question to the research assistant put it, the easy part. The hard part is the faithful person who prays rightly and is still refused. Scripture has many such cases.

Abraham asks for Ishmael

When God promised Abraham a son by Sarah, Abraham prayed for the son he already had: "Oh that Ishmael might live before you!" (Genesis 17:18). God's answer began with "No": "No, but Sarah your wife shall bear you a son, and you shall call his name Isaac. I will establish my covenant with him" (Genesis 17:19). But it did not end there: "As for Ishmael, I have heard you; behold, I have blessed him and will make him fruitful" (Genesis 17:20). The prayer was heard, and answered, but not in the way Abraham meant. God's plan ran through Isaac, and Ishmael was blessed in another way.

Moses asks to enter the land

Moses pleaded to cross the Jordan: "Please let me go over and see the good land beyond the Jordan" (Deuteronomy 3:25). God refused, and told him to stop asking: "Enough from you; do not speak to me of this matter again" (Deuteronomy 3:26). The reason lay in Moses' failure at Meribah, "Because you did not believe in me, to uphold me as holy in the eyes of the people of Israel" (Numbers 20:12). The research assistant noted that this denial came not from Moses' unworthiness as a man of prayer, but from God's judgment on that earlier act. The consequence stood.

Even so, the refusal had mercy in it. God took him up the mountain: "I have let you see it with your eyes, but you shall not go over there" (Deuteronomy 34:4). And Luke records that centuries later Moses appeared with Elijah on the mountain of the transfiguration, in the land he had longed to enter, speaking with Jesus about "his departure, which he was about to accomplish at Jerusalem" (Luke 9:31).

Moses offers himself

After the golden calf, Moses made another request: "if you will forgive their sin — but if not, please blot me out of your book that you have written" (Exodus 32:32). God would not accept the exchange: "Whoever has sinned against me, I will blot out of my book" (Exodus 32:33). He still sent Moses on, with his angel going before them (Exodus 32:34). Paul felt the same longing for his own people: "I could wish that I myself were accursed and cut off from Christ for the sake of my brothers" (Romans 9:3). No human being could be the substitute for others. That place belonged to someone else.

Samuel grieves over Saul

When God rejected Saul, "Samuel was angry, and he cried to the Lord all night" (1 Samuel 15:11). He went on grieving. God's answer was a question and a new task: "How long will you grieve over Saul, since I have rejected him from being king over Israel? Fill your horn with oil, and go" (1 Samuel 16:1). The prayer for Saul was not granted. Samuel was moved on to anoint David.

David's child

After David's sin with Bathsheba, the child she bore became ill. "David therefore sought God on behalf of the child. And David fasted and went in and lay all night on the ground" (2 Samuel 12:16). On the seventh day the child died. The Baker Encyclopedia gives this as one of its two examples of unanswered prayer.

David's prayer had been honest about its uncertainty: "Who knows whether the Lord will be gracious to me, that the child may live?" (2 Samuel 12:22). When the answer came, he accepted it: he "arose from the earth and washed and anointed himself and changed his clothes. And he went into the house of the Lord and worshiped" (2 Samuel 12:20). And he held to a hope beyond death: "I shall go to him, but he will not return to me" (2 Samuel 12:23).

Elijah asks to die

Exhausted after Carmel and threatened by Jezebel, Elijah "asked that he might die, saying, 'It is enough; now, O Lord, take away my life'" (1 Kings 19:4). God did not grant it. He gave him sleep, and an angel brought food twice: "Arise and eat, for the journey is too great for you" (1 Kings 19:7). Then he gave him work to do. Smith and Lee draw the lesson that "the Lord often gives far above what we ask": the prophet who asked to die never died at all, but "went up by a whirlwind into heaven" (2 Kings 2:11).

Jonah asks to die

Jonah's request came from a different place. Angry that God had spared Nineveh, he prayed, "please take my life from me, for it is better for me to die than to live" (Jonah 4:3). God answered with a question: "Do you do well to be angry?" (Jonah 4:4). He then taught Jonah, through a plant that grew and withered, about his own compassion: "And should not I pity Nineveh, that great city?" (Jonah 4:11). The prayer was refused because it was wrong, and the refusal was a lesson in God's mercy.

Job demands an answer

Job cried out, "I cry to you for help and you do not answer me; I stand, and you only look at me" (Job 30:20). He wanted a hearing: "Oh, that I knew where I might find him ... I would lay my case before him" (Job 23:3–4). When God finally answered "out of the whirlwind" (Job 38:1), he did not explain Job's suffering. He asked Job questions instead: "Where were you when I laid the foundation of the earth?" (Job 38:4). The Holman dictionary says that Job's demand for answers "was eclipsed by the awesome privilege of encountering Him". Job's own words were, "I had heard of you by the hearing of the ear, but now my eye sees you" (Job 42:5). It adds: "Modern believers must also cherish communion with the Father more than their petitions."

The silence of God

The psalmist cried, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me? ... O my God, I cry by day, but you do not answer, and by night, but I find no rest" (Psalm 22:1–2). Habakkuk complained, "O Lord, how long shall I cry for help, and you will not hear?" (Habakkuk 1:2). In both, the silence was not the end. The psalm turns to praise: "he has not hidden his face from him, but has heard, when he cried to him" (Psalm 22:24). Habakkuk took his stand on the watchtower, and God answered with a vision and a word about time: "If it seems slow, wait for it; it will surely come; it will not delay" (Habakkuk 2:3).

Gethsemane

The greatest of all the prayers not granted was Jesus' own. "My Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass from me; nevertheless, not as I will, but as you will" (Matthew 26:39). He prayed three times (Matthew 26:44). The cup did not pass. Instead, "there appeared to him an angel from heaven, strengthening him" (Luke 22:43).

The Holman dictionary sees two things here: "the boldness of the petition to alter God's will and the submission to this 'hard' path of suffering". Both are right. Hebrews adds something surprising: Jesus "offered up prayers and supplications, with loud cries and tears, to him who was able to save him from death, and he was heard because of his reverence" (Hebrews 5:7). He was heard, and yet he died. He was saved from death not by being spared it, but by being raised through it, and "being made perfect, he became the source of eternal salvation to all who obey him" (Hebrews 5:9).

Paul's thorn

Paul was given "a thorn ... in the flesh, a messenger of Satan to harass me, to keep me from becoming conceited" (2 Corinthians 12:7). "Three times I pleaded with the Lord about this, that it should leave me. But he said to me, 'My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in weakness'" (2 Corinthians 12:8–9).

The Holman dictionary notes the irony: Paul says God gave him the thorn, and yet calls it a messenger of Satan. The answer to his prayer was not removal but grace, and a lesson: petitions are sometimes refused in view of a greater good, here God's power shown in Paul's humility. Paul came to welcome it: "when I am weak, then I am strong" (2 Corinthians 12:10). Smith and Lee add that the fact he prayed three times shows "he had been taught to expect what he asked". The refusal did not stop him asking.

Requests Jesus refused

The Gospels record requests made to Jesus himself that he turned down. James and John asked, "Grant us to sit, one at your right hand and one at your left, in your glory", and he said, "You do not know what you are asking" (Mark 10:37–38). When a Samaritan village refused him, the same two asked, "Lord, do you want us to tell fire to come down from heaven and consume them?", "but he turned and rebuked them" (Luke 9:54–55). The man freed from demons begged "that he might be with him", but Jesus "did not permit him but said to him, 'Go home to your friends and tell them how much the Lord has done for you'" (Mark 5:18–19). The first two requests were refused because they were mistaken. The third was a good desire, refused because Jesus had a better task for him, and the man went and proclaimed it throughout the Decapolis (Mark 5:20).

The martyrs' "how long"

Even in heaven, there is a prayer waiting for its answer. The souls of the martyrs cry, "O Sovereign Lord, holy and true, how long before you will judge and avenge our blood?" (Revelation 6:10). They are given white robes "and told to rest a little longer" (Revelation 6:11). Their prayer is not refused. It is deferred to God's time.

What these cases teach

The reference works draw several lessons from such cases.

  • Some things God always gives, and some he may give. The Baker Encyclopedia distinguishes the things God "will unfailingly grant upon true prayer being made" from those "he may grant". The blessings of redemption, "forgiveness of sins, sanctification, and strength and wisdom for the fulfilling of duties", are always given to those who truly ask: "If any of you lacks wisdom, let him ask God, who gives generously to all without reproach, and it will be given him" (James 1:5). But health, wealth or a particular career, though good and lawful to ask for, "may nevertheless not be best, in the wisdom of God, for a particular individual at a particular time". God's promise is that "for those who love God all things work together for good" (Romans 8:28), not that every request will be granted. The research assistant summed it up: when a promise seems unfulfilled, the question is often which promise applies.
  • The guide is Scripture, not feeling. The encyclopedia says that the guide for prayer is not the petitioner's needs, "real or imaginary", nor his feelings or state of mind, nor speculation about the future. "The guide is Scripture alone."
  • Delay is not denial. Even unconditional blessings "may come after apparent delay, or from an unexpected source", and that must be allowed for before deciding a prayer has not been answered. Spurgeon writes that true saints have "continued long in patient waiting without reply, not because their prayers were not vehement, nor because they were unaccepted, but because it so pleased him who is a Sovereign". The Evangelical Dictionary of Biblical Theology, cited by the research assistant, adds that God's apparent silence is typically temporary, set within a wider pattern of his responsiveness.
  • The answer may be different from the request. Hodge says the answer may come, as with Paul's thorn, "in a way we do not expect. But the answer will be such as we, if duly enlightened, would ourselves desire. More than this we need not wish."
  • Prayer shapes the one who prays. The encyclopedia calls part of petition "the discipline of conforming desires to the revealed will and the ongoing providence of God". In that sense prayer teaches. "'Not my will, but thine be done' is the concern of the sincere petitioner." Faith in prayer shows itself in submission to God's will. "Otherwise faith becomes presumption, and humility becomes arrogance."
  • Two extremes are to be avoided. The Holman dictionary warns: we must not think, with Jesus' own example in view, that faith will always get our wishes granted; and we must not go through the motions of prayer without faith.
  • Answers rest on God. In the end, as the research assistant summarised the Evangelical Dictionary, answers to prayer rest on God's graciousness and faithfulness to his promises, "not on the petitioner's claims or demands".

Praying on

None of these cases tells anyone to stop praying. Jesus told the parable of the widow and the judge "to the effect that they ought always to pray and not lose heart" (Luke 18:1). The psalmist waits: "I wait for the Lord, my soul waits, and in his word I hope; my soul waits for the Lord more than watchmen for the morning" (Psalm 130:5–6). And he leaves the timing to God: "my prayer is to you, O Lord. At an acceptable time, O God, in the abundance of your steadfast love answer me" (Psalm 69:13).
