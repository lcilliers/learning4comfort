The Practice of Prayer

The simplest answer Scripture gives to the question "When should I pray?" is "always". But it also shows God's people praying at set times of day, through the night, in trouble and in joy, before decisions, in sickness and persecution, with fasting for a season, at meals, when they meet and part, and at death. This chapter follows those times.

At all times

"Pray without ceasing" (1 Thessalonians 5:17). "Be constant in prayer" (Romans 12:12). "Continue steadfastly in prayer, being watchful in it with thanksgiving" (Colossians 4:2). "Praying at all times in the Spirit, with all prayer and supplication" (Ephesians 6:18). Jesus "told them a parable to the effect that they ought always to pray and not lose heart" (Luke 18:1).

This cannot mean that believers are to do nothing else but speak prayers. Hodge explains it as a habit of the soul: the person who "lives and walks with God, prays always", because the heart is turned toward God through the day, in reverence, gratitude and dependence. The Old Testament had already called for it: "Seek the Lord and his strength; seek his presence continually!" (1 Chronicles 16:11). Cornelius, before he had heard the gospel, "prayed continually to God" (Acts 10:2).

Morning and evening

Alongside the habit of the heart, Scripture shows set times. The morning is the first. "O Lord, in the morning you hear my voice; in the morning I prepare a sacrifice for you and watch" (Psalm 5:3). "In the morning my prayer comes before you" (Psalm 88:13). "I rise before dawn and cry for help; I hope in your words" (Psalm 119:147). Jesus rose "very early in the morning, while it was still dark", to pray (Mark 1:35).

The evening is the second. The time of the evening sacrifice was a time of prayer: "Let my prayer be counted as incense before you, and the lifting up of my hands as the evening sacrifice!" (Psalm 141:2). Ezra prayed "at the evening sacrifice" (Ezra 9:5), and it was "at the time of the evening sacrifice" that Gabriel came to Daniel as he prayed (Daniel 9:21). The Levites were "to stand every morning, thanking and praising the Lord, and likewise at evening" (1 Chronicles 23:30). "It is good ... to declare your steadfast love in the morning, and your faithfulness by night" (Psalm 92:1–2).

Hodge's counsel follows from this: "Let each day, at least, begin and end with God." He urges the same for families, gathered "morning and evening" (Chapter 3).

Through the day

Some prayed more often. "Evening and morning and at noon I utter my complaint and moan, and he hears my voice" (Psalm 55:17). Daniel prayed "three times a day", and would not stop when it was made a crime (Daniel 6:10, 13). One psalmist went further: "Seven times a day I praise you for your righteous rules" (Psalm 119:164).

In the New Testament, the temple's hours of prayer were kept. "Peter and John were going up to the temple at the hour of prayer, the ninth hour" (Acts 3:1), that is, about three in the afternoon. Peter "went up on the housetop about the sixth hour to pray" (Acts 10:9), at noon. Cornelius was praying "at the ninth hour" when the angel came (Acts 10:30). At the birth of John the Baptist, "the whole multitude of the people were praying outside at the hour of incense" (Luke 1:10).

In the night

The night is a time of prayer in Scripture, both chosen and forced. Jesus chose it: "all night he continued in prayer to God" (Luke 6:12). The psalmist chose it: "At midnight I rise to praise you" (Psalm 119:62), and "I remember you upon my bed, and meditate on you in the watches of the night" (Psalm 63:6). Paul and Silas, beaten and chained, were "about midnight ... praying and singing hymns to God" (Acts 16:25).

For others, the night is when trouble keeps them awake. "In the night my hand is stretched out without wearying; my soul refuses to be comforted" (Psalm 77:2). "My soul yearns for you in the night" (Isaiah 26:9). Lamentations calls the grieving city to "cry out in the night, at the beginning of the night watches!" (Lamentations 2:19). Some prayed day and night: Nehemiah for his people (Nehemiah 1:6), the widow Anna in the temple, "worshiping with fasting and prayer night and day" (Luke 2:37), and every widow who "has set her hope on God and continues in supplications and prayers night and day" (1 Timothy 5:5).

In trouble

Trouble is the most natural time to pray, and God invites it. "Call upon me in the day of trouble; I will deliver you, and you shall glorify me" (Psalm 50:15). "In the day of my trouble I call upon you, for you answer me" (Psalm 86:7). "Is anyone among you suffering? Let him pray" (James 5:13).

The verses gathered for this study are full of people who did. Jonah prayed from the belly of the fish (Jonah 2:1). Manasseh prayed "when he was in distress" (2 Chronicles 33:12). Jehoshaphat, facing an army, prayed, "We do not know what to do, but our eyes are on you" (2 Chronicles 20:12). Over and over in Psalm 107, people in every kind of danger "cried to the Lord in their trouble, and he delivered them from their distress" (Psalm 107:6). Smith and Lee draw the lesson that "we should bring all our wants to the Lord", as Moses did when the people were ready to stone him: "Moses cried to the Lord, 'What shall I do with this people?'" (Exodus 17:4).

In joy

But prayer is not only for bad days. "Is anyone cheerful? Let him sing praise" (James 5:13). When prayers are answered, the answer is a time to pray again, in thanks: Daniel gave thanks when the king's dream was revealed (Daniel 2:23), and Hannah sang when Samuel was given (1 Samuel 2:1). The one healed leper who returned "praising God with a loud voice" (Luke 17:15) is set beside the nine who did not.

Before decisions

Scripture shows prayer at turning points. Jesus spent the night in prayer before choosing the twelve: "when day came, he called his disciples and chose from them twelve" (Luke 6:13). The church prayed before choosing a replacement for Judas: "You, Lord, who know the hearts of all, show which one of these two you have chosen" (Acts 1:24). It prayed over the seven (Acts 6:6), over Barnabas and Saul before sending them out (Acts 13:3), and over the elders appointed in each church: "with prayer and fasting they committed them to the Lord" (Acts 14:23). Abraham's servant prayed before choosing a wife for Isaac (Genesis 24:12).

The warning case is Joshua and the Gibeonites. They came pretending to be from far away, and Israel made a treaty with them: "the men took some of their provisions, but did not ask counsel from the Lord" (Joshua 9:14). The Holman dictionary notes that Joshua was tricked when he did not seek God's mind by prayer.

In the moment

Not every decision allows time to withdraw and pray. Nehemiah, asked by the king, "What are you requesting?", "prayed to the God of heaven" and answered at once (Nehemiah 2:4). Such short prayers in the moment (Chapter 3) are always available. No situation is too sudden for a word to God.

In sickness

"Is anyone among you sick? Let him call for the elders of the church, and let them pray over him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord. And the prayer of faith will save the one who is sick, and the Lord will raise him up" (James 5:14–15). Hezekiah prayed on his sickbed (2 Kings 20:2). Paul prayed for the sick father of Publius, "and putting his hands on him, healed him" (Acts 28:8). The psalmist prayed for his enemies when they were sick (Psalm 35:13).

Under persecution

The early church prayed whenever it was threatened. When the apostles were warned to stop preaching, the believers prayed for boldness (Acts 4:24–31). When Peter was imprisoned, "earnest prayer for him was made to God by the church" (Acts 12:5). Jesus commanded prayer for persecutors themselves (Matthew 5:44), and Stephen, as the stones fell, prayed for those throwing them (Acts 7:60).

With fasting, for a season

At certain times God's people set aside a period for concentrated prayer, often with fasting. Ezra proclaimed a fast before the dangerous journey to Jerusalem: "So we fasted and implored our God for this, and he listened to our entreaty" (Ezra 8:23). Nehemiah "wept and mourned for days" and continued "fasting and praying" (Nehemiah 1:4). Daniel sought God "with fasting and sackcloth and ashes" (Daniel 9:3). The church at Antioch was "worshiping the Lord and fasting" when the Spirit spoke (Acts 13:2). Jesus said of one kind of evil spirit, "This kind cannot be driven out by anything but prayer" (Mark 9:29). Paul even allowed married couples to abstain from each other "by agreement for a limited time, that you may devote yourselves to prayer" (1 Corinthians 7:5). Hodge mentions the church's "special service on days of thanksgiving or of fasting and humiliation" as occasions that call for prayer suited to them.

At meals

Food was received with prayer. Jesus, feeding the five thousand, "looked up to heaven and said a blessing" (Matthew 14:19). Paul, in a storm at sea, "took bread, and giving thanks to God in the presence of all he broke it and began to eat" (Acts 27:35). He taught that everything God created is good, "if it is received with thanksgiving, for it is made holy by the word of God and prayer" (1 Timothy 4:4–5).

When believers meet, and when they part

The first Christians gathered for "the apostles' teaching and the fellowship, to the breaking of bread and the prayers" (Acts 2:42). The gathered church on the Lord's day prays together (Chapter 3). Partings, too, were marked with prayer. When Paul left the elders of Ephesus, "he knelt down and prayed with them all" (Acts 20:36). At Tyre, the whole church, "with wives and children", went with him out of the city, "and kneeling down on the beach, we prayed" (Acts 21:5).

At death

Jesus died praying: "Father, into your hands I commit my spirit!" (Luke 23:46). Stephen died the same way: "Lord Jesus, receive my spirit", and then, "Lord, do not hold this sin against them" (Acts 7:59–60). Prayer is the last thing a believer can do in this life, and in Christ it is enough.

Until the end

Peter links prayer to the end of all things: "The end of all things is at hand; therefore be self-controlled and sober-minded for the sake of your prayers" (1 Peter 4:7). Jesus said the same: "stay awake at all times, praying that you may have strength ... to stand before the Son of Man" (Luke 21:36).

The Baker Encyclopedia ends its article here. The church, it says, lives "in an in-between situation". The kingdom of God has begun but is not complete. The church looks back with thanks to what God has done in Christ, and forward to what he will yet do. So her prayer is, "Your kingdom come" (Matthew 6:10). But one day "the kingdom of the world has become the kingdom of our Lord and of his Christ" (Revelation 11:15), and "Death is swallowed up in victory" (1 Corinthians 15:54). When that day comes, the encyclopedia says, such petitions will cease. "Petition will dissolve into ascriptions of praise."

Until then, the prayers of the saints rise before God like incense from golden bowls (Revelation 5:8; 8:4), and the last prayer of the Bible is the church's prayer every day: "Amen. Come, Lord Jesus!" (Revelation 22:20).
