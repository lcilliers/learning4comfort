The Practice of Prayer

9. What to pray

Paul admits that "we do not know what to pray for as we ought" (Romans 8:26). Yet Scripture says a great deal about what to pray, and how. This chapter gathers it: where prayer should begin, the conditions attached to the promises, the spirit in which to pray, and the things Scripture tells believers to ask for.

Begin with God

The Lord's Prayer gives the order. The Baker Encyclopedia notes that its requests are "concerned first with God, his kingdom and his glory, and then with the disciples' needs for forgiveness and for daily support and deliverance". Before anything is asked, God's name is to be hallowed, his kingdom to come and his will to be done "on earth as it is in heaven" (Matthew 6:10). Jesus says the same elsewhere: "seek first the kingdom of God and his righteousness, and all these things will be added to you" (Matthew 6:33).

That order does not make the needs unimportant. It puts them in their place. A prayer that begins with God's glory asks for bread differently from one that begins with hunger.

The conditions of the promises

Smith and Lee set out the conditions attached to the promises of answered prayer. Like the promises, they say, these conditions are "plain and simple".

  • Ask. "Ask, and it will be given to you" (Matthew 7:7). James gives the reverse: "You do not have, because you do not ask" (James 4:2). The Holman dictionary names the irony: God knows our needs, yet we must ask.
  • Ask the Father. "Whatever you ask of the Father in my name, he will give it to you" (John 16:23). He is a Father who "knows what you need before you ask him" (Matthew 6:8), and believers have free access to him.
  • Ask in the name of Jesus. In Hebrew thought, the Holman dictionary explains, a name was linked to the person's character and authority. So prayer in Jesus' name "is prayer that is seeking His will and submissive to His authority" (John 14:13). Hodge adds that to ask in someone's name is to ask "for his sake". To pray in Christ's name is "to urge what Christ is and what He has done, as the reason why we should be heard", not our own merit, nor even simply God's mercy, but Christ's worth. It is not a formula to add at the end of a prayer. It is the ground on which the whole prayer stands.
  • Ask according to his will. "This is the confidence that we have toward him, that if we ask anything according to his will he hears us" (1 John 5:14). Hodge argues that all God's promises about prayer are conditional, and that this verse states the condition the others assume. No sensible person would want God to promise to do whatever short-sighted humans ask. Smith and Lee add: if the word of Christ is living in us richly, we will ask the things that please him.
  • Ask in faith. "Whatever you ask in prayer, believe that you have received it, and it will be yours" (Mark 11:24). "Whoever would draw near to God must believe that he exists and that he rewards those who seek him" (Hebrews 11:6).
  • Abide in him. "If you abide in me, and my words abide in you, ask whatever you wish, and it will be done for you" (John 15:7). Smith and Lee: the branch that is not abiding in the vine "need not pray to be filled with sap".
  • With a heart that does not condemn. "If our heart does not condemn us, we have confidence before God; and whatever we ask we receive from him" (1 John 3:21–22). It is possible, they warn, "to ask with the lips what the heart never expects".

The spirit of prayer

Hodge lists the "requisites of acceptable prayer". They describe not what to ask but how to come.

  • Sincerity. "God is a Spirit. He searches the heart. He is not satisfied with words." It is a great offence to "utter words before Him in which our hearts do not join": thanks without gratitude, confession without any sense of unworthiness, petition without desire.
  • Reverence. God is infinitely exalted, and the prayers of the Bible are pervaded by reverence. Hodge warns against addressing God "as we would a fellow creature". He notes that Luther often began his prayers with Lieber Herr Gott, "dear Lord God", the ordinary greeting among friends: in Luther it was faith and love, but in many who imitate him it shows an irreverent spirit.
  • Humility. This means a due sense of being a creature and a sinner before a holy God. Hodge gives three examples: Job, who said, "I lay my hand on my mouth" (Job 40:4) and "I despise myself, and repent in dust and ashes" (Job 42:6); Isaiah, "Woe is me! For I am lost" (Isaiah 6:5); and the tax collector, "God, be merciful to me, a sinner!" (Luke 18:13).
  • Importunity. Jesus taught persistence three times, Hodge notes. The Canaanite woman would not be put off, though at first "he did not answer her a word" (Matthew 15:23), and was told at last, "O woman, great is your faith! Let it be done for you as you desire" (Matthew 15:28). The widow kept coming to the unjust judge (Luke 18:1–8). The friend at midnight was given what he needed "because of his impudence" (Luke 11:8). God, Hodge says, wants us to value what we ask for, and to show it.
  • Submission. Whatever the request, the one who knows his place before God will say, "not my will, but yours, be done" (Luke 22:42). "He alone knows what is best; granting our request might, in many cases, be our destruction."
  • Faith. We must believe that God is, that he is able to hear and answer, that he is willing, and that he will answer, "if consistent with his own wise purposes and with our best good". Hodge counts lack of confidence in God's readiness to hear "one of the greatest and most common defects in the prayers of Christians". Every father wants his children to trust him.

What Scripture says to pray for

The Baker Encyclopedia lists the chief motives for petition in the New Testament: forgiveness of sins (1 John 1:8–9), freedom from anxiety (Philippians 4:6–7), deliverance from temptation (Matthew 26:41), and the provision of daily needs (Matthew 6:11). The rest of Scripture fills out the list.

For oneself:

  • Daily needs. "Give us this day our daily bread" (Matthew 6:11). The wise man asks for just enough: "give me neither poverty nor riches; feed me with the food that is needful for me" (Proverbs 30:8).
  • Forgiveness and a clean heart. "Forgive us our debts" (Matthew 6:12). "Create in me a clean heart, O God" (Psalm 51:10).
  • Protection from temptation and evil. "Watch and pray that you may not enter into temptation" (Matthew 26:41). "Deliver us from evil" (Matthew 6:13).
  • Freedom from anxiety. "Do not be anxious about anything, but in everything by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known to God. And the peace of God, which surpasses all understanding, will guard your hearts" (Philippians 4:6–7). "Casting all your anxieties on him, because he cares for you" (1 Peter 5:7).
  • Wisdom. "If any of you lacks wisdom, let him ask God" (James 1:5). Solomon asked for "an understanding mind" (1 Kings 3:9), and the psalmist, "teach us to number our days that we may get a heart of wisdom" (Psalm 90:12).
  • The Holy Spirit. "How much more will the heavenly Father give the Holy Spirit to those who ask him!" (Luke 11:13).
  • Healing. "Is anyone among you sick? Let him call for the elders of the church, and let them pray over him" (James 5:14).
  • Searching and guidance. "Search me, O God, and know my heart! ... And see if there be any grievous way in me, and lead me in the way everlasting!" (Psalm 139:23–24).

For others:

  • All people, and those in authority. "Supplications, prayers, intercessions, and thanksgivings be made for all people, for kings and all who are in high positions, that we may lead a peaceful and quiet life" (1 Timothy 2:1–2). This pleases God, "who desires all people to be saved" (1 Timothy 2:4).
  • The spread of the gospel, and those who preach it. "Pray earnestly to the Lord of the harvest to send out laborers into his harvest" (Matthew 9:38). "Pray for us, that the word of the Lord may speed ahead and be honored" (2 Thessalonians 3:1). Paul asked prayer "that words may be given to me in opening my mouth boldly to proclaim the mystery of the gospel" (Ephesians 6:19), and "that God may open to us a door for the word" (Colossians 4:3). The Baker Encyclopedia notes that the effective ministry of the word depends on the prayers of God's people.
  • The growth of believers. Paul's prayers for the churches are a model: "that your love may abound more and more, with knowledge and all discernment" (Philippians 1:9); "that you may be filled with the knowledge of his will in all spiritual wisdom and understanding, so as to walk in a manner worthy of the Lord" (Colossians 1:9–10).
  • All the saints. "Making supplication for all the saints" (Ephesians 6:18). "Pray for one another, that you may be healed" (James 5:16).
  • The salvation of others. "My heart's desire and prayer to God for them is that they may be saved" (Romans 10:1).
  • Enemies. "Love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you" (Matthew 5:44). "Bless those who curse you, pray for those who abuse you" (Luke 6:28).
  • God's people and his city. "Pray for the peace of Jerusalem!" (Psalm 122:6).

For the coming of the kingdom. Above all, the church prays, "Your kingdom come" (Matthew 6:10), and "Come, Lord Jesus!" (Revelation 22:20).

In Scripture's words

Hodge advises those who lead public prayer to have their minds "well stored with the thoughts and language of Scripture". The writers of Scripture spoke as they were moved by the Spirit, so their words "express the mind of the Spirit" and are "the most appropriate vehicles" for the feelings and desires the Spirit awakens. The Lexham Survey calls the Psalms "the richest source of inspiration and example for prayer". Praying Scripture, and praying God's promises back to him (Chapter 7), is one of the surest ways to pray according to his will.

The psalms even give words for beginning: "O Lord, open my lips, and my mouth will declare your praise" (Psalm 51:15), and for ending: "Let the words of my mouth and the meditation of my heart be acceptable in your sight, O Lord, my rock and my redeemer" (Psalm 19:14).

When there are no words

For the times when the one praying does not know what to ask, there is a promise. "The Spirit helps us in our weakness. For we do not know what to pray for as we ought, but the Spirit himself intercedes for us with groanings too deep for words. And he who searches hearts knows what is the mind of the Spirit, because the Spirit intercedes for the saints according to the will of God" (Romans 8:26–27). The Holman dictionary says that without the Spirit, Christians pray without discernment; he "takes up our petitions with an earnest pleading beyond words".

Everything

In the end, nothing is outside the reach of prayer. Smith and Lee put Paul's command (Philippians 4:6) in a phrase of their own: "Be careful for nothing, be prayerful in everything." The Lord is interested in all that concerns his children. Nothing is too hard for him: "Is anything too hard for the Lord?" (Genesis 18:14); "Ah, Lord God! ... Nothing is too hard for you" (Jeremiah 32:17). And he often gives more than was asked. Solomon asked for wisdom, and God said, "I give you also what you have not asked, both riches and honor" (1 Kings 3:13). Paul's prayer for the Ephesians ends in praise "to him who is able to do far more abundantly than all that we ask or think" (Ephesians 3:20).
