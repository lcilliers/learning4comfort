The Practice of Prayer

Introduction

In this chapter

  1. What the study is built from
  2. How it is put together

This is a short study of the practice of prayer: what it is, the forms it takes, how it is expressed, the different kinds of prayer, some special prayers, the way God's people have reminded him of his promises, what to pray, and when to pray.

Prayer is one of the first things Scripture records people doing, and one of the last. Near the beginning, "people began to call upon the name of the Lord" (Genesis 4:26). Near the end, the prayers of the saints rise before God like incense (Revelation 8:4), and the last prayer of the Bible is, "Come, Lord Jesus!" (Revelation 22:20). Between the two lie thousands of prayers, short and long, glad and desperate, spoken aloud and kept in the heart. The psalmist names God by this one thing: "O you who hear prayer, to you shall all flesh come" (Psalm 65:2).

The subject is simple enough for a child, and deep enough to have puzzled the wisest. Prayer is talking to God. Yet it raises real questions. Why ask, if God already knows what we need? Why do some prayers seem to go unanswered? Can prayer change what God does? This study does not avoid those questions, but its main aim is practical: to see from Scripture how God's people have prayed, and so to learn to pray.

What the study is built from

The study began with a set of questions about prayer put to the research assistant in the Logos Bible software, and the answers it gave from standard reference works. Those questions asked for passages where believers reminded God of his promises, how to understand unanswered prayer in the light of those promises, and examples of prayers that were blocked or not granted. The last question asked for the harder cases: prayers that did not materialise for reasons other than sin, unbelief or wrong motives.

Behind those answers stand several longer pieces, each read in full:

  • two dictionary articles on prayer, from the Baker Encyclopedia of the Bible and the Holman Illustrated Bible Dictionary, which trace prayer through the Old and New Testaments and discuss the difficulties it raises;
  • an article on prayer in the life of the church, from the Lexham Survey of Theology, which sorts prayer into its kinds and lists the great prayers of the Old Testament;
  • the chapter on prayer in Charles Hodge's Systematic Theology, a nineteenth-century work that sets out what prayer assumes about God, what makes prayer acceptable, and the forms of secret, family and public prayer;
  • an outline on prayer's promises, conditions, hindrances and examples, from Smith and Lee's Handfuls on Purpose;
  • word studies and commentary notes on how prayer is expressed: the lifted voice, weeping, lament and song.

Alongside these is a list of about 990 verses exported from the STEP Bible software, gathered by searching the English Bible for the words of prayer and speech. Many of them turned out to be about other kinds of words: the words of kings, of prophets, or of ordinary conversation. About 180 speak directly of prayer, pleading, crying out and entreating the Lord. They are read here together with the passages the reference works point to.

How it is put together

Chapter 1 asks what prayer is, and what it takes for granted about God. Chapter 2 follows prayer through Scripture, from the first calling on the Lord's name to the prayers of the early church. Chapter 3 looks at the forms prayer takes: alone and in secret, in the family, and in the gathered church, spontaneous or set. Chapter 4 looks at how prayer is expressed, in words and without them, in tears and song, and through the body. Chapter 5 sets out the kinds of prayer: praise, thanksgiving, confession, petition, intercession, the hard prayers against the wicked, and the prayer of complaint. Chapter 6 reads some of the special prayers of Scripture, from Abraham's plea for Sodom to the Lord's Prayer. Chapter 7 turns to the way God's people reminded him of his promises. Chapter 8 faces the prayers that were not granted, or not yet. Chapter 9 asks what to pray, and Chapter 10 when to pray. Chapter 11 lists the sources.

Every quotation of Scripture is from the English Standard Version, and each has been checked against the ESV text. Where the ESV prints LORD for the divine name, it is written here as "the Lord". The reference works quote older translations; their Scripture is given here in the ESV wording, while their own words are quoted as they wrote them.
