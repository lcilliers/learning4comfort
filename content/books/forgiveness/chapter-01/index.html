1. Definitions and Conceptual Foundations

The psychology of forgiveness is a relatively young field. As of 1997, only 58 empirical studies on forgiveness had been published. Since then, the number has more than quadrupled, with forgiveness becoming one of the most actively researched areas in positive psychology and clinical psychology.

1.1 Core Definitions

Enright and Fitzgibbons (2000, 2015): "People, upon rationally determining that they have been unfairly treated, forgive when they willfully abandon resentment and related responses (to which they have a right), and endeavor to respond to the wrongdoer based on the moral principle of beneficence, which may include compassion, unconditional worth, generosity, and moral love (to which the wrongdoer, by nature of the hurtful acts, has no right)."

Enright (2001): Forgiveness occurs when individuals who have been unjustly hurt are able to overcome their resentments and offer compassion to the wrongdoer. The approach focuses on changing feelings, thoughts, and behaviours towards those who have been hurtful.

Worthington (2004): Forgiveness is conceptualised as an emotional juxtaposition of positive emotions (empathy, sympathy, compassion, or love) against the negative emotions of unforgiveness. Forgiveness can thus be used as an emotion-focused coping strategy to reduce a stressful reaction to a transgression.

McCullough, Pargament & Thoresen (2000): Forgiveness is defined as a prosocial change in the victim’s motivations, thoughts, and feelings toward a perceived transgressor. This involves a decrease in negative motivations (revenge, avoidance) and potentially an increase in positive motivations (benevolence) toward the offender.

Freedman & Enright (1996): Forgiveness is "an act of deliberately giving up resentment toward an offender while fostering the undeserved qualities of beneficence and compassion toward that offender."

Key observation: There is currently no single consensus definition of forgiveness in the psychological literature (Gregory, 2025; Boon, 2025). This itself is a significant finding. Most definitions share common elements: (a) acknowledgement of wrongdoing, (b) a voluntary process, (c) reduction of negative responses, and (d) potential cultivation of positive responses toward the offender.

1.2 Decisional vs. Emotional Forgiveness

Decisional forgiveness involves making a conscious decision to forgive a personal offence, let go of angry and resentful thoughts, and treat the offender with dignity. Worthington describes this as a "behaviour intention statement" — not necessarily a behaviour in itself. A person can sincerely make a decision to forgive and still feel unforgiveness emotions such as resentment, bitterness, hostility, hatred, anger, and fear.

Emotional forgiveness involves replacing those negative emotions with positive feelings like compassion, sympathy, and empathy. Research shows that emotional forgiveness is where most health benefits are found. Emotional forgiveness affects mental health and physical health directly.

Lichtenfeld et al. (2019) confirmed experimentally that emotional forgiveness, but not decisional forgiveness, affects cognition — specifically, individuals who had emotionally forgiven held the transgressor less responsible for the offence. This finding reinforces that the two forms should be treated as distinct facets.

2. Major Psychological Models of Forgiveness

Multiple process models of forgiveness have been developed. The two most empirically supported are the Enright Process Model and Worthington’s REACH Forgiveness model. Approximately one-third of all forgiveness intervention studies have used each of these two models, with the remaining third spread across other approaches.

2.1 Enright’s Process Model of Forgiveness

The Enright Process Model, first described by Enright and the Human Development Study Group (1991) and first empirically tested by Hebl and Enright (1993), proceeds through four phases:

Phase 1 — Uncovering: The person gains insight into whether and how the injustice and subsequent injury have compromised their life. This involves confronting the nature of the offence and uncovering the consequences. A fundamental step is clarifying as objectively as possible who did what to whom. One cannot forgive an offence that did not occur, although one may resolve the anger from a perceived offence when the actual nature of the event is understood.

Phase 2 — Decision: The person is taught about forgiveness and decides to commit to forgiving. Forgiveness is explicitly chosen as a response.

Phase 3 — Work: The person seeks to understand the offender, develops empathy, and works through the process of reframing the event. The person attempts to feel what the wrongdoer felt during the hurtful act. The ‘Altruistic Gift of Forgiveness’ sub-phase asks the hurt person to recall a time when they themselves hurt someone, the guilt attached to that feeling, and how it felt to be forgiven, thus hoping to motivate extending forgiveness to their own offender.

Phase 4 — Deepening: The person seeks to find meaning or purpose in suffering. They may discover release from the "emotional prison" of unforgiveness, bitterness, resentment, and anger. People often feel less sad, anxious, and suspicious, and may find renewed purpose in life.

Clinical evidence: The Enright model has been shown through randomised experimental and control group clinical trials to improve emotional well-being in multiple settings across diverse cultures. Populations studied include incest survivors, emotionally abused women, cancer patients, substance abusers, elderly adults, and children in conflict zones (Belfast, Northern Ireland). Reference: Handbook of Forgiveness, 2nd Ed., Worthington & Wade (2020), pp. 266–276.

2.2 Worthington’s REACH Forgiveness Model

REACH is an acronym representing five steps:

R — Recall the Hurt: Acknowledge the hurt without minimising or catastrophising. When hurt, it is natural to experience fear or anger.

E — Empathise with the Offender: Try to understand the offender’s perspective and emotional state at the time of the transgression.

A — Altruistic Gift: Recall a time when you yourself were forgiven and draw on that experience to offer forgiveness as an altruistic gift.

C — Commit: Make a public or concrete commitment to forgive.

H — Hold onto Forgiveness: When doubts arise, remind yourself of the commitment and the work already done.

Personal note on Worthington: Worthington is not an armchair scientist. In 1996, his elderly mother was raped and beaten to death. His personal struggle with forgiveness following this horrific event, and his subsequent forgiveness of the perpetrators, informs both the academic and deeply human dimensions of his work.

2.3 Other Notable Models

Luskin’s Forgive for Good (FFG) model: Has less research support than either Enright’s or REACH, but is considered an evidence-based practice for promoting better cardiovascular health. Collaborator Loren Toussaint has conducted recent studies comparing FFG to REACH, with both showing efficacy.

Gordon and Baucom (1998) model: Specifically developed for couples dealing with affairs. Implies forgiveness occurs when one no longer experiences negative cognition, affect, and behaviour.

DiBlasio’s decision-based approach: Focuses on couples using a decision-based framework.

Note on model divergence: Models differ on what constitutes the endpoint of the forgiveness process. Some posit loving, compassionate, and benevolent responses as the endpoint. Others propose interpersonal interaction and reconciliation as essential. Still others suggest forgiveness occurs simply when negative responses cease. This lack of consensus on endpoints mirrors the definitional ambiguity noted in Section 1.

3. Types and Dimensions of Forgiveness

Research identifies multiple dimensions and types of forgiveness, each with distinct characteristics and predictors:

Trait forgiveness (forgivingness): The capacity to forgive interpersonal transgressions across situations over time. Often reliant on a pattern of contemplation, as emotion peaks immediately following transgression and slowly declines.

Dispositional forgiveness: An enduring personality trait not specific to a particular situation or transgressor. Occurs without the need for extended contemplation.

Offence-specific (state) forgiveness: Forgiveness of a specific person for a single transgression.

Dyadic forgiveness: Forgiveness in the context of a relationship with a specific person, occurring over a history of transgressions.

Self-forgiveness: Relevant when a person has done something to hurt another, is aware of the nature and extent of their actions, and may consequently experience debilitating degrees of guilt, shame, or self-loathing. (Covered in depth in Section 10.)

Forgiveness of situations: The ability to forgive circumstances beyond human control (e.g., natural disasters, illness).

Divine forgiveness: The experience of being forgiven by God or a Higher Power. Research indicates divine forgiveness, self-forgiveness, and interpersonal forgiveness are positively correlated with each other but are distinct constructs.

Important finding: Episodic (state) and dispositional forgiveness have different predictors and consequences. Contextual factors such as transgression severity, apology, and reparation significantly influence episodic forgiveness but are less influential on dispositional forgiveness (Thompson et al., 2005).

Negative forgiveness: Research also identifies that forgiveness can sometimes occur through negative processes. Motives are considered negative when an individual forgives another to: (a) manipulate that person, (b) make that person feel indebted, or (c) create guilt. This is an important caveat in understanding forgiveness as universally positive.

4. What Forgiveness Is Not — Key Distinctions

The psychological literature identifies several critical distinctions about what forgiveness is not. These distinctions are consistently emphasised across both secular and faith-based sources:

Forgiveness is not condoning or excusing

Forgiveness does not minimise or justify the wrongdoer’s actions. It acknowledges the harm caused while choosing to release oneself from the negative emotional burden. It does not imply that the actions were acceptable or permissible.

Forgiveness is not forgetting

The popular phrase "forgive and forget" is not supported by psychological research. Instead, forgiveness creates a new way to remember — perhaps with more understanding about why someone acted as they did, or viewing the event from a different perspective, while still not excusing or justifying the behaviour.

Forgiveness is not reconciliation

This is the most commonly conflated distinction. Forgiveness is an internal, individual process; reconciliation is an interpersonal process involving both parties. One can forgive without reconciling. Forgiveness does not require re-entering the relationship or restoring trust. (See Section 12 for detailed analysis.)

Forgiveness does not remove accountability

Forgiveness does not absolve the wrongdoer of responsibility for their actions or imply they should not face consequences.

Forgiveness does not require tolerating ongoing harm

Forgiveness can coexist with healthy boundaries. It does not require the forgiver to continue engaging with the wrongdoer or to tolerate further harm.

Forgiveness is not weakness

Forgiveness demonstrates strength of character, wisdom, courage, and fortitude. It takes considerable effort and is often a prolonged process.

Forgiveness is not necessarily instantaneous

Research shows forgiveness follows a dynamic process that changes over time, typically following a logarithmic function where forgiving motivations increase rapidly at first and then level off. It is not a single event but an ongoing process.

5. Why Forgive? — Documented Benefits

5.1 Physical Health Benefits

Empirical research has shown positive relationships between forgiveness and physical health in areas including but not limited to:

  • Cholesterol levels
  • Heart health and cardiovascular functioning
  • HIV progression
  • Hypertension (blood pressure reduction)
  • Physical pain (lower intensity and less daily interference)
  • Sleep quality
  • Fibromyalgia symptom reduction
  • Chronic low back pain reduction
  • Longer lifespan

Physiological research by Witvliet et al. (2001) found that when people adopted an empathic mindset toward a transgressor or recalled forgiving someone, they showed marked reductions in mean arterial pressure, heart rate, and skin conductance compared to unforgiving states. This suggests forgiveness directly reduces sympathetic nervous system arousal.

Worthington identified specific dangers of chronic anger (a hallmark of unforgiveness) on the body: elevated blood pressure, elevated heart rate, decreased heart-rate variability, and chronically elevated cortisol and adrenaline. While short-term cortisol is adaptive, chronically elevated cortisol is seriously disturbing to body systems.

5.2 Mental Health Benefits

A systematic review and meta-analysis by Akhtar and Barlow (2016) of randomised controlled trials found forgiveness interventions are effective in reducing:

  • Depression (SMD = −0.37, 95% CI [−0.68, −0.07])
  • Anger and hostility (SMD = −0.49, 95% CI [−0.77, −0.22])
  • Stress and distress (SMD = −0.66, 95% CI [−0.91, −0.41])

Additional documented mental health benefits include:

  • Reduced anxiety (Freedman & Enright, 1996; Lin et al., 2004)
  • Reduced post-traumatic stress symptoms (Reed & Enright, 2006)
  • Increased self-esteem and hopefulness for the future
  • Increased life satisfaction (Toussaint et al., 2001)
  • Reduced suicidal ideation (Quintana-Orts & Rey, 2018)
  • Improved memory in older adults (Toussaint)
  • Freedom from guilt and prevention of misdirected anger (Coyle & Enright, 1998)

5.3 Relational Benefits

Willingness to forgive and be forgiven was identified as one of the 10 most important characteristics of long-term relationships (Fenell, 1993). Forgiveness creates closeness in romantic relationships (Tsang, McCullough & Fincham, 2006) and promotes social connections in general (Karremans, Van Lange & Holland, 2005).

5.4 Forgiveness as Coping Strategy

Toussaint et al. (PMC5055412) conducted a 5-week dynamic parallel process study with 332 adults and found that increases in forgiveness were associated with decreases in perceived stress, which were in turn related to decreases in mental health symptoms. Importantly, the relationship was reciprocal: decreases in mental health symptoms also led to decreases in stress, which in turn increased forgiveness. This suggests a virtuous cycle.

Loren Toussaint described forgiveness as having a dual role: it is good for health directly, but also helps protect against things that are not good for health that one cannot avoid. It serves as a protective coping mechanism.

6. Unforgiveness: Nature and Consequences

6.1 Definition

Worthington and Wade (1999) defined unforgiveness as a complex combination of delayed negative emotions — including resentment, bitterness, hostility, hatred, anger, and fear — that arise in response to a perceived transgression. Unforgiveness is distinct from the initial emotional response to being hurt; it is a sustained state that develops over time.

6.2 Unforgiveness as Stress Response

Many researchers conceptualise unforgiveness as a stress response to a transgression. The stress-and-coping model of forgiveness holds that: (a) unforgiveness, indexed by anger, hate, and resentment, creates stressful intrapersonal and interpersonal situations; (b) unforgiveness contributes to the detrimental effects of stress on health; and (c) forgiveness is a coping mechanism capable of reducing stress associated with unforgiveness.

Unforgiveness is reflected in specific cortisol levels, adrenaline production, and cytokine balance. Chronic unforgiveness maintains the body in a state of stress arousal that, over time, contributes to cardiovascular disease, immune dysfunction, and other health problems.

6.3 Rumination as a Maintenance Mechanism

Rumination on the transgression is thought to be central to establishing and maintaining unforgiveness. A study by Qinglu et al. (2019) showed that anger mediates the relationship between rumination on the transgression and forgiveness, demonstrating the powerful role of anger in sustaining the unforgiveness state. Berry et al. (2005) similarly connected vengeful rumination with lower levels of forgivingness.

6.4 Consequences of Unforgiveness

Qualitative research from the University of Warwick (PMC5320019) found that all participants spoke of negative consequences of unforgiveness on mental health. Three key themes emerged:

(1) Negative effect on mental health in terms of how participants felt (emotional impact)
(2) Negative effect on cognitive abilities
(3) Barriers to personal and social growth

Additional consequences documented in the literature include depression, anxiety, increased somatic complaints, social dysfunction, impaired interpersonal relationships, and physical health problems.

6.5 A Nuanced View: Unforgiveness Is Not Always Pathological

Important counterpoint: Boon (2025) argues that the psychology literature’s tendency to treat unforgiveness as pathology may have two undesirable consequences: (1) it may stifle inquiry into when unforgiveness might be an appropriate, legitimate, and even adaptive response to wrongdoing; and (2) it may place an additional burden on victims.

Stackhouse, Ross & Boon (2016) found that while emotional-ruminative unforgiveness was associated with poor psychological well-being, cognitive-evaluative unforgiveness (a deliberate evaluative stance) was not. This suggests individuals who move past negative affect while maintaining an unforgiving evaluative stance may not suffer the same adverse health impacts.

Kira et al. (2009) found that among Iraqi refugees, unforgiveness of Saddam Hussein was associated with higher, not lower, scores on health and well-being measures.

7. Emotions That Support Forgiveness

Research has identified several positive emotions and character traits that facilitate the forgiveness process:

Empathy

Empathy is consistently identified as the most significant facilitator of forgiveness. Worthington’s entire REACH model positions empathy as a central mechanism. Fatfouta et al. (2014) found that empathy for the transgressor was the significant mediator that created more room for forgiveness, even among narcissistic personalities. Multiple studies confirm forgiving people are more empathic than less forgiving people (Tangney, Fee, Reinsmith, Boone & Lee, 1999).

Compassion and Self-Compassion

Compassion, along with its self-directed form, is fundamental. Exline, Baumeister, Zell, Kraft & Witvliet (2008) found experimentally that people asked to look for similarities between themselves and transgressors were more likely to be forgiving. Self-compassion reflects the ability to hold a kind, non-judgemental view of oneself and recognise similarities between oneself and others.

Humility

Humility facilitates forgiveness by enabling perspective-taking and reducing self-focus. Humility is amplified when a person experiences being forgiven, which in turn facilitates empathy for others’ need for forgiveness. It is also accompanied by gratitude.

Gratitude

Gratitude and forgiveness are theoretically linked character strengths. Both are positively correlated with agreeableness and negatively correlated with neuroticism. Gratitude reinforces future prosocial behaviours and diminishes tendencies to hold grudges. McCullough et al. (2002) found gratitude positively correlates with both cognitive (perspective-taking) and affective (warm feelings) aspects of empathy.

Personality Traits: Agreeableness and Emotional Stability

People with high agreeableness and emotional stability have a high propensity to forgive (Brose et al., 2005; Maltby et al., 2008; McCullough et al., 2001; Walker & Gorsuch, 2002). Forgiving people are found to be less ruminative and less narcissistic than less forgiving people (Metts & Cupach, 1998; Davidson, 1993).

8. Barriers to Forgiveness

8.1 Narcissistic Entitlement

Exline, Baumeister, Bushman, Campbell & Finkel (2004) demonstrated across six studies that narcissistic entitlement impedes forgiveness in ways not captured by other robust predictors (offence severity, apology, relationship closeness, religiosity, Big Five personality factors). Narcissistic entitlement involves expectations of special treatment and preoccupation with defending one’s rights. It is a robust, distinct predictor of unforgiveness.

Further research by Fatfouta et al. (2014, N=1040) distinguished between two narcissistic styles: admiration (“I am great”) and rivalry (“I want my rivals to fail”). The rivalry style was positively related to revenge and avoidance via greater anger, rumination, and less empathy. The admiration style, by contrast, was associated with greater empathy and more capacity for forgiveness.

8.2 Dark Personality Traits (Dark Triad)

Machiavellianism and psychopathy were negatively related to both aspects of forgiveness — the absence of negative thoughts and the presence of positive attitudes toward the wrongdoer. Angry afterthoughts, angry memories, and especially thoughts of revenge mediated these relationships. Narcissism (as measured by the Short Dark Triad) showed weaker associations, though this may reflect measurement differences with the entitlement-focused NPI used by Exline et al.

8.3 Rumination

Anger rumination — repeatedly dwelling on angry memories, afterthoughts, and revenge fantasies — is a significant barrier. It prolongs the natural anger response and prevents the emotional processing necessary for forgiveness. Anger rumination fully mediated the relationship between Machiavellianism/psychopathy and the absence of positive forgiveness.

8.4 Severity of Offence and Lack of Apology

While not personality-based, the severity of the transgression and absence of apology or remorse from the offender are well-established situational barriers to forgiveness.

8.5 Pride

Related to but distinct from narcissism, pride acts as a barrier by creating a sense that forgiving is a concession of status or an admission that the hurt was acceptable.

8.6 Fear

Fear of re-victimisation or of being perceived as weak inhibits forgiveness. The common misconception that forgiveness requires reconciliation (see Section 4) amplifies this barrier.

8.7 Perceived Injustice and Need for Justice

A strong sense of injustice, particularly when coupled with a desire for retributive justice, works against forgiveness. However, research by Witvliet et al. (2003) suggested that for victims, restorative justice combined with granting forgiveness may yield the strongest emotional benefits.

9. Forgiveness and Mental Health Conditions

9.1 Depression

The relationship between lack of forgiveness and depression is well-documented. Hunter (1978) was the first to observe the disappearance of anxiety, anger, depression, and obsessions following forgiveness. More recently, meta-analytic evidence confirms a consistent negative association between forgiveness and depression (Akhtar & Barlow, 2016: SMD = −0.37).

Forgiveness has been associated with reduced clinical symptoms of depression across university students, older adults, incest survivors, and emotionally abused women.

9.2 Anxiety Disorders

Forgiveness interventions have consistently shown reductions in anxiety. Lin et al. (2004) found that forgiveness therapy led to decreases in anxiety among substance-dependent patients. Freedman and Enright (1996) found anxiety decreased in incest survivors.

9.3 Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD)

A systematic review of forgiveness in PTSD after man-made traumatic events found a consistent negative correlation between forgiveness and PTSD symptoms across multiple studies and populations, including terror attack survivors, genocide survivors, childhood maltreatment survivors, military veterans, and betrayal trauma victims.

Reed and Enright (2006) found that emotionally abused women showed greater improvement in depression, trait anxiety, and post-traumatic stress symptoms in a forgiveness therapy group compared to an alternative therapy group (which included anger validation, assertiveness strategies, and interpersonal skills). Improvements were retained during follow-up.

Caveat: One study (Witvliet et al., 2004, with military veterans; and a UK addiction study by Flanagan et al., 2012) found that forgiveness did not predict outcomes in all populations, particularly those with substance abuse comorbidities. This suggests that the forgiveness-health relationship may have boundary conditions that are not yet fully understood.

9.4 Addiction and Substance Abuse

A study of 81 people in recovery from drug and alcohol addiction found that 54% met criteria for full PTSD and reported anxiety, somatic problems, and depression. While forgiveness itself did not predict outcomes in this particular study, spirituality and guilt were significant predictors. Self-forgiveness is considered particularly relevant in addiction recovery.

9.5 Chronic Pain

A preliminary study of 61 chronic low back pain patients (Carson, 2005) showed that higher forgiveness scores were associated with lower levels of pain, anger, and psychological distress. State anger largely mediated associations between forgiveness and sensory pain.

10. Self-Forgiveness

10.1 Nature and Importance

Self-forgiveness is conceptually similar to interpersonal forgiveness in that it involves prosocial motivational change. However, it targets the self rather than another person. It is relevant when a person has done something harmful, is aware of it, and experiences guilt, shame, self-loathing, or similar responses. Self-forgiveness can also address harm done to oneself (e.g., violating one’s moral code, self-sabotage).

Individuals who score highly on self-forgiveness measures exhibit greater life satisfaction (Macaskill, 2012), lower levels of depression (Sternthal et al., 2010), and enhanced physiological health (Wilson et al., 2008).

10.2 The Roles of Guilt and Shame

Research distinguishes the effects of guilt and shame on self-forgiveness:

Guilt: Involves a negative evaluation of one’s behaviour ("I did a bad thing"). Guilt-prone individuals are more likely to apologise, approach offences, and bring behaviour in line with standards. Guilt generally has a positive association with self-forgiveness because its motivational properties encourage reparation.

Shame: Involves a negative evaluation of the self ("I am a bad person"). Shame is associated with avoidance, withdrawal, and secrecy. Shame is broadly and consistently negatively associated with self-forgiveness. It is the primary emotional inhibitor of self-forgiveness.

In substance abuse recovery, consistent patterns emerge: guilt has a positive association with self-forgiveness, while shame is negatively associated. Acceptance, conciliatory behaviour, and empathy serve as mediators.

10.3 Self-Forgiveness as Distinct from Pseudo-Forgiveness

Genuine self-forgiveness must be distinguished from pseudo-self-forgiveness, which involves minimising one’s wrongdoing or shifting blame. True self-forgiveness involves acknowledging responsibility, experiencing appropriate guilt, making amends where possible, and then releasing self-condemnation. It is paradoxical in that it requires experiencing psychological distress while also working to neutralise its impact.

Unlike forgiving others, self-forgiveness must involve reconciliation — with oneself. It requires integrating previously unacceptable characteristics so that one can accept all of who one is without self-sabotaging, which could lead to self-abuse or addiction.

11. Cancel Culture and Its Impact on Forgiveness

11.1 Definition and Rise

Cancel culture represents a form of social ostracism where individuals or groups are excluded from social or professional circles, either online, in the real world, or both. It has been accelerated by social media, leading to rapid and widespread public judgements and reactions. There is no definitive consensus on its exact meaning, though it generally involves publicly withdrawing support from ("cancelling") individuals who have done or said something considered objectionable.

11.2 Psychological Impact

Research identifies three main psychological processes at play:

(1) Identifying a wrongdoing and assessing its gravity
(2) Taking part in punishing actions that the wrongdoer may suffer
(3) Consequences of the cancellation action

The psychological effects on those targeted include severe emotional suffering from cyberbullying, reputational harm, and public humiliation. These create cycles of fear and self-censorship. Research shows 73% of social media users worried about being "cancelled" and changed their online behaviour accordingly.

Effects on the broader society include heightened polarisation, reluctance to have candid conversations, self-censorship in academic and professional environments, and what amounts to a chilling effect on free expression.

11.3 Cancel Culture’s Tension with Forgiveness

Cancel culture operates in fundamental tension with the principles of forgiveness identified in the psychological literature:

  • It is predicated on an ideal of perfection, whereas forgiveness acknowledges human fallibility
  • It does not allow people to learn from their mistakes, whereas forgiveness models emphasise growth through the process
  • It relies on shame as a primary mechanism, whereas shame is identified as the primary inhibitor of self-forgiveness
  • It operates through group/mob dynamics, whereas forgiveness is an individual, voluntary process
  • Core mechanism is exclusion, whereas forgiveness involves (though does not require) potential re-engagement

Michael Eric Dyson, writing in the New York Times (2021), asked: "Where is the forgiveness and grace in cancel culture?" — highlighting the absence of these restorative elements.

11.4 Potential Positive Dimensions

Counterpoint: Research also identifies potential benefits. Cancel culture can elevate under-represented voices, create accountability, and provide collective validation for harmed groups. A study (PMC10399695) found that episodes of cancel culture could provide conditions for harmed groups to experience collective validation, increasing empowerment and inspiring collective action. This is the first social psychological analysis of cancel culture’s impact on collective action.

11.5 Restorative Alternatives

Counselling professionals advocate for approaches that include acceptance and forgiveness rather than pure punitive responses. Restorative justice approaches are being explored as alternatives that maintain accountability while allowing for growth and change — consistent with the models of forgiveness identified in Section 2.

12. Forgiveness vs. Reconciliation

The distinction between forgiveness and reconciliation is one of the most critical in the entire forgiveness literature. Confusion between these concepts is one of the most significant barriers to forgiveness.

12.1 Key Distinctions

ForgivenessReconciliation
Internal, individual processInterpersonal process requiring both parties
Between the person and God/themselvesBetween two or more people
Always possible and always plausibleNot always possible or advisable
Can be a moment of decisionOften a marathon — a prolonged process
Does not require the offender’s participationRequires acknowledgement, remorse, and commitment to change from offender
Does not require restoring trustInvolves rebuilding trust
Releases the forgiver from emotional burdenRestores or rebuilds a damaged relationship
A personal act of faith/willA collaborative process

It is possible to forgive someone without reconciling with them. It is not possible to truly reconcile without forgiving. Sometimes the healthiest thing for two people is distance. Forgiveness is always commanded (in the religious sense) and always beneficial (in the psychological sense), but reconciliation is not always safe, healthy, or possible.

12.2 The Danger of Conflating the Two

Gregory (2025) describes clinical cases where the confusion between forgiveness and reconciliation led to further harm — particularly in cases of domestic abuse where "forgiving" was understood by family members to mean the victim should resume contact with their abuser. In some cases, people pressured to reconcile with abusers were further abused, and in extreme cases, even murdered.

13. Key Researchers and Their Contributions

Robert D. Enright (University of Wisconsin-Madison): Developer of the Process Model of Forgiveness. Pioneer of forgiveness education in conflict zones (Belfast). Author of Forgiveness Is a Choice (2001) and Forgiveness Therapy (2015, with Fitzgibbons). Catholic psychologist whose model is psychological rather than religious.

Everett L. Worthington Jr. (Virginia Commonwealth University, Emeritus): Developer of REACH Forgiveness. Distinguished between decisional and emotional forgiveness. Author of Forgiving and Reconciling (2003) and Forgiveness and Reconciliation: Theory and Application (2006). Personal experience of his mother’s murder informs his work.

Michael E. McCullough: Foundational work on forgiveness measurement, motivational definitions, and longitudinal dynamics of forgiveness. Co-editor of Forgiveness: Theory, Research, and Practice (2000).

Loren Toussaint: Health psychologist specialising in forgiveness and physical health outcomes. Research on forgiveness as protective coping mechanism, memory and aging.

Fred Luskin: Developer of the Forgive for Good approach. Focus on forgiveness and cardiovascular health.

Nathaniel Wade: Research on forgiveness interventions and meta-analyses of group forgiveness programs.

Julie Exline: (Case Western Reserve University): Research on narcissistic entitlement as a barrier to forgiveness.

Charlotte vanOyen Witvliet: Pioneering physiological research on the bodily effects of forgiving and unforgiving mental states.

14. Identified Gaps and Unresolved Questions

The following gaps and unresolved questions emerge from this research collation:

(1) There is no consensus definition of forgiveness in the psychological literature. This fundamental ambiguity affects measurement, comparison across studies, and clinical application.

(2) The boundary conditions of the forgiveness-health relationship are not fully understood. Some populations (e.g., those with substance abuse comorbidities, certain trauma survivors) do not show the expected benefits.

(3) Research on unforgiveness as a potentially adaptive response remains limited. The nuanced view of cognitive-evaluative unforgiveness (Boon, 2025) deserves further investigation.

(4) Self-forgiveness remains under-researched compared to interpersonal forgiveness, and systematic reviews in this area are limited.

(5) The impact of cancel culture on forgiveness processes has not been directly studied through empirical research — most analysis is theoretical or commentary-based.

(6) Cross-cultural variation in forgiveness processes and definitions needs further study, though some work exists (e.g., Enright’s Belfast studies, Iraqi refugee research).

(7) The role of forgiveness in specific clinical populations (e.g., borderline personality disorder, where Linehan’s DBT is being integrated with forgiveness therapy) is still emerging.

(8) Gender differences in forgiveness are under-explored. Some meta-analytic findings suggest the relationship between personality forgiveness and negative emotion is moderated by gender (the higher the proportion of females, the weaker the relationship).

(9) The relationship between forgiveness and justice — particularly how restorative vs. retributive justice frameworks interact with forgiveness — remains an active research area.
