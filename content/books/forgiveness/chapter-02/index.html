15. Hard Scenarios — Forgiveness Under Extreme Conditions

Three scenarios pose the most challenging questions for forgiveness theory and practice. These are not edge cases — they represent the situations where people most urgently need guidance and where confusion causes the greatest harm. This section collates available research evidence for each. These scenarios carry directly into the biblical pillars (divine forgiveness and human forgiveness in Scripture) and will be cross-referenced there.

15.1 Scenario 1: Does Forgiveness Require Forgoing Values or Minimising the Harm?

Core question: Must the forgiver abandon their moral position, water down the severity of what was done, or accept the transgression as less harmful than it was in order to forgive?

Research answer: No — authentic forgiveness explicitly requires the opposite

The Enright Process Model begins with the Uncovering Phase, which requires the injured person to confront the full objective nature and impact of the offence before any decision to forgive. Enright states one cannot forgive an offence not fully acknowledged. The model requires determining "as objectively as possible who did what to whom" and how the injustice "has compromised his or her life." Skipping this phase produces pseudo-forgiveness — not genuine forgiveness.

Source: Enright, R. D. (2001). Forgiveness Is a Choice. APA Books. Enright Process Model summary in CourageRC document.

The Enright definition preserves moral evaluation: "People, upon rationally determining that they have been unfairly treated, forgive when they willfully abandon resentment and related responses (to which they have a right)." The phrase "to which they have a right" explicitly affirms the moral legitimacy of the victim's negative response before they choose to release it. Forgiveness does not deny the right to resentment; it transcends it.

Source: Enright, R. D. & Fitzgibbons, R. P. (2015). Forgiveness Therapy (2nd ed.). APA.

Empirical evidence: Value integrity is preserved or enhanced by forgiving

Quinney, Zubielevitch & Okimoto (2025) conducted three studies (N=300, N=327, N=296 pre-registered replication) examining the psychological consequences of offering forgiveness versus refusing forgiveness versus inaction. They found that both offering forgiveness and refusing to forgive resulted in greater value integrity and greater state self-esteem compared to inaction. The critical finding: it is the deliberate moral act of taking a stance — whether forgiving or refusing — that affirms values. Passivity does not.

Source: Quinney, B., Zubielevitch, E. & Okimoto, T. G. (2025). Refusing to forgive can have psychological benefits. Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 122, 104848.

Key finding: Both forgiving and deliberately refusing to forgive increase value integrity and self-esteem compared to inaction. The act of taking a moral stance — either way — preserves the forgiver's sense of values. The danger lies in passivity, not in the direction of the choice.

The danger of minimising: Pseudo-forgiveness

When forgiveness involves minimising the harm, denying the severity of the offence, or pretending the transgression was less significant, the research identifies this as pseudo-forgiveness. Pseudo-forgiveness is associated with deterioration in self-respect and self-concept clarity, particularly when the offender has not demonstrated remorse or willingness to change.

Freedman and Zarifikar (2016) emphasised that admitting and expressing feelings about the transgression is a critical step before forgiveness can occur, as outlined in the first phase of Enright's process model. Forgiveness is often criticised precisely because people mistakenly believe it involves suppression or denial of emotions.

Source: Freedman, S. & Zarifikar, T. (2016). The psychology of interpersonal forgiveness and guidelines for forgiveness therapy. Spirituality in Clinical Practice, 3(1), 45–58.

Research on intimate partner violence found that women in abusive relationships sometimes achieved apparent "forgiveness" through actively minimising or denying the magnitude of their partners' transgressions. This was associated with worse outcomes. Gilbert et al. (2010) and Gordon, Burton & Porter (2004) documented that forgiveness predicated on minimisation may increase the likelihood of returning to the abusive relationship.

Source: Gilbert, S. E. et al. (2010). Predicting Forgiveness in Women Experiencing IPV. | Gordon, K. C., Burton, S. & Porter, L. (2004). Journal of Family Psychology, 18, 331–338.

Clinical warning: Research on domestic violence consistently shows that forgiveness predicated on minimising the severity of the offence is associated with returning to abusive relationships and further victimisation. This is pseudo-forgiveness, not genuine forgiveness. Genuine forgiveness requires full acknowledgement of the harm.

Refusing to forgive can also protect values

Strelan, McKee & Pearce (2018) developed the Barriers to Forgiveness Scale identifying two distinct barrier types: reactive barriers (inability to forgive due to emotional weight and moral repugnance of the offence) and active barriers (purposeful choice not to forgive to protect oneself and one's values). Active barriers reflect forward-looking self-protection against further damage to identity and against future harm.

Source: Pearce, H., Strelan, P. & Burns, N. R. (2018). The barriers to forgiveness scale. Personality and Individual Differences, 134, 337–347.

Baumeister, Exline & Sommer (1998) proposed grudge theory, identifying functional psychological benefits to withholding forgiveness: maintaining the perceived right to compensation, asserting moral superiority, and exerting influence over the offender. While sustained grudge-holding carries health costs (see Section 6), initial refusal to forgive may serve legitimate moral and protective functions.

Source: Baumeister, R. F., Exline, J. J. & Sommer, K. L. (1998). The victim role, grudge theory. In Dimensions of Forgiveness (pp. 79–104). Templeton Foundation Press.

Summary for Scenario 1: The research is clear that authentic forgiveness does NOT require forgoing values or minimising harm. Genuine forgiveness requires full acknowledgement of the transgression's severity. Value integrity is preserved or enhanced through the deliberate act of forgiving. When forgiveness involves minimising harm, it becomes pseudo-forgiveness — associated with erosion of self-respect, continued victimisation, and worse outcomes.

15.2 Scenario 2: Forgiving When the Transgressor Does Not Stop the Damaging Behaviour

Core question: What does the research say about forgiving someone who has not changed their behaviour, shows no remorse, and may continue to inflict harm?

The 'Dark Side' of Forgiveness — McNulty's landmark research

James K. McNulty (2011) conducted the foundational study on this question. In a longitudinal study of newlywed couples, he found a positive association between spouses' reported tendency to express forgiveness and their partners' subsequent psychological and physical aggression:

Finding 1: Spouses who reported being relatively more forgiving experienced psychological and physical aggression that remained stable over four years of marriage.

Finding 2: Spouses who reported being relatively less forgiving experienced a decline in aggression from their partners over the same period.

Finding 3 (daily diary): McNulty (2010) showed that partners were more likely to transgress on days after they had been forgiven than on other days.

Source: McNulty, J. K. (2011). The dark side of forgiveness. PSPB, 37(6), 770–783. | McNulty (2010). Journal of Family Psychology, 90, 787–790.

The operant learning explanation: Forgiveness, by removing negative consequences (anger, criticism, rejection), may communicate that behaviour is tolerable. Research by Overall et al. (2009) confirmed that direct negative behaviours from partners predicted the transgressor's willingness to change.

Source: Overall, N. C. et al. (2009). Regulating partners in intimate relationships. JPSP, 96, 620–640.

Critical clinical finding: McNulty recommends practitioners weigh potential benefits against costs before promoting forgiveness in ongoing relationships. Forgiving infrequent or minor offences may be advisable. Forgiving frequent or major offences — such as verbal abuse or any physical abuse — may be less advisable because repeat occurrences may cause more damage than forgiveness benefits can offset.

The Doormat Effect — When forgiveness erodes the self

Luchies, Finkel, McNulty & Kumashiro (2010) demonstrated the "Doormat Effect" across four studies:

When the perpetrator signals safety and value (through amends, remorse, agreeableness): Forgiveness bolsters the victim's self-respect and self-concept clarity.

When the perpetrator does NOT signal safety: Forgiveness diminishes self-respect and self-concept clarity. The victim begins to feel like a "doormat" — repeatedly forgiving while the offender shows no change.

Source: Luchies, L. B. et al. (2010). The Doormat Effect. JPSP, 98(5), 734–749.

The longitudinal component tracked marital forgiveness and self-respect over five years, demonstrating this is not momentary but accumulates. The Doormat Effect can lead to learned helplessness. Reasons for repeated forgiving-as-doormat include: established emotional dependency, belief that forgiveness is inherently good regardless of context, and social or religious pressure.

Source: Psychology-Spot (2023). The Doormat Effect: When unconditional forgiveness self-destroys us.

Evolutionary psychology: The exploitation risk calculus

McCullough and colleagues (2008, 2013) proposed that cognitive forgiveness mechanisms weigh two variables:

Relationship value: Net benefits expected from continued interaction with the transgressor.

Exploitation risk: Likelihood that the transgressor will impose future costs on the victim.

Burnette et al. (2012) validated the RVEX scale confirming the greatest forgiveness occurs when relationship value is high AND exploitation risk is low. When the transgressor continues damaging behaviour, exploitation risk stays elevated and cognitive systems naturally suppress forgiveness. This is adaptive, not a moral failure.

Source: Burnette et al. (2012). PSPB, 38(9), 1141–1154. | McCauley, Billingsley & McCullough (2022). Current Opinion in Psychology, 44, 275–280.

Apologies lose effectiveness with repeated transgressions

Research shows apologies become less effective when transgressions are repeated or expected to repeat (Gold & Weiner, 2000; Davis & Gold, 2011; Morse & Metts, 2011), when more severe (Ohbuchi et al., 1989; Merolla, 2008), and when perceived as intentional (Struthers et al., 2008). In domestic violence, abusers regularly dilute apologies with blame-shifting, maintaining freedom to continue destructive behaviour.

Source: Frontiers in Psychology (2021). Victim's Perspective of Forgiveness Seeking Behaviors. | Psychology Today (2023). The Limits of Forgiveness.

The distinction between internal and expressed forgiveness

A nuanced position emerges: the research distinguishes internal (emotional/decisional) forgiveness from expressed (communicated) forgiveness. A person may internally work through forgiveness — releasing bitterness, anger, rumination — without communicating it to the transgressor. Health benefits accrue primarily from the internal process. The risks identified by McNulty pertain specifically to expressed forgiveness communicated to an unchanged transgressor.

Summary for Scenario 2: Internal forgiveness benefits the forgiver's health. However, expressing forgiveness to an unchanged transgressor may remove consequences that motivate change, lead to continued aggression, erode self-respect (Doormat Effect), and be maladaptive from an evolutionary perspective. The research supports internal forgiveness proceeding while external expressions remain contingent on the transgressor's demonstrated change.

15.3 Scenario 3: Protecting Yourself While Forgiving — Is It Right to Cut Off Someone Who Is Forgiven?

Core question: Can you genuinely forgive someone and simultaneously refuse contact, set firm boundaries, or permanently end the relationship? Is it morally and psychologically consistent to forgive yet "lock the door"?

Research answer: Yes — one of the most strongly supported positions in the literature

The forgiveness-reconciliation distinction (Section 12) is foundational. Forgiveness is internal and individual. Reconciliation is interpersonal, requires both parties, involves rebuilding trust, and demands demonstrated change from the offender. The literature is unequivocal: one can forgive without reconciling, and reconciliation is often unsafe, inadvisable, or impossible.

Source: Freedman, S. (1998). Counseling and Values, 42(3), 200–216. | Enright, Freedman & Rique (1998). The psychology of interpersonal forgiveness.

Boundaries as invitations, not rejections

Hambrick (Making Sense of Forgiveness, New Growth Press) reframes boundaries: a healthy concept places the barrier between wisdom and folly, not between people. "Forgiveness does not commit me to an unwise or destructive pattern of relating. If someone will not receive your invitation to healthy relationship, they are rejecting biblical wisdom; you are not rejecting them." Forgiveness makes trust conditional, not forgiveness conditional.

Source: Hambrick, B. (2020). Making Sense of Forgiveness. New Growth Press. Chapter 10.

Two uses of "boundaries" are distinguished: (a) wisdom-based limits protecting from destructive patterns — healthy, compatible with forgiveness; and (b) emotional walls preventing all authentic connection — understandable but potentially preventing restorative relationships.

Safety must take precedence over forgiveness

Gregory (2025), author of You Don't Need to Forgive, states: "Your safety is more important than forgiveness." She documents cases where professionals and clergy encouraged domestic violence victims to forgive abusers. Some returned to unsafe environments and were murdered. Enright himself states: "some people choose not to forgive. We should respect this."

Source: Gregory, A. A. (2022, 2025). Psychology Today. Also: You Don't Need to Forgive: Trauma Recovery on Your Own Terms.

Life-threatening danger: Research documents cases where conflating forgiveness with reconciliation in domestic violence contexts led to further abuse and murder. Clinicians and pastoral counsellors must never equate forgiveness with resuming contact with an abuser. This is the most dangerous misconception in the entire forgiveness literature.

Forgiveness without reconciliation in severe transgressions

Research on child sexual abuse (PMC7859423) states resuming the relationship should only be considered when there are indicators of genuine repentance: (a) assuming full responsibility, (b) recognising the magnitude of damage, (c) showing remorse, (d) setting limits so abuse does not reoccur, (e) taking steps to change. Absent these conditions, forgiveness can proceed without any restoration of contact.

Source: PMC7859423. Is It Possible to Forgive Child Sexual Abuse? | Cooney et al. (2011). Australian Journal of Psychology, 63, 107–118.

"Negotiated forgiveness" (Casey, 1998) requires conditions from the offender before relationship restoration. The tension: if forgiveness depends on abuser behaviour, the abuser retains power. The resolution: forgiveness itself remains unconditional and internal; reconciliation remains conditional and interpersonal.

Self-protection as a legitimate forgiveness dimension

The Forgiveness Aversion Scale (Strelan et al., 2013) identifies three dimensions: Unreadiness (reactive — cannot yet forgive), Self-protection (active, forward-looking — concerns about how offenders will interpret forgiveness), and Face concerns (reputation). Self-protection specifically addresses the legitimate fear that forgiveness will be misinterpreted as permission to re-offend.

Source: Strelan, P. et al. (2013). Forgiveness aversion. Motivation and Emotion, 38, 529–543.

Graham (2014, Greater Good, UC Berkeley) confirms one of the hardest decisions is: "Can I get my core needs met in this relationship?" People who experience repeated harm and no remorse most resist forgiving — and this may be entirely rational. Williamson and Gonzales found that even the strongest motivation to forgive can be mitigated by perceived severity and lack of sincere apology.

Source: Graham, L. (2014). How to Overcome Barriers to Forgiveness. Greater Good Magazine, UC Berkeley.

Power dynamics and the right to set boundaries

People perceiving themselves as more powerful are more likely to forgive, likely because they have fewer self-protection concerns (Karremans & Smith, 2010). Setting and enforcing boundaries re-establishes appropriate power dynamics — not a contradiction of forgiveness but a necessary companion. Boundaries are not punishment or revenge. They protect against overstepping, not against relationship itself. Forgiveness can coexist with permanent no-contact without contradiction.

Source: Karremans, J. C. & Smith, P. K. (2010). PSPB, 36(8), 1010–1023. | HealthyPlace (2019). Setting Healthy Boundaries After an Abusive Relationship.

Social costs of non-forgiveness: A counterbalancing consideration

Okimoto and colleagues found non-forgiveness can trigger negative reactions in offenders: disempowerment, apology regret, and sometimes greater willingness to reoffend (Thai, Wenzel & Okimoto, 2023). Gromet & Okimoto (2015) found social backlash against unforgiving victims when offenders had made sincere amends. These findings suggest that while boundary-setting is justified, there may be interpersonal costs to withholding forgiveness — particularly when the offender has made genuine amends.

Source: Thai, Wenzel & Okimoto (2023). PSPB, 49(2), 233–250. | Gromet & Okimoto (2015). Business Ethics Quarterly, 24(3), 411–441.

Summary for Scenario 3: Forgiveness and protective boundaries (including permanent no-contact) are psychologically and morally compatible. Forgiveness is internal; relationship structure is a separate, prudential decision based on safety, demonstrated change, and trust. Cutting off contact is justified when the offender has not demonstrated change, safety concerns exist, continued contact causes harm, or dynamics are exploitative. The greatest danger lies in conflating forgiveness with reconciliation — which has, in documented cases, led to further victimisation and death.

15.4 Cross-Reference Note: Implications for Biblical Research Pillars

These three scenarios raise questions extending directly into the biblical research pillars:

For Divine Forgiveness: How does God model forgiveness? Does God's forgiveness require minimising sin or abandoning holiness? How does God respond to unrepentant transgressors? Does God maintain boundaries (consequences, exile, separation) while offering forgiveness? What is the relationship between divine justice and divine forgiveness?

For Human Forgiveness in Scripture: What does Scripture instruct about forgiving repeated offences (e.g., Matthew 18:21–22)? How do biblical figures handle ongoing transgressions? What is the relationship between forgiveness and accountability? Does biblical forgiveness require reconciliation in every case?

The psychology research here provides the empirical framework. The biblical research will provide the theological framework. Convergences and divergences between these frameworks will be critical material for final narrative chapters.
