When Forgiveness Feels Impossible

A journey from struggle to strength

By le Roux Cilliers

Phase 1 Research · February 2026

The summary

A close friend sat across from me one evening, and I could see the weight of it. The hurt was not fresh — it was old, layered, the kind that accumulates when someone you trusted wounds you not once, but repeatedly. My friend was not asking whether the pain was real. That was never in question. The question was harder: What do I do with this? Do I forgive? Can I forgive? And if I do, does that mean I have to pretend it didn’t happen, or go back for more?

I didn’t have a neat answer that night. But something in that conversation set me on a path I hadn’t anticipated. I began to dig — not casually, but deeply. What does psychology actually tell us about forgiveness? What does the Bible say when you go past the surface quotations and into the original languages? And when science reaches its limits and willpower falls short, is there something more — something that could actually make forgiveness possible when everything in you says it isn’t?

What followed was months of research: over seventy-five scholarly sources in psychology and behavioural science, more than three hundred and fifty biblical references examined in their original Hebrew and Greek, and a systematic study that bridges the two. I did not do this work alone — an AI research assistant helped me organise, cross-reference, and validate the material at a pace that would otherwise have taken far longer. But the questions were mine, the faith journey was mine, and the discoveries were profoundly personal.

This is a summary of what I found. The full narrative, with all its depth, cross-references, and practical application, follows in a separate study guide. But this account stands on its own — because the core of what I discovered can be told simply, even if the evidence behind it runs deep.

• • •

The Struggle Is Real

The first thing the research confirmed is what my friend already knew instinctively: forgiveness is extraordinarily difficult. This is not a weakness. It is a well-documented reality.

Psychology has studied forgiveness intensively over the past three decades. The findings are striking. Unforgiveness — that sustained state of resentment, bitterness, and rumination — functions as a chronic stress response in the body. Researchers have documented elevated cortisol and adrenaline, increased blood pressure, reduced heart-rate variability, and a cascade of health consequences that accumulate over time. Meta-analyses confirm that unforgiveness correlates with higher rates of depression, anxiety, and even post-traumatic stress symptoms.

Conversely, forgiveness has measurable benefits: reduced depression and anxiety, lower stress, improved cardiovascular health, and stronger relationships. One landmark five-week study found that increases in forgiveness led to decreases in stress, which in turn reduced mental health symptoms — and the cycle worked in reverse too, creating what researchers describe as a virtuous cycle.

But here is where the research reaches an honest boundary. Psychology can map the territory of forgiveness with increasing precision. It can measure the damage of holding on and the benefits of letting go. It can offer therapeutic models — Enright’s four-phase process, Worthington’s REACH model — that help people work through the cognitive and emotional dimensions. What it cannot provide is the power to forgive. Especially in the hardest cases.

And the hardest cases are precisely where people need help most. James McNulty’s landmark research revealed what he called “the dark side of forgiveness”: in relationships where harmful behaviour continues, expressing forgiveness can actually enable further aggression. The Doormat Effect, documented by Luchies and colleagues over five years, showed that forgiving someone who shows no remorse or change erodes the forgiver’s own self-respect. Quinney and Okimoto’s recent work found that refusing to forgive can sometimes serve a protective psychological function, preserving a person’s sense of values and self-worth.

So psychology leaves us with a tension: forgiveness is good for you, but it can also harm you. It is beneficial, but in certain circumstances it can become dangerous. Science describes the problem honestly — but it does not resolve it. This is not a failure of psychology. It is an honest boundary. And it is precisely at this boundary that another voice begins to speak.

God Takes Forgiveness Seriously

One of the most remarkable discoveries in this study came from the original languages of Scripture. The Old Testament uses several Hebrew words that English Bibles translate simply as “forgive.” But they do not all mean the same thing — and the distinctions are revelatory.

The Hebrew word salach means to pardon, to release from guilt. It appears approximately fifty times in the Old Testament. And here is what stopped me in my tracks: salach is used exclusively for God’s forgiveness. In not a single instance across the entire Old Testament is it used for one person forgiving another. Multiple Hebrew scholars confirm this. The ancient writers understood, at the level of language itself, that true pardon — the removal of guilt — is something only God can do.

When humans did “forgive” in the Old Testament, a different word was used: nasa’, meaning to lift or carry away a burden. Human forgiveness was understood as bearing the weight of what was done — not erasing the guilt. The distinction runs deep.

In fact, the research revealed something that surprised even me: explicit examples of interpersonal forgiveness in the Old Testament are remarkably rare. Classical scholar David Konstan has argued that the modern concept of forgiveness between people did not fully exist in the ancient world. The Old Testament culture operated primarily within a framework of retribution and proportional justice. The legal code prescribed punishment; forgiveness between people was the exception, not the norm.

This means that when Jesus commanded forgiveness — not seven times, but seventy times seven — He was not reinforcing an existing ethic. He was overturning a prevailing framework. His words were genuinely revolutionary, and His listeners would have heard them that way. He deliberately inverted Lamech’s ancient boast of seventy-sevenfold vengeance into seventy-sevenfold forgiveness. A new era had begun.

And the New Testament grounded this radical command in something equally radical: God’s own prior action. “Forgive one another, as God in Christ forgave you” (Ephesians 4:32). The standard was not human decency or moral effort. The standard was the way God Himself forgives — completely, sacrificially, at immeasurable cost. The Greek word charizomai, used here, means to forgive graciously, as an unmerited gift. It shares its root with charis — grace.

He Knew We Couldn’t Do It Alone

Here is where the two streams of research converge in a way I did not fully expect.

Psychology documents that human beings have a measurable ceiling in their capacity to forgive — particularly in cases of severe trauma, repeated offences, and ongoing harm. Scripture describes the same reality theologically: the human soul, oriented toward self-protection, cannot reach a God-level standard of forgiveness by its own effort. Both disciplines arrive at the same conclusion from different directions. We have a limit.

But the biblical narrative does not stop at the diagnosis. It provides a mechanism. Through the prophet Ezekiel, God promised: “I will give you a new heart and put a new spirit in you; I will remove from you your heart of stone and give you a heart of flesh. And I will put my Spirit in you” (Ezekiel 36:26–27). What the Old Testament promised, the New Testament delivered. At Pentecost, the Holy Spirit was poured out — not on selected individuals temporarily, as in the Old Testament, but on all believers permanently.

The Spirit’s work in enabling forgiveness is not abstract theology. It is documented with remarkable specificity in Scripture. The fruit of the Spirit — love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control (Galatians 5:22–23) — reads like a precise toolkit for the act of forgiving. Love that keeps no record of wrongs. Patience that endures repeated hurt. Gentleness that responds to harshness without retaliating. Self-control that interrupts the cycle of bitterness and rumination.

The Spirit intercedes when we cannot even articulate the struggle (Romans 8:26–27). He works in us both the desire and the ability to do what God asks (Philippians 2:13). He transforms character progressively, “from one degree of glory to another” (2 Corinthians 3:18), which means that what feels impossible today may become possible as the Spirit’s work deepens over time.

The difficulty of forgiveness in human power is not a sign of personal failure. It is precisely why God made a way. He modelled forgiveness Himself, set the standard, and then provided His own Spirit as the power to meet it.

• • •

Following His Example, in His Power

The research did not leave me with a programme or a checklist. It left me with something more demanding and more liberating: a transformed way of understanding what forgiveness actually is, and what it is not.

Forgiveness is not pretending the offence did not happen. It is not minimising harm. It is not excusing behaviour or abandoning boundaries. The psychological and biblical evidence are united on this: forgiveness and reconciliation are not the same thing. You can forgive someone completely and still maintain firm boundaries. You can release bitterness internally while refusing to place yourself in harm’s way externally. Safety matters. Self-respect matters. Wisdom matters.

But neither is forgiveness merely a decision of the will or a suppression of emotion. The research revealed that the deepest forgiveness involves a genuine internal transformation — a shift from resentment toward something that psychology struggles to name but Scripture calls grace. It is a process, often gradual, sometimes involving setbacks, always costly. It does not erase the memory; it changes what the memory does to you.

For the hardest scenarios — forgiving when the harm continues, forgiving when there is no remorse, maintaining boundaries while still releasing bitterness — the research points toward the distinction between internal and expressed forgiveness. The health benefits accrue from the internal work: the releasing of chronic resentment, the interruption of rumination, the refusal to let another person’s actions define your inner life. Whether and how that forgiveness is expressed externally depends on safety, wisdom, and the other person’s demonstrated change.

And for the believer, this internal work is not done alone. It is done in cooperation with a Spirit who shares the burden, who intercedes when words fail, and who produces the very qualities — love, patience, gentleness, self-control — that make forgiveness possible beyond the limits of human capacity.

An Invitation

I went searching for answers because someone I cared about was in pain, and I didn’t have the words to help. What I found was far more than I expected. The science is rigorous. The biblical depth is extraordinary. And the convergence between the two — the way psychology’s honest boundary becomes the exact point where God’s provision begins — is, to me, one of the most compelling things I have ever studied.

If you are wrestling with forgiveness right now — whether to forgive, how to forgive, or whether forgiveness means you have to accept what should never be accepted — you are not alone. The struggle is real, and it is not a sign of weakness. But there is a way through, and it is not the way most people expect.

The full study guide that follows this summary walks through every dimension of this journey in detail: the psychology, the original-language word studies, God’s own example, the Holy Spirit’s enabling work, and the practical outworking in the hardest real-life scenarios. It contains the evidence, the cross-references, and the depth that a summary cannot carry.

But the heart of it is this: forgiveness is not something you have to manufacture from your own depleted resources. It is something God takes so seriously that He modelled it Himself, commanded it explicitly, and then provided His own Spirit to make it possible. The question was never whether you are strong enough. The question is whether you are willing to let Him do what you cannot.

What follows in the full narrative is the complete journey — every finding, every discovery, every hard question faced honestly. If this summary has stirred something in you, I invite you to read on.
