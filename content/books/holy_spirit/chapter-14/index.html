14.1 Word Restored, Spirit Released

The Reformation represents the reversal of the medieval pattern: when the Word was restored to the people, the conditions for the Spirit’s renewed activity were re-established. The primary source is period5_reformation_postreformation_findings.md (43KB).

14.2 The Key Reformers and the Spirit

Martin Luther (1483–1546) emphasised “Spirit through Word” — the Spirit works through the preached and read Word, not apart from it. Luther’s insistence on Scripture in the vernacular (his German New Testament appeared in 1522, Old Testament in 1534) directly addressed the medieval severing of Word and Spirit. Luther was cautious about charismatic claims, distinguishing between the Spirit’s normative work through Scripture and extraordinary manifestations.

John Calvin (1509–1564) developed the doctrine of the Spirit’s internal testimony — the Holy Spirit bearing witness within the believer that Scripture is the Word of God. This was a profound pneumatological contribution: it placed the Spirit’s work at the centre of every believer’s relationship with Scripture, not limited to clergy or scholars.

The Anabaptists (Radical Reformation) emphasised the Spirit’s direct guidance in the community of believers, sometimes in tension with the magisterial Reformers’ emphasis on Word-centred Spirit work.

The Puritans (late 16th–17th century) developed what can be called experimental pneumatology — a systematic theology of the Spirit’s experiential work in conversion, assurance, and sanctification. They expected believers to have discernible, experiential knowledge of the Spirit’s work in their lives.

14.3 The Recovery of Biblical Balance

The Reformation recovered the inseparable connection between Word and Spirit. Scripture was translated into the people’s languages (Tyndale’s English New Testament in 1526, the Geneva Bible in 1560, the King James Version in 1611). The printing press (c.1450) made mass distribution possible. Education was promoted so that people could read Scripture for themselves. The theological emphasis on sola Scriptura ensured that the Word regained its centrality.

The result followed the pattern the study predicts: where the Word flourishes, the Spirit moves. The Reformation itself was primarily a doctrinal recovery (justification by faith, authority of Scripture). The experiential Spirit movements came in the next phase: Pietism in the late 17th century (Spener, Francke) emphasised personal conversion and small-group Bible study; the Moravians (1727) launched modern missions with a prayer movement that lasted over a hundred years; and the Great Awakenings (Chapter 15) saw the Spirit move in power across continents. The Reformation prepared the ground; the Awakenings reaped the harvest.
