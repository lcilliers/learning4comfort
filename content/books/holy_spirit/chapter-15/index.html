15.1 The Great Awakenings (1730s–1850s)

The Great Awakenings represent the Spirit working primarily through conviction of sin, mass conversions, and social transformation — powerful but not charismatic in the modern sense. There were no reported tongues, no healings, no physical phenomena beyond the emotional impact of conviction.

The First Great Awakening (1730s–1740s) began in Jonathan Edwards’ congregation in Northampton, Massachusetts, and spread through George Whitefield’s itinerant preaching across the American colonies and Britain. Approximately 150 new churches were established and 30,000 converts were added in two years. The Awakening profoundly shaped American religious identity and demonstrated that the Spirit could work on a continental scale through the preaching of the Word.

The Second Great Awakening (1790s–1840s) featured camp meetings (most notably Cane Ridge, Kentucky, 1801, where an estimated 20,000–30,000 gathered) and produced dramatic social consequences: the abolition movement, temperance, prison reform, and educational reform. Baptists tripled and Methodists quadrupled in Kentucky. The Student Volunteer Movement sent thousands of missionaries worldwide.

The Great Awakenings are significant for the study because they represent the Spirit working in a mode that is different from Acts 2 — no tongues, no fire, no wind — yet undeniably powerful, producing Tier 3 fruit (character transformation, church growth, social impact, missionary zeal) on a massive scale.

15.2 Pentecostal Origins and Azusa Street (1906)

The modern Pentecostal movement emerged at the turn of the twentieth century. Charles Parham’s Bible school in Topeka, Kansas (1901) first formulated the “initial evidence” doctrine — that speaking in tongues is the necessary sign of Spirit baptism. But the decisive event was the Azusa Street Revival (1906–1909) in Los Angeles, led by William Seymour, an African-American Holiness preacher.

Azusa Street was notable for its radical racial unity in an era of entrenched segregation — black, white, Hispanic, and Asian believers worshipping together, a phenomenon so unusual it drew comment from the secular press. Services ran nearly continuously for three years. The revival emphasised tongues, prophecy, healing, and the experiential power of the Spirit. From Azusa Street, missionaries were dispatched to every continent, initially believing that the gift of tongues would enable them to preach in foreign languages (xenolalia). This expectation was not fulfilled — the tongues experienced were glossolalia, not verified foreign languages — but the fervour and expectancy fuelled global expansion nonetheless.

15.3 Charismatic Renewal and Global Expansion (1960s–2020s)

The Charismatic Renewal represents the Spirit’s work breaking beyond Pentecostal denominational boundaries into mainline Christianity. Dennis Bennett, an Episcopal priest in Van Nuys, California (1960), is often identified as the starting point. The renewal spread to Catholics (Catholic Charismatic Renewal, eventually 120 million adherents), Lutherans, Presbyterians, Methodists, and Anglicans. The Jesus Movement (late 1960s–1970s) transformed counterculture youth into worshippers, birthing contemporary worship and Calvary Chapel. The Vineyard movement (John Wimber, from 1982) taught “naturally supernatural” living.

15.3.1 The Global South Explosion

The most significant dimension of the modern era — and the one most frequently overlooked in Western-centred histories — is the transformation of the Global South. The statistics are staggering:

Korea: The Korean Revival (from 1907) took the nation from less than one percent Christian to thirty percent. Yoido Full Gospel Church (David Yonggi Cho) peaked at 800,000 members. 5 AM prayer meetings are attended by thousands daily. Korea became one of the world’s largest missionary-sending nations.

China: The house church movement grew from roughly one million believers in 1949 to an estimated 70–100 million by 2020, sustained through decades of state persecution. Faithfulness, radical generosity, and boldness under suffering characterise this movement.

Africa: African Christianity exploded from 10 million (1900) to 685 million (2020), with a large percentage Pentecostal or Charismatic. Nigerian mega-churches seat 50,000 or more. Ghanaian and Nigerian churches now send reverse missions to Europe.

Latin America: Brazil became home to approximately 50 million Pentecostals. Assemblies of God Brazil has roughly 12 million members. Major Pentecostal presence across Argentina, Chile, and Guatemala.

15.3.2 The Scale of the Movement

By 2020, the combined Pentecostal and Charismatic movements encompassed 644 million adherents — 26 percent of all Christians worldwide. Over 70 percent live in the Global South. In 1900, eighty percent of the world’s Christians lived in Europe and North America; by 2020, the centre of gravity had shifted entirely. This is the most dramatic geographic, cultural, and demographic transformation in the two-thousand-year history of the Christian faith.

15.4 The Phase 2 Reframing

The research initially sought to find whether the Acts 2 pattern of mass corporate outpouring had been repeated in history. It had not — not in the precise form of simultaneous corporate outpouring with verified supernatural languages. But this finding led to the study’s most important methodological insight: the Spirit works in multiple modes across history. Each era saw the Spirit work in ways suited to God’s purposes for that generation.

The Great Awakenings saw the Spirit work through conviction and conversion — powerful but not charismatic. The Pentecostal movement saw the Spirit work through experiential empowerment and global expansion. The Global South saw the Spirit work through persecuted faithfulness and explosive church growth. These are not failed attempts to replicate Acts 2. They are the Spirit’s varied modes of working, each producing genuine Tier 3 fruit, each meeting the seven conditions documented in Chapter 9.

The modern era is therefore not a disappointment but the largest sustained work of the Holy Spirit in church history outside the apostolic era — working through different modes to accomplish God’s purposes for this generation.

15.5 Contemporary Movements: Documented with Discernment

The research documents several contemporary movements with appropriate discernment. The Toronto Blessing (1994) was controversial — millions visited, some profoundly affected, others rejected the experience. The Asbury Revival (February 2023) was student-led, spontaneous, drew 50,000–70,000 visitors over 16 days, and spread to 37 or more campuses. It was characterised by worship, prayer, and confession, with no spectacular manifestations but deep devotion and repentance. The New Apostolic Reformation (NAR) claims the restoration of apostolic and prophetic offices — a highly controversial position that many consider extra-biblical. The study documents these movements as part of the historical record without endorsing or condemning any specific claim, applying the three-tier evaluation framework developed in Chapter 8.
