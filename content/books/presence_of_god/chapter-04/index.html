The Presence of God

4. Cloud, fire and glory

In this chapter

In the Old Testament God's presence is often made known by what can be seen: a pillar of cloud, a fire, smoke on a mountain, a brightness that fills a house. These are not God himself. They are signs that he is there, and they say something about what his presence is like.

The pillar that led them

From the Exodus on, the people travelled with a visible sign of God going ahead of them. "The Lord went before them by day in a pillar of cloud to lead them along the way, and by night in a pillar of fire to give them light, that they might travel by day and by night" (Exodus 13:21). It did not leave them: "The pillar of cloud by day and the pillar of fire by night did not depart from before the people" (Exodus 13:22). When Egypt pursued them, it moved to protect them: "the angel of God who was going before the host of Israel moved and went behind them, and the pillar of cloud moved from before them and stood behind them" (Exodus 14:19).

Moses later reminds the people what that presence did. It was God "who went before you in the way to seek you out a place to pitch your tents, in fire by night and in the cloud by day, to show you by what way you should go" (Deuteronomy 1:33). The sign led, lit the way, protected and chose the place to stop. Isaiah remembers it in the language of love: "In all their affliction he was afflicted, and the angel of his presence saved them; in his love and in his pity he redeemed them" (Isaiah 63:9).

Fire on the mountain

At Sinai the presence was overwhelming. "Mount Sinai was wrapped in smoke because the Lord had descended on it in fire. The smoke of it went up like the smoke of a kiln, and the whole mountain trembled greatly" (Exodus 19:18). God spoke "face to face at the mountain, out of the midst of the fire" (Deuteronomy 5:4), and the people could not bear it: "you were afraid because of the fire, and you did not go up into the mountain" (Deuteronomy 5:5).

Fire is one of the most constant signs. "Fire goes before him and burns up his adversaries all around" (Psalm 97:3). "Our God comes; he does not keep silence; before him is a devouring fire" (Psalm 50:3). It shows both his power to save and his holiness. The same God who goes before Israel "as a consuming fire" against their enemies (Deuteronomy 9:3) is also a fire among his own people when they despise him: "the fire of the Lord burned among them and consumed some outlying parts of the camp" (Numbers 11:1).

Fire from before the Lord

At the tabernacle the two sides meet in a single chapter. When the priests first offered sacrifice, "fire came out from before the Lord and consumed the burnt offering and the pieces of fat on the altar, and when all the people saw it, they shouted and fell on their faces" (Leviticus 9:24). Days later Nadab and Abihu offered fire that God had not commanded, and the same words are used: "fire came out from before the Lord and consumed them, and they died before the Lord" (Leviticus 10:2). Moses explains: "This is what the Lord has said: 'Among those who are near me I will be sanctified, and before all the people I will be glorified'" (Leviticus 10:3).

God's presence is a gift and also a danger. It accepts the offering, and it consumes the one who treats it lightly. That is why so much of the law is about approaching rightly.

The cloud over the mercy seat

The cloud also marked the most holy place. "Tell Aaron your brother not to come at any time into the Holy Place inside the veil, before the mercy seat that is on the ark, so that he may not die. For I will appear in the cloud over the mercy seat" (Leviticus 16:2). Even the high priest, once a year, had to cover the place with incense: "that the cloud of the incense may cover the mercy seat that is over the testimony, so that he does not die" (Leviticus 16:13). The cloud both shows and veils. God is there, and he is not to be looked on.

Glory that fills the house

"The glory of the Lord" is the Old Testament's fullest phrase for God's presence made visible. It appears at moments of crisis: "the glory of the Lord appeared to them" when Moses and Aaron fell on their faces at the tent of meeting (Numbers 20:6). It filled the tabernacle when it was finished (Exodus 40:34–35; these verses were not among those gathered for the study). It filled the temple when it was dedicated, so completely that "the priests could not stand to minister because of the cloud, for the glory of the Lord filled the house of the Lord" (1 Kings 8:11; also 2 Chronicles 5:14).

The word for glory, kābôd, comes from a root meaning weight. God's glory is the weight of his presence, his reality pressing in on a place. It is related to his name and his goodness, as when he promised Moses, "I will make all my goodness pass before you and will proclaim before you my name" (Exodus 33:19).

Glory that leaves and returns

Ezekiel saw the glory move. In exile by the Chebar canal he saw "the appearance of the likeness of the glory of the Lord. And when I saw it, I fell on my face" (Ezekiel 1:28). The glory was not tied to the temple. It had come to the exiles. In his visions he then saw it leave Jerusalem: the cherubim "stood at the entrance of the east gate of the house of the Lord, and the glory of the God of Israel was over them" (Ezekiel 10:19), and from there it went out of the city (Ezekiel 11:23). The departure of the glory is the prophet's way of saying that God had withdrawn his presence from a temple that his people had filled with idols.

But Ezekiel also saw it come back. "The glory of the Lord entered the temple by the gate facing east" (Ezekiel 43:4), and "behold, the glory of the Lord filled the temple of the Lord. And I fell on my face" (Ezekiel 44:4). The return of the glory is the return of God himself to dwell among his people.

A sign, not the substance

Elijah's meeting with God on the mountain shows the limits of the signs. "Behold, the Lord passed by, and a great and strong wind tore the mountains and broke in pieces the rocks before the Lord, but the Lord was not in the wind. And after the wind an earthquake, but the Lord was not in the earthquake" (1 Kings 19:11). After the fire came the low whisper (1 Kings 19:12), "And when Elijah heard it, he wrapped his face in his cloak and went out and stood at the entrance of the cave. And behold, there came a voice to him" (1 Kings 19:13).

The signs go before God and announce him. They are not God. In the end what Elijah received was not a spectacle but a voice, a word spoken to him. The New Testament takes this further. The glory is seen in a face, the face of Jesus Christ (2 Corinthians 4:6), and on the mountain of transfiguration "the appearance of his face was altered, and his clothing became dazzling white" (Luke 9:29).
