The Presence of God

11. Face to face

In this chapter

  1. The coming, the presence
  2. Ready for his presence
  3. Brought into his presence
  4. Before the throne
  5. They will see his face

Scripture ends where the longing of the psalms pointed: God's people will see his face. Between now and then stands the coming of Christ, which the New Testament calls by a word that means "presence".

The coming, the presence

The Greek word parousia means presence or arrival (Chapter 2). It is used of friends arriving, but its great use is for the return of Jesus. The disciples asked, "what will be the sign of your coming and of the end of the age?" (Matthew 24:3). Jesus answered that it would not be hidden: "as the lightning comes from the east and shines as far as the west, so will be the coming of the Son of Man" (Matthew 24:27).

The coming brings the resurrection: "Christ the firstfruits, then at his coming those who belong to Christ" (1 Corinthians 15:23). It gathers God's people: "the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ and our being gathered together to him" (2 Thessalonians 2:1). It ends every power set against him: the lawless one will be brought "to nothing by the appearance of his coming" (2 Thessalonians 2:8).

Ready for his presence

Because his coming is a coming into his presence, the New Testament asks believers to be ready for it. Paul prays "that he may establish your hearts blameless in holiness before our God and Father, at the coming of our Lord Jesus with all his saints" (1 Thessalonians 3:13), and "may your whole spirit and soul and body be kept blameless at the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ" (1 Thessalonians 5:23). John gives the same charge more simply: "abide in him, so that when he appears we may have confidence and not shrink from him in shame at his coming" (1 John 2:28).

They are also asked to wait for it. "Be patient, therefore, brothers, until the coming of the Lord" (James 5:7). "Establish your hearts, for the coming of the Lord is at hand" (James 5:8). Some mock the delay: "Where is the promise of his coming?" (2 Peter 3:4). But the apostles had seen enough to trust it: "we did not follow cleverly devised myths when we made known to you the power and coming of our Lord Jesus Christ, but we were eyewitnesses of his majesty" (2 Peter 1:16).

Brought into his presence

The coming is the believer's hope because it brings them where they long to be. "He who raised the Lord Jesus will raise us also with Jesus and bring us with you into his presence" (2 Corinthians 4:14). The book of Jude closes with praise to God "who is able to keep you from stumbling and to present you blameless before the presence of his glory with great joy" (Jude 24).

Those who trembled before his presence in the Old Testament (Chapter 7) are here brought into it with joy. The difference is that they come presented by him, made blameless, not standing in their own right.

Before the throne

Revelation shows what that presence will be. John saw "a great multitude that no one could number, from every nation, from all tribes and peoples and languages, standing before the throne and before the Lamb" (Revelation 7:9). The promise of the tabernacle comes true at last: "Therefore they are before the throne of God, and serve him day and night in his temple; and he who sits on the throne will shelter them with his presence" (Revelation 7:15). The elders "fall down before him who is seated on the throne and worship him" (Revelation 4:10), and the redeemed sing "a new song before the throne" (Revelation 14:3).

In the last chapters of Revelation, God's dwelling is with man, and he will dwell with them, and they will be his people (Revelation 21:3; not among the verses gathered, so described here). The words are the same ones used for the tabernacle and for the Word who dwelt among us (Chapters 2 and 9). What began in a tent in the wilderness ends with God living among his people for ever.

They will see his face

Then the hope that runs through the whole of Scripture is fulfilled. Moses was told, "you cannot see my face, for man shall not see me and live" (Exodus 33:20). The psalmist hoped, "As for me, I shall behold your face in righteousness; when I awake, I shall be satisfied with your likeness" (Psalm 17:15), and "the upright shall behold his face" (Psalm 11:7). Jesus said that the angels of his little ones "always see the face of my Father who is in heaven" (Matthew 18:10).

Paul sets the present beside the future: "For now we see in a mirror dimly, but then face to face. Now I know in part; then I shall know fully, even as I have been fully known" (1 Corinthians 13:12). And the last vision of the Bible gives it in five words: "They will see his face, and his name will be on their foreheads" (Revelation 22:4).

The face that shone in blessing, that was hidden in judgment, that was sought in prayer and seen in Jesus Christ, will be seen by all his people. The presence of God, everywhere and always, will be known by them as it has never been known before: directly, without veil, and for ever.
