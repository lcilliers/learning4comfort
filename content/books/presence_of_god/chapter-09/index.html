The Presence of God

9. God with us: Christ and the Spirit

The New Testament speaks of God's presence differently from the Old. There are fewer clouds and fires, and the tabernacle and the temple are no longer at the centre. God's presence is located first in a person, Jesus Christ, and then by the Holy Spirit in a people. The change is not from a real presence to a vaguer one. It is from a presence that was outside and at a distance to one that is near and within. One of the reference works gathered for the study sums it up: Christianity is, at its heart, the religion of the presence of God and of communion with him.

Many of the passages in this chapter were not among the verses gathered for the study, because they do not use the search words. They are referred to and described here, not quoted.

The Word who dwelt among us

The Gospels open with God come near. Matthew names Jesus "Immanuel", God with us (Matthew 1:23). John says that the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and that we saw his glory (John 1:14). The verb John uses is the one built on the Greek word for the tabernacle (Chapter 2). The tent of meeting has become a human life.

The glory that once filled a house is now seen in a face. Paul puts it this way: "God, who said, 'Let light shine out of darkness,' has shone in our hearts to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ" (2 Corinthians 4:6). On the mountain the disciples saw it for a moment: "as he was praying, the appearance of his face was altered, and his clothing became dazzling white" (Luke 9:29). Simeon, holding the infant Jesus, spoke of the salvation "that you have prepared in the presence of all peoples" (Luke 2:31).

The name made known

In the Old Testament God's name stood for God himself, and the temple was the place of his name (Chapter 5). Jesus says in his prayer that he has made the Father's name known to those the Father gave him (John 17:6, 26). He did this by his whole life, his deeds as well as his words. His own name says it too: "Jesus" means "the Lord saves". In him, the work that God's name did in the Old Testament comes to its fulfilment. To know him is to know the God whose name dwelt in the temple.

The new temple

Jesus spoke of his own body as the temple (John 2:21). Paul says that in him the whole fullness of deity dwells bodily (Colossians 2:9), and that in him all the fullness of God was pleased to dwell (Colossians 1:19). The temple had been the place where God chose to be present among his people. Now that place is a person. Wherever Jesus was, God was present among his own.

His holiness shows it. The Old Testament knew God as "the Holy One" in the midst of his people. The New Testament gives Jesus the same title. In his conflict with evil, an unclean spirit recognises him as the Holy One of God (Mark 1:24), and Peter confesses him by the same name (John 6:69). Luke shows him full of the Holy Spirit at every step: at his baptism, in the wilderness, and in his ministry (Luke 3:22; 4:1, 14, 18). In the Gospels, God's powerful presence among his people comes first through the words and deeds of Jesus: his teaching, his healing and his authority over evil.

To be with Jesus was to be in God's presence, though many did not see it. Jesus warns that some will say, "We ate and drank in your presence, and you taught in our streets" (Luke 13:26), and still not know him. Being near him was not the same as belonging to him.

Glory at the cross

John shows something unexpected about where the glory is seen most clearly. When Jesus speaks of his coming death, he calls it the hour for the Son of Man to be glorified (John 12:23–33). The cross, the place of shame, is the hour of divine glory. Faith sees God present in the one who was crucified, and the glory of God shining in his face (2 Corinthians 4:4, 6).

The way into the presence opened

The tabernacle had a holy place and a most holy place, and only the high priest entered the inner room, once a year. Hebrews recalls "a tent ... in which were the lampstand and the table and the bread of the Presence. It is called the Holy Place" (Hebrews 9:2). That arrangement was a copy. "Christ has entered, not into holy places made with hands, which are copies of the true things, but into heaven itself, now to appear in the presence of God on our behalf" (Hebrews 9:24).

The reference works put the change sharply. Before Christ, God dwelt among his people, but not in them. Israel knew that God was in their midst, but could not enter the holy of holies. At the moment Jesus died, the curtain of the temple was torn in two, from top to bottom (Matthew 27:50–51), and the way into God's presence was opened. The barrier described in Chapter 8, sin that separates, is dealt with in his death. What he does is to "present you holy and blameless and above reproach before him" (Colossians 1:22), as God chose his people "that we should be holy and blameless before him" (Ephesians 1:4).

The Spirit before Pentecost

The Spirit was not new at Pentecost. In the Old Testament, the Spirit of God was already the way in which the God who is above all drew near to his people. Isaiah remembers the Exodus as the time when God put his Holy Spirit in the midst of his people and led them (Isaiah 63:11–14). Zechariah speaks of the words that God sent by his Spirit through the prophets (Zechariah 7:12), and of God's work done not by human might or power but by his Spirit (Zechariah 4:6). Again and again the Spirit came upon a person for a task, to speak God's word: on Azariah, on Jahaziel, on Zechariah the priest (2 Chronicles 15:1; 20:14; 24:20).

So the Spirit was the presence and power of God with his people, God himself acting in keeping with his nature. Without him there is no fellowship between God and man. That is why David's prayer joins the two: "Cast me not away from your presence, and take not your Holy Spirit from me" (Psalm 51:11). To lose the Spirit would be to lose God's presence.

What changes in the New Testament is not who the Spirit is, but how he is given: no longer upon a few, for a time and a task, but within all God's people, for good.

The Spirit within

Jesus said that the Spirit would not be given until he had been glorified (John 7:39). As he prepared his disciples for his going, he promised that the Father would send the Spirit to be with them and in them, and that the Father and the Son would come and make their home with those who love him (John 14:16–17, 23). The Spirit would teach them and bring to their remembrance all that Jesus had said (John 14:25–26). The Spirit who comes is both the Spirit of God and the Spirit of Christ (Romans 8:9).

At Pentecost the promise came true in wind and fire (Acts 2:2–4). The old signs of presence returned, but now they rested on people, not on a building. The Ezekiel promise was fulfilled. God had said that he would not hide his face again "when I pour out my Spirit upon the house of Israel" (Ezekiel 39:29). Paul can now speak of beholding the Lord's glory and being changed by it, and he says that "this comes from the Lord who is the Spirit" (2 Corinthians 3:18).

A temple of living stones

From then on the New Testament calls God's people his temple. The church together is God's temple, and God's Spirit dwells in it (1 Corinthians 3:16–17). In Christ the whole people of God is being built into a dwelling place for God by the Spirit (Ephesians 2:21–22). Peter calls believers living stones, built up as a spiritual house (1 Peter 2:5). This temple is far greater than the old one, whose curtain was torn when Christ died.

The temple is also each believer. Paul tells the Corinthians that their body is a temple of the Holy Spirit within them (1 Corinthians 6:19), and he applies God's promise to dwell among his people to the church (2 Corinthians 6:16). God is present in the believer's heart. He reigns there, and he is worshipped there, because his presence has made the heart a holy place.

One of the reference works points to three passages that, taken together, sum up the whole biblical theme of God's indwelling presence: all the fullness of God dwelling in Christ (Colossians 1:19; 2:9), and the Father and the Son making their home with the one who loves Jesus (John 14:23).

A holy people

Where God is present, there is holiness. In the Old Testament, places, objects and people were holy because they were near the holy God or belonged to him. The same is true now, but the holy place is a people. As the holy God called Israel to be holy as he is holy (Leviticus 19:2), so he calls his people in Christ to be holy in all their conduct (1 Peter 1:15–16). They are a chosen and holy people who belong to God, called to declare his praises (1 Peter 2:9–10).

So the New Testament often calls Christians "the saints", the holy ones. They are holy because God has called them and accepted them by grace, on the basis of Christ's atoning work, and because the Spirit dwelling in them makes them holy. Those who are led by the Spirit are God's children (Romans 8:14–17).

The holiness article in the gathered material uses a phrase from the title of a study by Gordon Fee: believers live by "God's empowering presence". The Spirit within is not a passive guest.

  • He forms character. Believers are to walk by the Spirit (Galatians 5:16; Romans 8:12–13), and the fruit of the Spirit grows in them: love, joy, peace and the rest (Galatians 5:22–23), the likeness of Christ.
  • He makes them one. By one Spirit they were all baptised into one body (1 Corinthians 12:12–13), and they are to keep the unity of the Spirit (Ephesians 4:3–4).
  • He sends them out. The Spirit's coming gives power to be Christ's witnesses to the ends of the earth (Acts 1:8).
  • He gives gifts for others. His gifts are given for the common good and the building up of the body (1 Corinthians 12:7, 27).

Outside, and inside

Moses' face shone after he had been in God's presence, and he put a veil over it. Paul recalls that "the Israelites could not gaze at Moses' face because of its glory, which was being brought to an end" (2 Corinthians 3:7). Now, he says, "we all, with unveiled face, beholding the glory of the Lord, are being transformed into the same image from one degree of glory to another" (2 Corinthians 3:18).

One of the reference works put it this way: the people of Israel knew God from the outside, and Christians know him from the inside. The presence is no less real for being unseen. It is more intimate. God's Spirit shares in his people's life, draws them into worship, and changes them into the likeness of Christ.

A presence that can be recognised

The New Testament still expects God's presence to be felt and seen, though not in fire and cloud. When an unbeliever comes into a gathering of the church where God's word is spoken plainly, "the secrets of his heart are disclosed, and so, falling on his face, he will worship God and declare that God is really among you" (1 Corinthians 14:25). Peter promises "times of refreshing ... from the presence of the Lord" for those who repent (Acts 3:20). Cornelius gathers his household and says, "we are all here in the presence of God to hear all that you have been commanded by the Lord" (Acts 10:33).

Living before God (Chapter 6) continues too. The apostles speak and act "in the sight of God" (2 Corinthians 2:17; 4:2; 12:19). Paul writes "before God, I do not lie!" (Galatians 1:20), and charges Timothy "in the presence of God and of Christ Jesus" (1 Timothy 5:21; 2 Timothy 4:1). Cornelius' prayers "have ascended as a memorial before God" (Acts 10:4). Abraham's faith was exercised "in the presence of the God in whom he believed, who gives life to the dead and calls into existence the things that do not exist" (Romans 4:17).

The New Testament does not leave the Old Testament's language behind. It fills it. The face, the name, the dwelling, the glory, the holiness and the Spirit all come together in Christ and in those who belong to him. Chapter 10 turns to what that means for the church now.
