The Presence of God

9. God with us: Christ and the Spirit

In this chapter

  1. The Word who dwelt among us
  2. The way into the presence opened
  3. The Spirit within
  4. Outside, and inside
  5. A presence that can be recognised

The New Testament speaks of God's presence differently from the Old. There are fewer clouds and fires, and the tabernacle and the temple are no longer at the centre. Instead, God's presence is located first in a person, Jesus Christ, and then by the Holy Spirit in a people. The change is not from a real presence to a vaguer one. It is from a presence that was outside and at a distance to one that is near and within.

The Word who dwelt among us

The Gospels open with God come near. Matthew names Jesus "Immanuel", God with us (Matthew 1:23). John says that the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and that we saw his glory (John 1:14). The verb John uses is the one built on the Greek word for the tabernacle (Chapter 2). The tent of meeting has become a human life. (These verses were not among those gathered for the study, so they are described here, not quoted.)

The glory that once filled a house is now seen in a face. Paul puts it this way: "God, who said, 'Let light shine out of darkness,' has shone in our hearts to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ" (2 Corinthians 4:6). On the mountain the disciples saw it for a moment: "as he was praying, the appearance of his face was altered, and his clothing became dazzling white" (Luke 9:29). Simeon, holding the infant Jesus, spoke of the salvation "that you have prepared in the presence of all peoples" (Luke 2:31).

To be with Jesus was to be in God's presence, though many did not see it. Jesus warns that some will say, "We ate and drank in your presence, and you taught in our streets" (Luke 13:26), and still not know him. Being near him was not the same as belonging to him.

The way into the presence opened

The tabernacle had a holy place and a most holy place, and only the high priest entered the inner room, once a year. Hebrews recalls "a tent ... in which were the lampstand and the table and the bread of the Presence. It is called the Holy Place" (Hebrews 9:2). That arrangement was a copy. "Christ has entered, not into holy places made with hands, which are copies of the true things, but into heaven itself, now to appear in the presence of God on our behalf" (Hebrews 9:24).

The reference works gathered for the study put the change sharply. Before Christ, God dwelt among his people, but not in them. Israel knew that God was in their midst, but could not enter the holy of holies. When Christ died, the curtain of the temple was torn in two (Matthew 27:51), and the way into God's presence was opened. The barrier described in Chapter 8, sin that separates, is dealt with in his death. What he does is to "present you holy and blameless and above reproach before him" (Colossians 1:22), as God chose his people "that we should be holy and blameless before him" (Ephesians 1:4).

The Spirit within

The second great move is the coming of the Spirit. Jesus promised that the Father would send the Spirit to be with his disciples and in them, and that the Father and the Son would make their home with those who love him (John 14:16–17, 23). At Pentecost the promise came true in wind and fire (Acts 2:2–4). The old signs of presence returned, but now they rested on people, not on a building.

From then on the New Testament calls God's people his temple. The church together is God's temple, and God's Spirit dwells in it (1 Corinthians 3:16). Each believer's body is a temple of the Holy Spirit (1 Corinthians 6:19). In Christ the whole people of God is being built into a dwelling place for God by the Spirit (Ephesians 2:21–22). (These verses too were not among those gathered, and are described, not quoted.)

The Old Testament promise is fulfilled. Ezekiel heard God say that he would not hide his face again "when I pour out my Spirit upon the house of Israel" (Ezekiel 39:29). David had prayed, "Cast me not away from your presence, and take not your Holy Spirit from me" (Psalm 51:11). The New Testament joins presence and Spirit for good. Paul can speak of beholding the Lord's glory and being changed by it, and he says that "this comes from the Lord who is the Spirit" (2 Corinthians 3:18).

Outside, and inside

Moses' face shone after he had been in God's presence, and he put a veil over it. Paul recalls that "the Israelites could not gaze at Moses' face because of its glory, which was being brought to an end" (2 Corinthians 3:7). Now, he says, "we all, with unveiled face, beholding the glory of the Lord, are being transformed into the same image from one degree of glory to another" (2 Corinthians 3:18).

One of the reference works put it this way: the people of Israel knew God from the outside, and Christians know him from the inside. The presence is no less real for being unseen. It is more intimate. God's Spirit shares in his people's life, draws them into worship, and changes them into the likeness of Christ.

A presence that can be recognised

The New Testament still expects God's presence to be felt and seen, though not in fire and cloud. When an unbeliever comes into a gathering of the church where God's word is spoken plainly, "the secrets of his heart are disclosed, and so, falling on his face, he will worship God and declare that God is really among you" (1 Corinthians 14:25). Peter promises "times of refreshing ... from the presence of the Lord" for those who repent (Acts 3:20). Cornelius gathers his household and says, "we are all here in the presence of God to hear all that you have been commanded by the Lord" (Acts 10:33).

Living before God (Chapter 6) continues too. The apostles speak and act "in the sight of God" (2 Corinthians 2:17; 4:2; 12:19). Paul writes "before God, I do not lie!" (Galatians 1:20), and charges Timothy "in the presence of God and of Christ Jesus" (1 Timothy 5:21; 2 Timothy 4:1). Cornelius' prayers "have ascended as a memorial before God" (Acts 10:4). Abraham's faith was exercised "in the presence of the God in whom he believed, who gives life to the dead and calls into existence the things that do not exist" (Romans 4:17).

The New Testament does not leave the Old Testament's language behind. It fills it. The face, the dwelling, the glory and the Spirit all come together in Christ and in those who belong to him.
