The Presence of God

8. Driven out, and brought back

In this chapter

  1. Hiding in the garden
  2. Fleeing, and finding there is nowhere to go
  3. When God is not among them
  4. Cast out of his sight
  5. What separates
  6. Brought back
  7. "I am with you"

If God is everywhere (Chapter 1), no one can leave his presence in the first sense. Yet the Bible's story begins with people hiding from it and being sent away from it, and the Old Testament ends with a nation cast out of it. Here "presence" means God's favour and fellowship. These verses show how it is lost, and how God brings his people back.

Hiding in the garden

The first time the word appears, it is in a hiding. "They heard the sound of the Lord God walking in the garden in the cool of the day, and the man and his wife hid themselves from the presence of the Lord God among the trees of the garden" (Genesis 3:8). Before this, God's walking in the garden was not something to fear. After the first sin it is.

Cain's story goes further. Under God's judgment he says, "you have driven me today away from the ground, and from your face I shall be hidden" (Genesis 4:14), and then, "Cain went away from the presence of the Lord and settled in the land of Nod, east of Eden" (Genesis 4:16). Cain does not leave the place where God is. He leaves the place where God is near and turned toward him.

Fleeing, and finding there is nowhere to go

Jonah tried to do what Cain had done. He "rose to flee to Tarshish from the presence of the Lord" (Jonah 1:3). The God he fled from met him in the storm and in the sea. From inside the fish Jonah speaks in two ways at once: "I am driven away from your sight; yet I shall again look upon your holy temple" (Jonah 2:4). He feels cast out, and yet he hopes. The prayer is heard, because the God who seems far off is not.

David, hunted by Saul and driven out of the land, pleads in the same language: "let not my blood fall to the earth away from the presence of the Lord" (1 Samuel 26:20). To be forced away from the land of God's people was to be pushed toward the edges of his presence.

When God is not among them

Some verses speak of God refusing to go with his people. When Israel tried to enter the land after refusing to trust him, Moses warned, "Do not go up, for the Lord is not among you ... Because you have turned back from following the Lord, the Lord will not be with you" (Numbers 14:42–43). After Achan's sin God said, "I will be with you no more, unless you destroy the devoted things from among you" (Joshua 7:12). God foresees a day when the people will say, "Have not these evils come upon us because our God is not among us?" (Deuteronomy 31:17).

This is why Moses refused to go on without God. When the Lord promised, "My presence will go with you, and I will give you rest" (Exodus 33:14), Moses answered, "If your presence will not go with me, do not bring us up from here" (Exodus 33:15). The land was worth nothing without the presence. "For how shall it be known that I have found favor in your sight, I and your people? Is it not in your going with us, so that we are distinct" (Exodus 33:16). What made Israel different from every other nation was not their law or their land but God's going with them.

Cast out of his sight

The history books end with the presence withdrawn. For a long time God held back: "the Lord was gracious to them and had compassion on them, and he turned toward them ... nor has he cast them from his presence until now" (2 Kings 13:23). But Israel in the north was "removed ... out of his sight" (2 Kings 17:18), and Judah followed: "I will remove Judah also out of my sight, as I have removed Israel, and I will cast off this city that I have chosen" (2 Kings 23:27). The account closes with the same words in two books: "For because of the anger of the Lord it came to the point in Jerusalem and Judah that he cast them out from his presence" (2 Kings 24:20; Jeremiah 52:3).

Exile was not only the loss of a land. It was the loss of the place where God had put his name, and it was understood as God's presence withdrawn. Jeremiah heard how final it had become: "Though Moses and Samuel stood before me, yet my heart would not turn toward this people. Send them out of my sight" (Jeremiah 15:1).

What separates

The verses are plain about the cause. "Your iniquities have made a separation between you and your God, and your sins have hidden his face from you so that he does not hear" (Isaiah 59:2). The law had already said that one who came to holy things while unclean "shall be cut off from my presence" (Leviticus 22:3). It is not that God moves away, as if he were a neighbour who had moved house. Sin makes a separation that a holy God will not ignore.

So the deepest prayer in the psalms asks for the one thing that matters: "Cast me not away from your presence, and take not your Holy Spirit from me" (Psalm 51:11). David had sinned grievously. He did not ask first to keep his throne, but to keep God's presence.

Brought back

The same verses that speak of being cast out also speak of the way back, and it is always by return. "If you return, O Israel, declares the Lord, to me you should return. If you remove your detestable things from my presence, and do not waver" (Jeremiah 4:1). Hezekiah invited the remnant of the north to Passover with the promise: "the Lord your God is gracious and merciful and will not turn away his face from you, if you return to him" (2 Chronicles 30:9). God himself waits for it: "I will return again to my place, until they acknowledge their guilt and seek my face" (Hosea 5:15).

The return is not only the people's doing. God promises to bring it about: "with everlasting love I will have compassion on you" (Isaiah 54:8), and "I will not hide my face anymore from them, when I pour out my Spirit upon the house of Israel" (Ezekiel 39:29). The final answer to the loss of God's presence is not a rebuilt building but the gift of his Spirit. That promise is where the New Testament begins.

"I am with you"

Alongside all of this runs a quieter promise, made to people sent out to do hard things. "It is the Lord who goes before you. He will be with you; he will not leave you or forsake you. Do not fear or be dismayed" (Deuteronomy 31:8). To Joshua: "Just as I was with Moses, so I will be with you. I will not leave you or forsake you" (Joshua 1:5). To Jeremiah: "Do not be afraid of them, for I am with you to deliver you" (Jeremiah 1:8). The presence that can be lost by sin is also promised, freely, to those God sends.
