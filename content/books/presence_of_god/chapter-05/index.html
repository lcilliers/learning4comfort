The Presence of God

5. A dwelling among them

In this chapter

  1. The place where I will meet with you
  2. Near, but guarded
  3. The ark that went before them
  4. The place for his name
  5. A house filled, and a house left
  6. Beyond the building

The God who led Israel by cloud and fire also chose to live among them. The tabernacle, and later the temple, was his dwelling. The verses show what that meant: where he met with his people, how near they could come, and what happened when the dwelling was treated as a guarantee.

The place where I will meet with you

God described the tabernacle by what would happen there. At the entrance of the tent of meeting, he said, "I will meet with you, to speak to you there" (Exodus 29:42). The incense altar was set "in front of the mercy seat that is above the testimony, where I will meet with you" (Exodus 30:6). The tent was not a home that God needed. It was an appointed meeting place, set up so that he could be with his people and speak to them.

The whole camp was arranged around it. "They shall camp facing the tent of meeting on every side" (Numbers 2:2). The presence was at the centre of the people's life, and everything faced it. Even the bread on the table had a name for it: "you shall set the bread of the Presence on the table before me regularly" (Exodus 25:30), bread kept continually before his face.

Near, but guarded

The same arrangement that brought God near also guarded his holiness. Moses, Aaron and his sons camped at the entrance, "guarding the sanctuary itself, to protect the people of Israel. And any outsider who came near was to be put to death" (Numbers 3:38). The guard was for the people's sake. To come near carelessly was to die, as Nadab and Abihu did (Chapter 4).

The point is pressed home when the ark comes to Beth-shemesh and to Jerusalem. When men of Beth-shemesh looked into it and died, the survivors asked, "Who is able to stand before the Lord, this holy God?" (1 Samuel 6:20). When Uzzah put out his hand to steady it, "the anger of the Lord was kindled against Uzzah, and he struck him down because he put out his hand to the ark, and he died there before God" (1 Chronicles 13:10). The nearness of God's presence never made it ordinary.

Holiness is at the heart of God's self-revelation, and the picture the verses give is not of a God far off, but of the Holy One in the midst of his people (Hosea 11:9). His holiness does not make him unapproachable. The one who dwells in the high and holy place also dwells with the contrite and lowly in spirit (Isaiah 57:15). He is a God who seeks his people. But any approach to him had to be made in the way he had himself provided. The whole order of sacrifice, priesthood and purity had one aim: to make it safe for God's people to meet the holy God who lived among them. Any other way of coming near was dangerous, as the boundary set around Sinai showed (Exodus 19:12).

The ark that went before them

The ark of the covenant was the chief sign of God's presence with the people on the move. "The ark of the covenant of the Lord went before them three days' journey, to seek out a resting place for them" (Numbers 10:33). Each time it set out, Moses prayed, "Arise, O Lord, and let your enemies be scattered, and let those who hate you flee before you" (Numbers 10:35). At the Jordan, Joshua told the people how they would know God was with them: "Here is how you shall know that the living God is among you", and he pointed to the ark: "Behold, the ark of the covenant of the Lord of all the earth is passing over before you into the Jordan" (Joshua 3:10–11).

But the ark was not a charm. After a defeat, the elders said, "Let us bring the ark of the covenant of the Lord here from Shiloh, that it may come among us and save us from the power of our enemies" (1 Samuel 4:3). They lost the battle and the ark with it. They had treated the sign of the presence as if it could be carried into battle without the Lord. In the Philistine temple, the ark told its own story: "Dagon had fallen face downward on the ground before the ark of the Lord" (1 Samuel 5:3). God's presence could not be captured or used.

Yet the ark was also the place where God was said to be enthroned above the cherubim (1 Samuel 4:4). Hezekiah prayed to him by that title: "O Lord, the God of Israel, enthroned above the cherubim" (2 Kings 19:15). The king of all the earth had a throne among his people.

The place for his name

Once Israel was in the land, God would choose "the place" for his presence. The phrase in Deuteronomy is that he would choose it "to make his name dwell there" (Deuteronomy 14:23). There the people would eat, rejoice and worship before him: "you shall rejoice before the Lord your God, you and your son and your daughter, your male servant and your female servant, the Levite ... the sojourner, the fatherless, and the widow who are among you, at the place that the Lord your God will choose, to make his name dwell there" (Deuteronomy 16:11). The whole household, and the poor with them, came into his presence together.

Three times a year they were to "appear before the Lord your God at the place that he will choose" (Deuteronomy 16:16). Going up to the sanctuary was called "appearing" before him. Hannah wanted her son to "appear in the presence of the Lord and dwell there forever" (1 Samuel 1:22). The longing of the psalms uses the same words: "My soul thirsts for God, for the living God. When shall I come and appear before God?" (Psalm 42:2).

The name was more than a word for God. Among the peoples of the Bible, a name and the person who bore it went together, and God's name stood for God himself, at work and making himself known. The angel who went before Israel could act with God's authority because, God said, "my name is in him" (Exodus 23:21; Chapter 4). The temple was the place of the name, "the city where I have chosen to put my name" (1 Kings 11:36), not only because his name was called on there but because God himself was present there.

A house filled, and a house left

When the temple was dedicated, God answered Solomon: "I have consecrated this house that you have built, by putting my name there forever. My eyes and my heart will be there for all time" (1 Kings 9:3). From there he heard prayer: "From his temple he heard my voice, and my cry to him reached his ears" (Psalm 18:6). All the earth is called to stillness before it: "the Lord is in his holy temple; let all the earth keep silence before him" (Habakkuk 2:20).

Yet the dwelling could be lost. Through Jeremiah God told the people who trusted in the temple to remember the first sanctuary: "Go now to my place that was in Shiloh, where I made my name dwell at first, and see what I did to it because of the evil of my people Israel" (Jeremiah 7:12). Hezekiah confessed what their fathers had done: "They have forsaken him and have turned away their faces from the habitation of the Lord and turned their backs" (2 Chronicles 29:6). And Isaiah heard God refuse worship that came without obedience: "When you come to appear before me, who has required of you this trampling of my courts?" (Isaiah 1:12).

The temple was the place of God's presence because he chose to be there. It was never a guarantee that he would stay whatever the people did. Ezekiel's vision of the glory leaving (Chapter 4) is the end of that road.

Yet the hope of the place did not die with the exile. Daniel in Babylon "went to his house where he had windows in his upper chamber open toward Jerusalem. He got down on his knees three times a day and prayed and gave thanks before his God" (Daniel 6:10). And Ezekiel's vision of the restored city ends with its new name, which declares that the Lord is there (Ezekiel 48:35).

Beyond the building

Even in the Old Testament the dwelling points beyond itself. Moses had already said, "The eternal God is your dwelling place, and underneath are the everlasting arms" (Deuteronomy 33:27). The psalms speak of dwelling in God's presence as something deeper than standing in a courtyard: "the upright shall dwell in your presence" (Psalm 140:13), and "In the cover of your presence you hide them from the plots of men" (Psalm 31:20). Ezekiel saw a restored temple from which water flowed out to give life (Ezekiel 47:1). Malachi promised that "the Lord whom you seek will suddenly come to his temple" (Malachi 3:1).

The New Testament reads these as fulfilled in a new way. The Lord came to his temple in Jesus. And the dwelling place of God becomes, in the end, a people (Chapter 9).
