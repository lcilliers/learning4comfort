The Presence of God

10. Seeking his presence now

In this chapter

  2. What changes
  3. The same shape as before
  4. The Spirit's presence in the church today
  5. A seeking that is answered

The Old Testament is full of calls to seek God's presence, to come before him and to seek his face. If God's Spirit now lives in his people (Chapter 9), is there anything left to seek? The question was one of those put at the start of this study, and the answer the verses give is that the seeking goes on. What changes is where it is done and how.

Seeking in the Old Testament

In the Old Testament, seeking God was often tied to a place and an act. People went up to the sanctuary, fasted, prayed, and sought his face. "David sought the face of the Lord" in a famine (2 Samuel 21:1). Jehoshaphat, afraid, "set his face to seek the Lord, and proclaimed a fast throughout all Judah" (2 Chronicles 20:3). Daniel "turned my face to the Lord God, seeking him by prayer and pleas for mercy with fasting and sackcloth and ashes" (Daniel 9:3). Ezra fasted "that we might humble ourselves before our God, to seek from him a safe journey" (Ezra 8:21).

Seeking was also a whole way of life. "Seek the Lord and his strength; seek his presence continually!" (1 Chronicles 16:11). It came with a promise and a warning: "The Lord is with you while you are with him. If you seek him, he will be found by you, but if you forsake him, he will forsake you" (2 Chronicles 15:2). It was expected to spread to the nations: "Many peoples and strong nations shall come to seek the Lord of hosts in Jerusalem and to entreat the favor of the Lord" (Zechariah 8:22).

What changes

The New Testament changes the location of the seeking, not its necessity. God's people no longer have to travel to a temple to come before him. They have themselves become his dwelling place (Chapter 9). The Old Testament believer sought God by approaching a holy place. The New Testament believer seeks God who is already present, by turning toward him and responding to his Spirit.

That is why seeking does not stop at conversion. The reference works gathered for this study make three points.

  • The Spirit draws into worship. Paul tells believers to be filled with the Spirit, and goes straight on to singing and thanksgiving (Ephesians 5:18–20). The Spirit's presence is not static. It draws people into communion with God, and they are called to answer.
  • The Spirit transforms. Believers are "being transformed into the same image from one degree of glory to another" (2 Corinthians 3:18). That change is God's work, and it asks for their response: obedience, prayer, and turning from sin.
  • The Spirit is a pledge. Paul calls the Spirit the guarantee of an inheritance not yet received (Ephesians 1:13–14). The fullness of God's presence is still to come. So believers still long for it, and still seek it.

The same shape as before

The shape of seeking in the New Testament is the same as in the Old, turned inward and made personal. The Old Testament called people to come before God humbly, and so does the New: "Humble yourselves before the Lord, and he will exalt you" (James 4:10). The Old Testament called people to pour out their hearts before him (Chapter 6), and the New Testament prays with the same freedom. Prayers still rise "as a memorial before God" (Acts 10:4).

The psalmist's "I have set the Lord always before me" (Psalm 16:8) is quoted by Peter as true of Christ: "I saw the Lord always before me, for he is at my right hand that I may not be shaken" (Acts 2:25). What was true of the Son is the pattern for those who follow him: a life lived with God always in view.

The Spirit's presence in the church today

How the church experiences God's presence by the Spirit has itself been a living question in recent times. The article on the Charismatic movement gathered for this study describes how, in the second half of the twentieth century, a renewal swept through the historic denominations, Protestant and Roman Catholic, across the world. Many of those involved preferred to call it a renewal, not a movement, because it sought to recover something within the churches rather than to stand beside them: the charismatic side of the Holy Spirit's work.

  • What it emphasised. It sought the recovery of the gifts listed in such passages as 1 Corinthians 12, and it often taught a "baptism in the Holy Spirit", with speaking in tongues as its sign. It grew out of the Pentecostal movement of the earlier twentieth century, but it aimed to renew the churches rather than to separate from them.
  • What it valued. At first, the article says, it cared far more about experiencing the Spirit and his gifts than about thinking about them. It has been called a swing of the pendulum from the cerebral to the experiential: from a faith for the mind to a faith for the heart, from doctrine held to a living God met.
  • What it raised. As it matured, its teaching about a baptism in the Spirit, distinct from and later than conversion and shown by tongues, raised questions both for its own members and for the churches they belonged to. A debate followed in which, in the article's words, the renewal both corrected and was corrected by the established theologies of the churches.

The article gives its own assessment of that debate, from the biblical side:

  • Baptism in the Spirit. Following James Dunn, it judges that in the New Testament being baptised in the Spirit belongs to becoming a Christian, not to a second blessing that comes later.
  • Tongues. It finds very little support in Scripture for tongues as the invariable evidence of that baptism, and notes that many charismatics accept this.
  • The gifts today. It finds equally little biblical support for the view, common in Protestant teaching, that the gifts of 1 Corinthians 12 were withdrawn once the canon of Scripture was complete. It notes the claims of charismatics to exercise these gifts today as credible, though still contested.

Two further points in the article bear directly on this study. First, an emphasis on the Spirit's power and the life of the resurrection carries a danger of shallow triumphalism, and it needs to be held together with the cross, which remains at the centre of all true Christian experience. Chapter 9 showed that John calls the cross the hour of glory. Second, the discussion has gained depth from a recovery of the Trinity: the chief work of the Spirit is not to hand out dramatic gifts, but to bring people into relationship with the Father and the Son, and to enlist them in God's mission of reconciliation to the whole creation.

That matches what the verses of the earlier chapters show about God's presence. Its purpose was always relationship: a face turned toward a person, a God who meets and speaks with his people, and a people who walk before him. The gifts of the Spirit are given for others, for the building up of the body (1 Corinthians 12:7, 27), and the fruit of his presence is the likeness of Christ (Galatians 5:22–23). Whatever form the experience of the Spirit takes in a believer or a church, the test of it is the same: does it draw people to the Father, through the Son, and send them out to others?

A seeking that is answered

The seeking is not a search for a God who might not be found. God himself invited it: "You have said, 'Seek my face.' My heart says to you, 'Your face, Lord, do I seek'" (Psalm 27:8). He has come near in Christ and lives in his people by the Spirit. To seek him now is to turn toward the one who has already turned toward us. It means prayer, obedience, worship and the pursuit of holiness, in the believer's own heart and in the gathered church.

It also means waiting. The face that Moses could not see and the psalmist longed to behold is promised, but it has not yet been seen. Chapter 11 turns to that promise.
